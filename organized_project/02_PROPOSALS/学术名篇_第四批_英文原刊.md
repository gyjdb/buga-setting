## On the Tides

**ON THE TIDES**

By ALDRIDGE.

[Communicated to the Silver Society, C.R. 1101.]

Severin’s Thirteenth Question asks: “Have the risings and fallings of the tides a fixed law?” He asked every city to keep a record of its tides, and to keep it as far as might be in one manner. More than a hundred years later Fanshawe made the mana meter for the Tower of All Things and laid down the rules of the stations, and from that time the cities kept their records in one manner. Those records now fill a hundred and fifty years, and I have spent thirty years in setting them in order. I shall show three things in this paper.

First, besides the ten-year tide and the rise and fall within a day, the risings and fallings of the tide may be resolved into constituent tides of fixed period, of which twelve have periods agreeing with the courses of the twelve mana moons; and there are a few small ones whose frequencies are the sums or differences of those of two moons. Secondly, if it be supposed that the free mana flows as a fluid flows, and that the moons are forces pushing it, a set of equations may be written in the reckoning of flux, from which it follows why the constituents keep fixed periods; and to the terms that ordinary fluids have, one more must be added, which I call the term of self-excitation. Thirdly, the simplified equations, with the term of self-excitation left out, suffice to foretell the ordinary tides from their constituents; but when the crests of several constituents fall together, the foretelling is much in error.

**I. THE RECORDS**

The stations of the cities are set according to Fanshawe’s rules: far from places of casting and from mana pivots; read four times a day, at the fixed hours of midnight, six in the morning, noon and six in the evening; the city, the place and the height of the station recorded; and the records sent to the Tower of All Things. The sensing piece of the mana meter is a fine crystal that resonates with the free mana about it, and the number of divisions by which its needle is turned is what is written down. I have used thirty-seven stations in eleven cities, the earliest begun in C.R. 950, the longest read without a break for a hundred and fifty years. The rest began earlier or later, and some broke off; where a station was moved, its city changed its anchorage or its meter was changed, the old and the new were read side by side for a while according to Fanshawe’s rules, and I have joined the two by the difference so found.

The records have their gaps and their errors. I first read together two stations not far apart in the same city; where their difference passed its usual bounds, I searched whether there had been casting or the starting or stopping of a pivot nearby on that day, and struck out what could be so accounted for, keeping and marking what could not. From this I judge the error of a single reading to be about a third of a division.

Four readings a day suffice to show the rise and fall within the day. Since the four are six hours apart, in their mean the rise and fall that comes once a day just cancels, and this is the mean of the day; in the risings and fallings of the daily means there remain, for the most part, only the constituents whose periods are longer than a day. Where a reading is missing, the mean of that day is left empty, and not made up from three. The constituents spoken of below, except the daily tide, are all found from the daily means.

**II. THE CONSTITUENT TIDES**

If the risings and fallings of the tide are made up of parts of fixed period, the record of a station may be written

$$ h(t) = h_0 + sum_k A_k cos(2 pi t / P_k - phi_k) $$ (1)

where $P_k$ is the period of the $k$th constituent, $A_k$ its amplitude, and $phi_k$ its phase. The time $t$ is reckoned in days from midnight at the beginning of the first day of the Month of the Winter Lyre, C.R. 950, and the mean of a day is set at the middle of its four readings, nine in the morning. If the period is known, the amplitude and phase may be found thus: multiply the record by the cosine and the sine of the same period, and sum over the whole record,

$$ A_k cos phi_k = 2/N sum_n h(t_n) cos(2 pi t_n / P_k), quad A_k sin phi_k = 2/N sum_n h(t_n) sin(2 pi t_n / P_k) $$ (2)

$N$ being the number of readings, from which the mean $h_0$ of the whole record is first taken away. When the period is wrong, the two right-hand sides of (2) tend to nothing as the record lengthens; when it is right, they tend to fixed values. To find the constituents one at a time in this way, the record must be long and their periods well apart; where several lie near together, they must be found jointly, together with the mean. When the period is not known, a series of trial periods is taken one by one, and it is seen at which of them fixed values come out; the longer the record, the narrower the crests, and the closer the trial periods must be set; so I first swept a record of ten years coarsely, to find roughly where the crests lie, and then set the trial periods close about each crest and swept the whole record of a hundred and fifty years. The tables of cosines and sines for the trial periods were given to the star-reckoning arrays of the Tower of All Things to unfold by differences; a table that an array unfolds in a day is worth a month of a company of reckoners. To multiply the readings by the tables and sum them is still the reckoners’ work, and every part was reckoned twice, by two men, and compared.

The constituents so found are set out in Table I. The daily tide has the period of a day. The ten-year tide is not a simple cosine: it rises only in the three months of Reawakening, Summer Haze and Scrolls of the tide year, and is almost still at other times. To make up three months so set apart from cosines of periods ten years, five years, three years and a third, and so on through the whole parts of ten years, would want more than a hundred of them; and the thirtieth and the fifty-fifth parts of ten years are so nearly the periods of the tenth and the eighth moons that not even a hundred and fifty years of records can part them. I have therefore not written it in cosines, but have taken the mean, day by day, of the daily means of those three months in every tide year, save that of 1040, which is mixed with the hundred-year tide, and set it in a table of its own; and in finding the constituents of the moons, the daily means of those three months of the tide years were first set aside. The hundred-year tide came only once in the hundred and fifty years of the records, about 1040; its period I have filled in from the old scrolls and from the chronicles of the lands below, in which its comings are from ninety-eight to a hundred and three years apart, and I take a hundred; its amplitude can be estimated only from that one coming.

Besides these, there are in the daily means twelve constituents whose periods each agree with the course of one mana moon, and Table I gives them in the order of the moons. Their amplitudes are all far smaller than that of the ten-year tide, the greatest less than four divisions and the least a little more than half a division; yet in a hundred and fifty years of records they are all plainly seen. How far the amplitudes may be trusted I judge thus: dividing the hundred and fifty years into five parts of thirty years, I sweep each part by (2). Between the five parts the ten constituents other than those of the twin moons differ by less than a tenth of the amplitude found from the whole record; the two of the twin moons can barely be parted in thirty years, the two crests of the sweep crowding together, and differ between the parts by as much as two tenths. Fig. 1 shows the daily means of the station of Lataki in 1093, with the sum of the twelve constituents and the five combined ones as reckoned from the records of 950 to 1089: the greater part of a year’s risings and fallings comes from these constituents.

**III. THE TWIN MOONS**

The fourth mana moon, Arcana, and the seventh, Ankaramu, are twins, and their periods differ by only a fourteenth part of a day. So do the periods of their constituents: taken together, the two first add, then after more than fourteen years all but cancel (their amplitudes being unequal, there remains seven tenths of a division), then after more than fourteen years add again, the whole round taking about twenty-nine years and three months. With records of ten or twenty years, therefore, sweeping by (2) cannot part the two; the reckoning finds only one, and that of an amplitude now great and now small. A record of thirty years can barely part them, and one of a hundred and fifty years parts them plainly. The upper part of Fig. 2 gives the amplitudes found by sweeping (2) over the trial periods for a record of ten years; the lower part enlarges the neighbourhood of twenty-seven days, where the record of ten years shows a single crest, and only the record of a hundred and fifty years parts it into two, one at the period of Arcana and one at that of Ankaramu.

Some before me have said that the tide has nothing to do with the moons, one of their reasons being that the constituent near twenty-seven days is now great and now small, as though it were not driven by a single moon. It now appears that it is the constituents of two moons laid one upon the other.

**IV. THE COMBINED CONSTITUENTS**

The periods of the last five constituents of Table I agree with no moon. The frequency of the constituent of 13.68 days is, within the reckoning, the sum of the frequencies of Arcana and Ankaramu; that of 8.04 days is the sum of those of the second and fourth moons, and that of 22.31 days the sum of those of the fourth and tenth; that of 19.56 days is the difference of the second and fourth, and that of 35.23 days the difference of the fourth and tenth. If each moon pushed only on its own account, the tide should have twelve periods and not their sums and differences. There must therefore be a term that joins two constituents together, and I take it to be in the flow of the mana itself: where both are high, it adds to the tide their product, and the product contains the sum and the difference of their frequencies. The amplitudes of these five go roughly as the products of the amplitudes of the two from which they come, and their phases are roughly the sums or differences of those two phases, which is just the manner of a product. Nor are there only five: other sums and differences of two moons (save the difference of the twins, of which below), and doubles of the frequency of one, are found in the records to the number of twenty and more, all smaller than these five, the greatest about a third of a division; I have set only the five greatest in Table I, and the rest in a table kept at the Tower of All Things. Every combined constituent in which Arcana has a part has beside it one in which Ankaramu takes its place, differing in frequency only by the difference of the twins; and these, like the twins, are plainly parted only in a hundred and fifty years of records. The difference of the twins has the period of twenty-nine years and three months spoken of above; there are only five rounds of it in a hundred and fifty years, and it is mixed with the rising and falling of the one hundred-year tide about 1040, so that I cannot part it out.

**V. THE TIDE EQUATIONS**

I suppose that the free mana flows in the world as a fluid flows, from the denser places to the thinner, and that the twelve mana moons are forces pushing it, each with its own period. On this supposition two equations may be written in the reckoning of flux. Let $rho$ be the density of the free mana at a place, and $bold(v)$ the velocity of its flow. Mana neither comes from nothing nor goes to nothing, which is Finch’s general account; therefore

$$ (partial rho) / (partial t) + nabla dot (rho bold(v)) = 0 $$ (3)

The flow of the mana is governed by four things: the push from denser to thinner, whose strength is as the slope of the density; the drag of the flow upon itself, which slowly evens out places of unequal speed; the pushing of the moons; and the term of self-excitation:

$$ (partial bold(v)) / (partial t) + (bold(v) dot nabla) bold(v) = - a nabla rho + nu nabla^2 bold(v) + sum_k bold(F)_k + kappa rho nabla rho $$ (4)

where $a$, $nu$ and $kappa$ are three positive constants, and $bold(F)_k$ is the push of the $k$th moon, rising and falling with its period as a single cosine. The first three terms ordinary fluids have also; the last, $kappa rho nabla rho$, is the one I add. It says that mana has a disposition to gather towards where it is denser, and gathers the more strongly the denser it is. It does not add mana from nothing, since (3) holds as before: where the mana grows more, it has flowed in from round about; the term only carries mana towards the denser places.

The term of self-excitation runs against the first term. Taken together the two are $-(a - kappa rho) nabla rho$: while the density is not great, the push is still from denser to thinner; when the density passes $a / kappa$, the push is turned about, and the denser place gathers ever denser. I take a local mana surge to be a stretch of air in which the density has passed this bound. When the ten-year and the hundred-year tides rise, the density over a wide stretch is raised together and comes nearer this bound, yet in the records no wide stretch has passed it; and it is perhaps for this reason that the local surges come mostly in the tide years.

I add this term for two reasons. The first is the combined constituents of Section IV. Ordinary fluids also have two terms that can join two constituents together, the term $(bold(v) dot nabla) bold(v)$ of (4), and in (3) the product of the rising and falling of the density with the velocity; but the tide flows so slowly, and rises and falls so little, that, by the speeds and risings I have estimated, the combined constituents these two give are less than a tenth of those found. With the term of self-excitation it is otherwise. Let $rho_0$ be the usual density, and $rho'$ its rising and falling about it. Taken with the first term, the term of self-excitation gives $-(a - kappa rho) nabla rho$, in which the part that goes as the square of $rho'$ stands to the part that goes as $rho'$ in the ratio $(kappa rho') / (a - kappa rho_0)$. The nearer the usual density comes to $a / kappa$, the smaller the divisor and the greater the ratio; and only once the usual density is past the half of $a / kappa$ are the combined constituents that the term of self-excitation gives greater than those of the two terms of ordinary fluids. Those found are more than ten times what these two give, so that to make them up the usual density must lie much nearer still to $a / kappa$. The second is three unusual risings in the records, at Varen in the Month of the Winter Lyre of 1012, at Onais in the Month of Summer Haze of 1047, and at Lataki in the Month of Reawakening of 1080, when the crest stood more than four tenths higher than the constituents, as reckoned from the tables, could then have raised it. Each of the three was recorded at a single station only, and I do not presume to say that none was misread; I say only that they agree with the term of self-excitation.

**VI. THE SIMPLIFIED EQUATIONS, AND FORETELLING**

Put $rho = rho_0 + rho'$, taking $rho_0$ to be the same everywhere and unchanging. When the term of self-excitation is taken with the first, the part that goes as $rho'$ has the same form as the first term, only with $a - kappa rho_0$ in place of $a$, which I write $b$; the part that goes as the square of $rho'$, since in ordinary tides $rho'$ is far less than $a / kappa - rho_0$, is far smaller than the first term, and may be left out together with the two terms of ordinary fluids. With $rho'$ and $bold(v)$ small, equations (3) and (4) become

$$ (partial rho') / (partial t) + rho_0 nabla dot bold(v) = 0, quad (partial bold(v)) / (partial t) = - b nabla rho' + nu nabla^2 bold(v) + sum_k bold(F)_k $$ (5)

These are the simplified equations. Each of their terms, but the push of the moons, is proportional to $rho'$ or $bold(v)$, so that the push of each moon, once it has been long at work, raises at each place only a rising and falling of the same period, with an amplitude and phase that differ from place to place: and this is why the constituents keep fixed periods. This may be seen in the records of the several stations. Table III gives the constituent of Arcana at four stations: its period, swept out at each station by itself, is everywhere 27.32 days to within a hundredth of a day, but its amplitude and phase differ from station to station. The push is that of one and the same moon, and the answer differs from place to place, as (5) says; were the risings and fallings of the tide only the confusion of each place by itself, the period would not be everywhere the same.

For daily foretelling the equations need not be solved: it suffices to carry forward the tables of constituents of each station by (1), adding the ten-year and the hundred-year tides from their own tables; what the simplified equations tell is why such carrying forward may be trusted.

**VII. THE TRIAL**

From the records of 950 to 1089 I found the constituents of every station, and then reckoned by (1) the years 1090 to 1099, and compared the reckoning with what was measured in those ten years. For this trial the number of the constituents, their periods and the manner of striking out readings were all settled from the records before 1090, and those after were not looked at. The result for the station of Lataki is given in Table II (the three tide months of 1090 are spoken of below, and the days whose means are empty are not counted); those of the other thirty-six stations are much the same, the greatest differences all falling on the days when the crests were piled. On ordinary days the reckoning and the measure differ on the mean by less than a division; when the crests of two or three constituents come near together, they differ by two divisions; and in the thirty days when the crests of several constituents fell together in one place, by nearly seven divisions, and on the worst day by more than thirteen. The higher the crests were piled, the lower the reckoning fell. This is just the manner of the term of self-excitation: the simplified equations leave it out, which does no harm in ordinary times, but cannot hold the tide down when it is piled high.

**VIII. WHAT REMAINS UNSETTLED**

First, whether the moons are the cause of the tides. This paper writes the moons as forces pushing the mana, because, so written, the tides can be reckoned. Others hold that the moons and the tides are alike shadows of the Sea of Mana, and that the waxing and waning of the moons are only signs of the tides. On that view the forces in the equations should be replaced by something else; but the constituents found would not change, so that from the records of the tides alone the two views cannot be told apart.

Secondly, the term of self-excitation. Its grounds are the combined constituents and the three unusual risings, and both are still slight. The constant $kappa$, and how far the usual density lies below $a / kappa$, I can estimate only roughly, from the amplitudes of the combined constituents. If in the records to come the unusual risings do not appear again, or if the combined constituents have another cause, the term should be struck out.

Thirdly, the hundred-year tide. It came only once in the records, and both its period and its amplitude are filled in.

Fourthly, the local mana surges. The equations say that when the density passes $a / kappa$ it gathers ever denser; but in the moment before it passes, the records show no sign, so that, with the records and reckonings we have, warning can be given only after the surge has begun, and it cannot yet be foretold.

Fifthly, the solutions of the equations. With the term of self-excitation the denser places gather ever denser, and whether a solution may cease within a finite time to be smooth, and bring forth an infinite density, I do not know. I know only that in the records of ordinary tides no loss of smoothness is to be seen.

**IX. THE TIDE-DISC**

To reckon a year of tides by (1), seventeen cosines must be added up day by day, which is hard labour for the reckoners. A tide-disc may be made: seventeen water-wheels, joined by gears whose teeth are so counted that each turns with the period of one constituent, and each driving a piston up and down in a cylinder of water, the throw of each set by its amplitude and its starting point by its phase; the seventeen cylinders open below into one narrow pipe, so that the water each drives in and draws out is gathered there, and the height of the water in the pipe is the sum of the seventeen risings and fallings, which a float draws as the height of the tide. The ten-year and the hundred-year tides are added afterwards from their own tables. I have asked the craftsmen of the Tower of All Things to make trial of such an instrument.

## On Certain Plagues Arising from Micro-Mana

**ON CERTAIN PLAGUES ARISING FROM MICRO-MANA**

**PREFACE**

Severin’s Twenty-First Question asks: “Whence come the plagues?” For more than three hundred years the physicians of the cities have each had their own answer. Some say the plagues come of foul mana, which in the tide years grows foul and is breathed in by mortals and beasts; others that they pass from the sick to the whole, and so travel along the air-routes from city to city. Both have something in them, and neither tells all: on the first, why of those who live in one city and breathe the same mana do some fall sick and others not; on the second, why in the tide years do many cities that have no dealings with one another fall sick at once?

The year C.R. 1190 was a tide year. From the Month of Reawakening the mortals of Derola, the people of mixed blood and the sheep of the city’s outskirts took the tide-carbuncle one after another: first a fever, then after some days black carbuncles under the skin, and in the worst cases death. The Silver Folk of the same city, as always, took it not at all. I watched this plague from the hospital of the city, and made use of it to do the things that follow. I shall prove that the tide-carbuncle, and certain other plagues that can be raised again in beasts, arise from a very small living thing that carries mana and multiplies. I call it micro-mana.

**CHAPTER 1. PLAGUE AND TIDE**

The hospital of Derola has kept a plague-register since 1151, in which every case and every sick beast is written with its day and its quarter of the city, the people of mixed blood being written among the mortals. I have summed the forty years of the register year by year; the sums are given in Table I, and drawn year by year in Fig. 1.

In the register the cases among mortals in the tide years are some twelve times those of the other years; and of the cases of the four tide years taken together, seventy-nine in a hundred fall in the three months of Reawakening, Summer Haze and Scrolls, the very months in which the moon of mana stands in the western sky. That the plague rises and falls with the tide others have said before; but the register shows a further thing that has not been remarked: outside the tide years the tide-carbuncle does not die out: every year there are cases, never fewer than twelve nor more than thirty-three, mostly in the quarters along the air-routes.

Of this outbreak of 1190 the register is most exact. On the third day of the Month of Reawakening a herdsman of the East Wall brought the first sick sheep; on the fifth, mortals fell into fever on the North Slope and at the West Quay; by the end of the month nine of the eleven quarters of the city had cases. In the three months after, when the moon of mana was not in the western sky, the new cases were far fewer. The Month of Summer Haze was the worst, with more than a hundred and ninety cases among mortals in the one month and more than twelve hundred sick sheep; after the Month of Scrolls the cases grew fewer, and by the end of the year there had been four hundred and twelve cases among mortals, sixty-three deaths, and two thousand six hundred and ten sick sheep. The mortals who fell sick were mostly herdsmen, tanners and the carriers of the quays; of those who lived under one roof, some fell sick and some did not.

**CHAPTER 2. SEEING THE MICRO-MANA**

If there is anything in a fluid of the body that carries mana, a working for the sensing of mana will feel it; but it is too small to be seen, only felt. I asked the array-maker of the hospital to carve an array of magnifying upon a thin plate of crystal: a drop of fluid is set upon the plate, a working of sensing of the first ring is cast upon it, and the array enlarges the light the working returns and throws it upon a white screen, where the image is four hundred times the size of the thing, the screen being kept always at one distance from the plate. The power of magnifying is fixed thus: several fine crystals of measured sizes, not all alike, are set upon the plate, and their sizes upon the screen are observed; for each of them it comes to some four hundredfold. Measured in this way, a micro-mana is not longer than a tenth of the thickness of a hair.

Not every point of light upon the screen is a living thing. Dust upon the plate, splinters of mana crystal in the fluid, and even glints of the array itself, all throw points of light. I tell them apart by two rules. The first is to follow one and the same point of light: the living divide, and what they divide into divides again, which dust, splinters and glints do not; that a point moves is no proof that it lives, since the fluid may carry it, and I take movement only as a sign beside the rest. The second is to set the plate within an order ward: the living cease to move and to divide within a day, while dust and glints remain as they were. By these two rules I learned what the living look like; once that was learned, they were known by their look, without waiting for each to divide. In counting, each drop is looked at in ten places, the number upon the screen counted at each, and the mean of the ten taken; this is the mean in a place of Table II, and the portions in which none were seen are reckoned in it.

Looked at in this way, the fluids of the sick show upon the screen many very small points of light, shaped like grains of rice, somewhat long, some joined in pairs, some dividing into two. They move of themselves and avoid one another, and left overnight beneath a sealed plate they double in number. In the fluids of the whole there is not one. They carry mana, they are alive and they multiply; I call them micro-mana. The left of Fig. 2 shows micro-mana as seen upon the screen.

I took the fluids of the sick in a tide year and in another year, of the whole, of sick sheep and of whole sheep, one portion from each man and each beast, and always of the blood; those of the tide year were taken in 1190, those of the other year in 1191, and those of the sheep in 1190. In one drop of each portion I counted the micro-mana; the counts are given in Table II.

Among the sick there were some in which none were seen, three portions of the sixty in the tide year and seven of the forty in the other; why, I cannot say.

**CHAPTER 3. THE REMAINS**

Latham’s Taxonomy of Monsters says that most monsters leave mana crystals when they die. If the micro-mana are monsters, they too should leave mana crystals when they die. I set apart micro-mana seen under the magnifying, and by the third day the points of light no longer moved, but shrank one by one into very fine grains of crystal, with edges and corners, and with a cold light unlike that of the living; these are the mana crystals of dead micro-mana, shown on the right of Fig. 2.

Accordingly I sought such grains in the bodies of the dead, men and beasts. Of twenty-five mortals dead of the tide-carbuncle, they were found in the carbuncles and the blood of twenty-two; of forty sheep dead of it, in thirty-seven; of thirty whole sheep slaughtered, and of twelve mortals dead of other sicknesses, not a grain. The grains go with the sickness and not with health; in the three mortals and three sheep dead of it in whom none were found, I could not learn why.

Fig. 2. Seen under the array of magnifying: left, living micro-mana, some of them dividing; right, the fine mana crystals into which they shrink when dead

**CHAPTER 4. KEEPING APART, AND INOCULATION**

Seeing alone cannot show that the sickness is caused by them: they may be the effect of the sickness and not its cause. To prove that it is caused by them, they must be kept apart from the sick body and raised elsewhere, until everything else brought out of the sick body is diluted to almost nothing, and then put into whole beasts, to see whether the sickness is raised again.

I use a sealed array-dish: a sealing array upon its mouth lets nothing in from without and nothing out from within, and the dish holds some hundred drops of a fluid for raising them, made from the serum of sheep. The serum is first boiled and strained, put into the dish and sealed, and kept for three days in a warmth near that of a sheep’s body; only if in three days not one point of light is seen upon the screen is it used. Every dish that fails this rule I pour away. A drop of blood is taken from a sheep sick in a tide year and put into the dish, and the micro-mana multiply generation after generation. When they have grown thick, in about three days, one drop is put into a new dish and raised again; and so from dish to dish, twenty passages taking two months. At every passage a blank dish was opened in the same way and given one drop in the same way, only of fluid without micro-mana, and raised beside the rest; in the blank dishes of all twenty passages not one point of light was ever seen. To obtain a single kind, a drop from the first dish is diluted until only one micro-mana is seen under the magnifying, all ten places being looked at, and that drop is put whole into a new dish and raised. I obtained a single kind in this way three times, from three different sick sheep, and what the three dishes raised was alike in shape, in size and in the manner of multiplying; the first of them is that used in Table III.

At each passage one drop goes into some hundred drops of new fluid, so that whatever was there before is left at a hundredth; by the tenth passage everything else in the first drop of blood had been diluted a hundredfold ten times over, and none of it could remain. I put micro-mana of the tenth and of the twentieth passage into whole sheep; I put the fluid from the blank dishes, raised alike, into another flock of whole sheep for comparison; and I put the blood of sick sheep, unraised, directly into a third, for reference. The results are given in Table III.

The sheep inoculated were all taken from a flock outside the city that had never been sick, and their blood was looked at beforehand and showed no micro-mana; each group was kept in its own fold and watched for twelve full days. The manner of inoculation is to prick the skin on the inner side of the sheep’s leg and let in one drop; counted as before, a drop of the culture showed some thirty micro-mana in a place. Of the twenty sheep into which raised micro-mana were put, eighteen took the tide-carbuncle, with the same signs as were seen in the city: fever on the second day, a black carbuncle at the place of the prick on the fourth, and from the sixth either death or a slow mending, seven of the eighteen dying; and from every sheep that fell sick the same micro-mana were raised again, alike in shape, in size and in the manner of their multiplying. Of the sheep into which the fluid for raising was put, not one fell sick. In groups A and B one sheep each did not fall sick, nor had any fever in the twelve days, nor showed micro-mana in its fluids; I suspect that the prick was too shallow and little of the drop went in, or that these two sheep do not take it by nature; but I did not search into it, and do not presume to say so, nor did I try again.

The tide-carbuncle of mortals I cannot raise again by inoculating men; but micro-mana taken from the sick and raised in the dish are alike with those of the sheep in shape, in size and in the manner of multiplying, and leave the same grains when dead. I also took a single kind from the blood of two of the sick, one from each, raised each to the tenth passage and put it into four whole sheep; seven of the eight took the tide-carbuncle, and from every one of them the same micro-mana were raised again. And so I take them to be one kind.

**CHAPTER 5. THE ORDER WARD**

Group D of Table III is the same kind, of the tenth passage, first set for two days within an order ward and then put in. Within the ward the micro-mana divided no more from the first day, and by the second had all shrunk into grains; a drop of it put into a new dish and raised for three days did not multiply again. Of twelve sheep, each given one drop of a culture so treated, not one fell sick: when the micro-mana of the culture are dead, inoculation causes the sickness no more.

I have also tried setting an order ward over a sick sheep. The sheep was not the better for it, nor did the micro-mana in its fluids grow fewer; it may be that a ward covering a whole sheep falls far short of the strength of a ward covering a single dish.

**CHAPTER 6. WHENCE THE MICRO-MANA COME**

I hold that the micro-mana are very small monsters cast forth by the tide. I have three reasons. First, in the tide years both the cases and the micro-mana in the fluids are several times as many, and mostly in the tide months. Secondly, in the first ten days of the Month of Reawakening of 1190, the first twenty and more cases in the city fell in four quarters far apart, the East Wall, the North Slope, the West Quay and the South Terrace; in those days the harbour book shows no ship come from any stricken city, and the sick had had no dealings with one another. Thirdly, the micro-mana leave mana crystals when they die, according to the rule of monsters; and monsters are what mana, twisted, casts forth into the present world, and in the tide mana rises and falls most, and casts forth most.

Yet I do not deny that the sick and the beasts carry them away. In the register, the cases of each year outside the tide years, a dozen to thirty and more, are mostly in the quarters along the air-routes; and after the Month of Summer Haze of 1190 the new cases in the city were mostly about the quays. Whether the micro-mana are cast forth by the tide where they are found, or brought along the air-routes by the sick and by beasts, or both, and in what measure each, the physicians of other cities must try each for himself by my method: in their own cities to see, to raise and to inoculate, and to compare the cases with the traffic of the routes.

**CHAPTER 7. CONCLUSION**

From the chapters above I have proved four things. First, the tide-carbuncle rises and falls with the ten-year tide, and in the tide years there are most micro-mana in the fluids of the sick. Secondly, the fine mana crystals into which dead micro-mana shrink are found in the bodies of men and beasts dead of the tide-carbuncle, and not in the whole. Thirdly, micro-mana taken from a sick body and kept apart multiply generation after generation; a single kind put into whole sheep raises the same sickness, and the same micro-mana can be raised again from the sick sheep; the fluid for raising without micro-mana does not do so. Fourthly, after treatment with an order ward the micro-mana multiply no more, and inoculation no longer causes sickness.

I hold that to say that a plague which rises with the tide and can be raised again in beasts arises from a micro-mana, all four must be proved of it; if any is lacking, one can say only that it is suspected. Those so proved are, to this day, the tide-carbuncle and two other plagues, of sheep and of the floating fowl, whose registers, fluids and inoculations are written in the books of the hospital and not printed here; of other plagues I do not yet know whether they can be raised again in beasts, and so I do not presume to say that they too arise from micro-mana.

There are two things more that I cannot answer. Why the Silver Folk do not take these plagues: I have taken the fluids of more than ten of the Silver Folk of the city and seen not one micro-mana, but whether they breathe them in and cast them out, or cannot take them in at all, I do not know. Whether the micro-mana in the sick can be killed without harm to the sick: the ward I tried over a sheep did no good, and some other means must be found.

I have had these chapters printed, and I ask the physicians of every city to come and search my register, to look at my dishes, and to do it all again by my method in their own cities.

## The Decipherment of the Third Script of the Babel Relic

**THE DECIPHERMENT OF THE THIRD SCRIPT OF THE BABEL RELIC**

By APPLEBY.

[Received the Month of Frostfall, F.E. 353.]

**Summary.** In the scrolls of the Babel Relic, beside the two scripts that can be read, there is a “third script” which no one since Severin has been able to read. It is shown here to be an archaic form of the thirty-six thousand characters: a sign is for the most part an old form of a single character with its strokes compressed, and in a few cases a joining of two or three characters; and its rules of combination, when the present order is reversed, agree with Roxburgh’s grammar of the characters. The inference engine went through every surviving fragment of the third script in the Relic: from a thousand of them it proposed the correspondences of form, which were checked one by one by the author and scholars of the Tower of Tongues; and when the table of correspondences had been fixed, the other four hundred and twelve were read by it. The checking had three standards: the correspondences must account for the phrases and rules of combination that recur in the fragments; what is read must agree with the known parallel passages; and they must read through the fragments that took no part in drawing up the table of correspondences. All three are met: of 1,148 recurring phrases, 1,102 read as lawful combinations of clear sense; all 27 parallel passages agree; and of 412 fragments that took no part in drawing up the table, 388 are read through. The dangerous fragments were handled only in safe transcriptions, in an authorised isolated room, and were not added to the materials of the general engine. Such contents as were read and belong to the Starlight grade or above have been reported to the Great Library for grading, and are not printed here.

**1. Introduction**

Severin’s Twenty-Fourth Question asks: “Can the third script in the scrolls of the Babel Relic be read?” [1] Two scripts in the scrolls of the Relic have always been read: the thirty-six thousand characters in their present form, and the script in which the people of Babel wrote their records. The third script lies among them, sometimes filling a whole scroll, sometimes only a few lines. The copyists copy it stroke by stroke as they copy the rest, but do not know what they copy.

Three roads have been tried. One is to compare it with the characters: its signs are like none of the thirty-six thousand, and the comparing has yielded only some tens of seeming likenesses. Another is to compare it with other ancient scripts: after Llewellyn had set out the descent of the scripts [3], some placed it in a branch of the primal runes, but read according to that branch it yields no sentence. The third is to count it: its signs are more than two thousand kinds, more than any script that writes sounds and far fewer than the thirty-six thousand characters, so that some have held it to be a lost tongue, of which the twenty-odd parallel passages noted in the catalogues of the Relic are only translations, too few to read the rest by.

This paper takes the first road, but compares in another way: not the single forms, but the places where they occur. Lynn’s correspondence formula can find, in a very long text, the places that are related to one another [6], and the inference engine that has appeared this year can verify across volumes [7]. I asked the engine to compare, for every sign of the third script, the places where it occurs, the signs before and after it, and the signs with which it occurs together, with the same distributions of the thirty-six thousand characters in known texts, and to propose candidates of the form “this sign is that character”. The candidates are not settled by the engine; they are checked one by one by men.

The conclusion of this paper is that the third script is an archaic form of the thirty-six thousand characters.

**2. Materials**

There are in the Relic 1,412 surviving fragments containing the third script; see Table I. Of these, 1,187 have been brought down by assessment of safety to the Dusk grade or below; 225 remain in the Starlight grade, and by the charter were read only in the Starlight Vault: those who handled them all hold the higher academic standing of the Silver Society and the written leave of the head of the Library; what was used was the safe transcriptions made by the Tower of Tongues within the Vault, in which signs that carry mana are kept only as codes, and which do not leave it; and the engine that read them was a separate one set up in the Vault, not joined to the general engine, whose readings and records are kept at the Starlight grade and are not added to the materials of the general engine. No fragment of the Abyss grade was used.

The thousand fragments used for the table were put into codes by the Great Library’s Code Table [5]; signs that carry mana are, as always, not copied in their own forms, but kept only as codes. The 412 fragments kept for the trial had been read neither by the engine nor by those who checked, before the table was drawn up; nor were they, or any copies of them, among the archives of the Code Table on which the first engine was trained, for the third script of the Relic has been kept only in the scrolls themselves and in the copies of the Tower of Tongues, and was never taken into those archives; and the four scholars who checked with me had not handled them before. Sixty-one of them were released, being brought down from the Starlight grade to the Dusk, only after the table had been fixed; they are counted among the 187 kept for the trial, Table I giving the grades as they stood at the trial. Release by bringing down the grade is, by the charter, proposed by the keeper of the hall and decided by the council of the Library; the fragment in which Stafford read the proof of the Fourth Question was released in the same way [8].

**3. What the engine did**

In drawing up the table the engine did one thing only: it compared distributions and proposed candidates. For each sign it reckoned the places where the sign occurs in the fragments, the signs that commonly come before and after it, and the number of times it occurs together with other signs; it then sought in the known texts of the thirty-six thousand characters the characters whose distributions are nearest, ranked several candidates by their nearness, and attached to each the passages of the fragments and the comparisons on which it was proposed [7]. By the distribution is meant the three signs on either side, counted without regard to the side on which they stand, the place in the line, and the number of times a sign stands in one line with others; the known texts are the old copies, from before the First Era, gathered in the Code Table, and not the scrolls of the Relic. The joinings of two or three characters were proposed in the same way, the engine taking two or three characters that commonly stand together in the present writing as one, and comparing their distribution. This year’s report proposed that whatever an inference engine gives should carry sources and derivations that can be checked; this paper has followed that proposal, and all the candidates with their sources are kept in the Tower of Tongues, where they may be consulted according to the grades of the fragments they touch.

The candidates were checked by men. Four scholars of the Tower of Tongues and I checked them one by one: a candidate was written into the table only if it accounted for the recurring phrases and rules of combination in which it stood, which is the first standard of Section 5. The engine proposed 3,871 candidates in all, of which 2,611 were written into the table; the 1,260 not written in were for the most part second choices for the same signs. Among those it proposed were many that looked well but failed when checked: some only because the two signs both stood commonly at the head of a sentence; some whose sources could not be found, or were found but did not say what was claimed. When the table had been fixed, it was set as a whole against the parallel passages and read upon the fragments kept for the trial, which are the second and third standards. Both these materials were held back while the table was drawn up: the copies of the parallel passages in the present characters are in the Relic, and so were never among the known texts with which the engine compared, nor did those who checked turn to them; and the fragments kept for the trial, as was said above, had been read by no one. Every correspondence in the table has passed the first; those that occur in the parallel passages or in the fragments kept for the trial had to pass the second and the third as well, and any found against them there would have been struck out; not one was.

**4. Forms and combinations**

The checked table shows several features of the script.

First, a sign is for the most part an old form of one character. The old forms have fewer strokes than the present ones, as though several strokes of the present form had been drawn into one and the places of the parts had been moved. Each of the 2,611 checked signs has one checked reading only: 2,437 correspond each to one character, and 174 are joinings of two or three characters, like the joined writing of today. Among the 2,437 there are ninety-eight pairs that are two old forms of one character, so that they answer to 2,339 different characters.

Secondly, its rules of combination are the reverse of today’s. Roxburgh’s grammar of the characters [2] lays down the lawful combinations of today: the qualifying character first, the qualified after. The third script writes them the other way about. What is reversed is only the order of the qualifying and the qualified within a combination; the order of the combinations in a line, and the order in which the four elements are named, are as today. When its combinations are reversed, they accord with Roxburgh’s grammar. It was for this reason that those before, comparing in today’s order, could find no likeness.

Thirdly, its signs are fewer than today’s characters. One reason is that these fragments write of only so many things; another, it may be, is that some characters did not yet exist. Of the 2,339 characters so checked, fewer than half are in common use today; many are rare today, or have been seen only in old inscriptions.

Let one sign show how a correspondence was settled. The sign coded T-0417 in the Code Table occurs 1,926 times in the fragments used for the table, sixty-two times in a hundred at the head of a line, and is commonly followed by several signs of another family; it stands in the same line with the three signs afterwards read as Oss, Vo and Flame 112 times. Among the candidates the engine ranked for it, the first was Eaam, and the second another character that commonly opens a calling. By the first standard, the forty-one recurring phrases in which it stands all become lawful invocations when it is read as Eaam, while read as the second, seventeen of them break the grammar; and T-0417 was written into the table as Eaam. The three signs read as Oss, Vo and Flame were each settled by the phrases in which they themselves stand, and not by T-0417. When the table had been fixed, it was found to stand, in the nine parallel passages of invocations, exactly where the present characters write Eaam; and among the fragments kept for the trial there are seventeen with lines calling upon the four elements, in all of which it reads through. The reversed order of combination was found in the same way: of the pairs of candidates the engine proposed, eight or nine in ten ran contrary to the order of today’s combinations, and only when they were reversed did the checked combinations all agree with Roxburgh’s grammar.

Fig. 1 groups the signs into twelve families by the parts they share, and the characters into ten classes by their parts, both groupings being fixed before the table was drawn up, and the classes set in order, after the table was fixed, by the family to which each most corresponds; the shade of each cell is the number of checked correspondences between the two, a joining being counted by the first character of its reading. The signs of each family correspond for the most part to one or two classes of characters, which agrees with their coming from one set of parts.

The first standard. Of the 1,148 recurring phrases, 1,102 when read by the table accord with Roxburgh’s grammar and their sense is clear. Of the other forty-six, thirty-one contain signs not yet read; fifteen can be read and are lawful, but their sense is obscure. Not one reads against the grammar.

The second standard. A parallel passage is one of which the Relic holds another copy written in the present characters; which answers to which was noted long ago in the catalogues of the Relic, by the titles in the present characters at the heads of the scrolls, and was not picked out after the reading, and the catalogues note these twenty-seven and no others. Of the twenty-seven, nine are invocations of activation, such as the four lines calling upon earth, wind, water and flame, where the signs of the third script answer exactly to Eaam, Oss, Vo and Flame [2]; seven are the names and order of the twelve mana moons; six are pieces of the Hymn of Creation; and the other five are catalogues and colophons. The nine invocations and the seven namings of the moons, however, are formulas known to all, and phrases of the same kind were among those of the first standard, so that their agreement weighs the less; the six pieces of the Hymn and the five catalogues and colophons are longer and are no formulas, and their agreement weighs the more.

The third standard is the most important: had the table merely been fitted together, it would hardly read the fragments it had never seen. The manner of dividing the lines, of opening the joinings and of reversing the order was fixed before the trial and not changed during it; and whether a fragment reads or not was judged by two scholars of the Tower of Tongues who had taken no part in drawing up the table. Of the 412 fragments kept for the trial, 388 are read through, that is, more than nine in ten of the signs that remain can be read and the sense runs on; 19 are read more than half, but not through; and the five not read are all fragments so damaged that only some ten or twenty signs remain. Of the sixty-one released only after the table had been fixed, fifty-nine are read through, and the other two more than half. For comparison a table falsely matched was also tried: the checked correspondences were exchanged at random among signs of one family that occur about equally often, and the 412 fragments read by it; not one could be read through.

**6. What was read, and what is not printed**

The fragments read through are for the most part descriptions of laws, orders of rites, catalogues and colophons. Part of their contents belongs to the Starlight grade or above: by section 18 of the Law on Research and the Keeping of Privileged Knowledge it was reported to the Great Library for grading within ninety days [10], and by section 21 of the League Heritage Law this paper was sent, before printing, to the Great Library and the Commission for the Control of Forbidden Arts for review [11]. The examples and the figures given in this paper are all taken from the parts the review allowed to be printed: the only signs it allowed to be shown are those of the line of Fig. 2, and only a few codes are named in the text; the whole table of correspondences is kept in the Tower of Tongues according to the grades of the fragments it touches. The counts the review allowed to be printed as they are. The whole of what was read is kept in the Tower of Tongues, to be consulted according to the grade fixed.

**7. What remains**

Two hundred and ninety-three signs have not yet been read; together they make only eight in a thousand of all occurrences, and most occur only a few times.

Some signs of the third script carry mana. Llewellyn says that within one branch of writing, the later the form, the weaker the mana it carries [3]; on the reading of this paper the third script is an older branch of the thirty-six thousand characters, and that it carries mana is no wonder. But whether the thirty-six thousand characters descend from it, or both come from a still older branch, this paper cannot decide.

Whether the characters suffice, that is, the first half of Severin’s Tenth Question, of which Monkton settled only a part [4], this paper does not touch: nothing in what has been read of the third script is beyond the present characters to write; but this shows only that what has been read can be written, says nothing of the 293 signs not read, and does not show that every law can be. The second half, whether there are superfluous characters, Merrick has proved [9]; in the third script the ten or so superfluous characters each have their old forms as well, so that they were already in use in old times.
