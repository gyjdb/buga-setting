# 《联盟学术名篇》英文原刊（第一批）

## On the Drawing of Power by String-Spells

**ON THE DRAWING OF POWER BY STRING-SPELLS**

By WESTPHAL, of White Tower, Fellow of the White Tower Society.

[Read before the Society in the Month of Frostfall, C.R. 929; printed in the Annals for the Year of the Hourglass, C.R. 930.]

It is the common opinion, which I shall call the doctrine of two layers, that the world consists of the elements and of Tiamat only, and that the lines of law are Tiamat itself; that elemental workings draw their power through the elements, and that law-workings and string-spells alike draw theirs directly from the lines of law. On this opinion a law-working and a string-spell draw from one and the same place, and whatever disturbs the lines of law must disturb them both.

Yet it has long been reported among casters that a place of warding against magic will often fail to hold a string-spell. The older explanation is that string-spells are strong and so break the ward. But a string-spell of a low ring is not necessarily stronger than a law-working of a high one; and in the same warded place the latter cannot be cast while the former is cast as easily as anywhere. This difference is not to be explained by strength. The doctrine of two layers requires the two workings to be disturbed together; the report shows them to behave quite otherwise in a warded place.

The friends of the doctrine have offered two other explanations of the report. Some say that a ward is never perfectly close, and that the string-spell draws its power through the gaps. If this were so, a string-spell should fail in some place more strictly warded; yet no one has ever found a ward that holds it. Others say that string-spells are worked in the tongue of dragons, which the lines of law cannot lock. This is merely to give the matter a new name. It does not explain why locking the lines should not lock the dragon tongue; and if the dragon tongue does indeed pass by the lines of law, it concedes that the string-spell draws its power from some other place.

There is a further circumstance. Where the Babel fragments speak of a web, they use three different words. Former interpreters took the three for one, and rendered each as Tiamat. But the same fragments say that Masha wove Tiamat upon the Sea of Mana. If Tiamat were no more than a layer of the lines of law, then what was woven upon the sea and what the law-mage plucks would be one thing, and the fragments would have had no occasion for three words.

These things led me to suppose that a law-working and a string-spell draw their power from different places; and further, that every working draws its power through several layers lying one upon another, and the higher it takes hold, the more layers its power must pass. I shall raise this supposition, which I call the doctrine of layers, to the rank of a premiss, and I shall show that the assumption that the lines of law are Tiamat is superfluous for the explanation of what is observed. The difficulty of the doctrine of two layers lies in a matter that has never been thought through: by what right one asserts that two workings draw their power from the same place. With that matter I begin.

The First Part (§§ 1–5) explains what is meant by disturbance, how the places whence power is drawn are to be discerned, how the order of the layers may be read from the manner of disturbance, and how losses compound when several layers are disturbed together. The Second Part (§§ 6–14) records the trials, answers the objections that may be raised, establishes the doctrine upon the trials, and draws some consequences.

**PART I. — OF DISCERNMENT**

**§ 1. Of Disturbance**

When a working is cast in some surrounding, and its expenditure or its steadiness differs from what it shows when cast in a place of comparison, and the difference appears again and again on repetition and exceeds the variation among the comparisons themselves, I say that the working is disturbed in that surrounding.

There is as yet no instrument that weighs the mana spent in a single casting, and the expenditure must therefore be measured indirectly. Charged crystals are taken from one vein, none of them used before; the caster casts the same working again and again, drawing upon the power of a single crystal only, until its light goes out, and the number of castings is recorded. The fewer the castings, the greater the expenditure. The light is reckoned out when the face of the crystal, held under the same shaded lamp, no longer shows its grain; a crystal once spent is not used again. Every casting is counted, whether it takes form or not and whether it deviates or not; but a last casting begun as the light goes out, which takes no form, is not counted.

That the caster draws upon the crystal alone is tested thus: before and after each day’s castings he tries his own remaining strength with one and the same small working, and finds it undiminished. This trial can discern a loss of about two or three castings of C, and no smaller; nor can it discern what he may recover between castings. That all that was spent came from the crystal is therefore an approximation only; but comparison and disturbance are cast to the same timetable, without rest within a round and with an hour between rounds, so that the approximation is the same on both sides.

One point more. The crystal is only the vessel that supplies the power for the time being. I assume that a working cast upon a crystal’s power still takes hold at its own layer and draws through the same path, the power stored in the crystal having come in the end from the Sea of Mana; were it otherwise, the trials would measure only how a crystal supplies different workings, and not the places whence they draw. To test this we asked the casters to cast the three workings in storm and within the array upon their own strength, some hundreds of times each, counting not the castings but the deviations only: A deviated more in both places, B within the array only, and C in neither, as when cast upon crystals. These hundreds of castings were spread over more than ten days, no more than twenty or thirty of each working in a day, lest the casters tire; the same castings in a calm place served for comparison.

Steadiness is measured thus. At each casting it is observed whether the working takes the form intended. For A and B, a flame higher or lower than the hand’s breadth set down by a quarter of a hand or more, or of any colour but the orange-yellow set down, is counted a deviation; for C, the object to be perceived is chosen each time by the assistant, by lot, from twelve small things and laid in the casket, the caster not knowing which, and a perception that is wrong or that finds nothing is counted a deviation. Whether a casting deviates is decided by the recorder, by the standards set down and the slip of the lot. The number of deviations in a hundred castings is taken as the measure of steadiness.

A surrounding acts not only upon the drawing of power but upon what the working produces. A flame in a storm is bent by the wind, which is an action of the wind upon the fire and has nothing to do with the place whence the fire draws its power. To remove this the caster stands within the disturbance, but what he produces is formed within a small chamber, closed against the wind and graven on every wall with signs that bar the elements; and only the instant of forming is observed. The castings of comparison are formed in the same chamber, so that whatever the chamber itself may do is the same on both sides. Whether the chamber acts together with the disturbances was also tried. A second chamber was made, closed against the wind but without the signs that bar the elements, and some sets of A and B were cast with it in storm and within the array. In storm A deviated more in this chamber, but gave the same number of castings as in the graven one; within the array the two chambers gave the same. The signs, then, keep the disturbance from what is produced, and do not alter the drawing of power. In this paper the drawing of power is judged chiefly by the castings, the deviations serving as further witness.

The casting of one crystal from its first use until it goes dark I call a round; several rounds by the same caster, of the same working, in the same surrounding, I call a set. The numbers of castings given in the tables are the means of all the rounds in the place concerned, and the deviations are reckoned in each hundred castings. By the variation I mean the greatest difference between the means of the sets of comparison.

**§ 2. Of the Place whence Power is Drawn**

We cannot see whence a working draws its power; we see only in what disturbances it is disturbed. In this paper, therefore, the place whence power is drawn is judged by the disturbances alone. If two workings are disturbed or undisturbed alike by every disturbance, we have no means of telling their places apart; if a single disturbance disturbs the one and not the other, they differ at least in that part of their drawing which the disturbance reaches, and this I call drawing from different places. The more disturbances are tried, the more places can be told apart; and there are at least as many places as there are different manners of being disturbed among the workings tried. By “places” I mean such as the trials can distinguish; whether any of them may be divided further I do not consider. To judge in this way it is not necessary to assume any doctrine of Tiamat beforehand; a doctrine serves only to explain what the trials show.

**§ 3. That Sameness of Place is Relative**

By the rule of the preceding section it cannot be said absolutely whether two workings draw from one place; it depends upon the disturbances with which they are tried. If storm alone is tried, neither the law-working nor the string-spell is disturbed, and they cannot be told apart; if the tide alone, all three workings are disturbed, and again they cannot be told apart; only when the locking of lines is tried do the law-working and the string-spell part company. “The same place” and “a different place” are therefore spoken relatively to the disturbances tried. As more disturbances are tried, what was taken for one place may be divided; but what has once been divided is never joined again, so long as the judgments of disturbance on which the division rests are not themselves overturned.

The friends of the doctrine of two layers say that law-workings and string-spells draw from one place because they have tried them only by storms and by the unbalancing of the elements, in which neither is disturbed, so that the two look alike. They did not see wrongly; they tried too few disturbances.

**§ 4. How the Order of the Layers may be Read from the Manner of Disturbance**

When several disturbances and several workings have been tried, let the disturbances that disturb each working be written in a row; this I call a table of disturbance.

If the layers lie one upon another, if each disturbance reaches one layer only, if the path by which each working draws is a continuous stretch beginning from the lowest layer, and if every working whose path passes a disturbed layer is found disturbed in the trials, then the table of disturbance must have a particular form. Let the workings be arranged according to the number of disturbances that disturb them: then the disturbances of each working include all those of the working before it. Such a table I call a nested table.

Conversely, if the table of disturbance is nested, an order of the layers agreeing with these conditions may be read from it thus. The disturbances of the working least disturbed are to be taken as reaching the lowest layer; those by which the next working exceeds it, as reaching the layer above; and so on. The layer at which each working takes hold is that reached by the uppermost of its disturbances. The form of the table shows only that the conditions can hold together; it does not by itself prove that the world is so made. And the order is fixed uniquely only if each working exceeds the one before by a single disturbance; where it exceeds it by two or more, the order of the layers these reach cannot be told from the table, and workings that take hold between them must be tried.

If the table is not nested — if, for example, each of two workings is disturbed by something that does not disturb the other — then one at least of the conditions fails: either the layers do not simply lie one upon another, or some disturbance reaches more than one layer, or the path of some working is broken; and the doctrine of layers must be amended. Here the doctrine may be overthrown.

**§ 5. Of the Compounding of Losses**

Since power passes layer by layer, each disturbed layer that it passes takes its own abatement. Let a crystal give n₀ castings in comparison, and nᵢ when the i-th layer alone is disturbed; I call fᵢ = nᵢ/n₀ the abatement of the i-th layer. Suppose that the power stored in a crystal is fixed, so that the number of castings is inversely as the expenditure of each, the remnant short of a casting not being counted; and suppose that the disturbance of each layer increases the expenditure of a casting in a fixed proportion, and not by a fixed amount, the proportions of the several layers being independent of one another. Then when several layers are disturbed together the number of castings a crystal gives should be

$$ n = n_0 times f_1 times f_2 times dots $$ (1)

where only the disturbed layers on the working’s path enter the product, the abatement of any layer not on the path being reckoned as one. This rule I call the compounding of losses. It is not among the three premisses of § 12 but is added to them, and Table III of § 9 is a test of it. Were the disturbance of each layer merely to add a fixed amount to the expenditure, the castings under several disturbances would not be the product but somewhat more; and Table III decides between the two. By the additive reckoning the castings under several disturbances satisfy 1/n = 1/n₁ + 1/n₂ − 1/n₀.

The compounding of losses says only how the abatements of the several layers combine for one and the same working; whether one layer abates different workings alike is another matter, and does not follow from it. In Table I the abatement by the locking of lines is 31/40, or about 0.78, for the elemental working, and 29/41, or about 0.71, for the law-working; both are of the second ring, but the difference lies at the edge of the variation and cannot be settled. The abatement by the tide, taken from the second tide year (§ 8), is 29/40 for the elemental working and 30/41 for the law-working, both about 0.73, but only 13/22, or about 0.59, for the string-spell, which is plainly different. The abatement of one layer, then, may differ from working to working. How it varies with the kind of working, its ring and its expenditure, the workings tried here, all of low rings, permit no finer division, and I leave this point to others.

**PART II. — OF THE TRIALS**

**§ 6. The Three Workings**

The three workings tried were all of low rings, lest the expenditure be too great and the casters, being wearied, disturb the counts.

A, the elemental working: a flame some hand’s breadth high, gathered from the element of fire; of the second ring.

B, the law-working: a flame of the same height, made by plucking the lines of law; also of the second ring. A and B produce the same thing by different paths, and are therefore fit to be compared.

C, the string-spell: a perceiving string-spell of a low ring, cast by a fellow of the White Tower Society versed in the lower string-spells, by leave of the Tower.

Three casters were engaged for each of A and B; for C there was but one who could cast it. That C came from a single hand, and that only one string-spell was tried, are two defects of this paper.

**§ 7. The Three Disturbances**

First, the elemental disturbance: the stormy airs where the elements are out of balance. In the Months of Autumn Dusk and of Frostfall the element of air runs wild in the stormy airs north of White Tower, and the other elements fall short. We sailed into these airs and cast aboard the ship. Whether the elements were out of balance was judged by four crystal plates of the four elements hung from the mast: the trials began when the plate of air shone and the other three were dim, and when the four were of like brightness the ship sailed out and the castings served for comparison.

Second, the disturbance of laws: within a line-locking array (Fig. 1), in which several lines of law are locked. The lines locked were to have nothing to do with those used by the three workings; had the very line of fire been locked, A and B could not have been cast at all, which would show only that the line of fire was locked and nothing of the place whence power is drawn. That they had nothing to do with them was judged first by the customary division of the lines among law-mages and in the manuscripts of the Tower, by which the lines that serve a flame and a perception belong to other divisions than those locked, and have never been found joined with them; and then by trial: after each group was locked, a candle lit without any working was set in the chamber, and the height and colour of its flame did not change, nor did the look of the things in the casket, so that the lines locked act directly neither upon the flame nor upon the thing perceived. After each opening of the array a law-mage first tried to pluck the locked lines, and only when they would not move were they held locked. The lines locked were changed seven times in all; see Table IV. That the locking reaches only the layer woven of the lines of law is one of the premisses of § 12; these trials support it, but do not prove it.

Fig. 1. The arrangement of the trial by locking. The array stands in the middle; the three casters stand within it some scores of paces apart, with screens between them; the flames are formed in the chamber barred against the elements within the array, and the recorder watches the chamber through a window from outside the hall; C’s casket stands beside the chamber; the assistant, outside the array, draws a lot to decide whether it shall be opened, and changes the thing in the casket through a passage from outside.

Third, the tidal disturbance: the height of the tide of mana. The tide comes once in ten years, and its height lasts no more than some ten days. The days of the height were judged by the tide-reports of the cities and the common feeling of the casters; the charged crystals also glow faintly of themselves about the height, which is further evidence. Since the crystals glow of themselves, the point at which the light is reckoned out might be moved. We therefore kept the crystals that had gone dark at the height until the tide had ebbed, and looked again: their faces still showed no grain, and not a single casting could be had from them; and crystals that had gone dark in storm or within the array were the same when taken out. None of the three disturbances, then, alters the relation between the going out of the light and the power remaining: when the light is out, what remains is short of a single casting.

**§ 8. Of Comparison and Repetition**

To every disturbance belongs a set of comparison, cast by the same caster, with the same batch of crystals and the same working, outside the disturbance. For the trial by storm the comparison was cast in calm air after the same ship had sailed out of the stormy airs, the ship sailing in and out by turns on the same day, with some rounds cast each time; for the trial by locking, in the same hall when the array was not opened; for the trial by tide, in the same place, once in the month before the tide and once after it had ebbed, the two agreeing, so that the height lay between them. The three sets of comparison agree with one another and are recorded together below.

The three workings were cast in the same place at the same instant: the three casters stood some scores of paces apart and cast at a signal, lest a difference of hours be mixed with the difference of workings. That casting at such distances causes no mutual disturbance was ascertained beforehand. The three casters of A and of B took their turns. Whether the array was opened was decided by the assistant by lot, the casters not knowing beforehand, lest expectation affect the casting. The procedure was the same whether the array was opened or not: the assistant went to the urn of lots outside the array, and the law-mage tried the lines, as always; when the array was not opened the lines moved under his hand, and he said nothing. The figures of the array were graven beneath the floor of the hall, out of the casters’ sight. The recorder who judged the deviations watched the chamber through a window from outside the hall, and he too did not know whether the array was open; the lots were unsealed only when the day’s trials were done. The chamber had a window on the side towards the outside of the hall; C’s casket stood beside the chamber, and the assistant changed the thing in it through a passage from outside the array, never entering it. Screens stood between the three casters, and between rounds they rested apart and did not speak, so that none could see when another stopped because his crystal had gone dark.

The casters practised for a year before the trials proper began, until the number of castings a crystal gave them in comparison varied by no more than two from day to day. The castings and deviations of each round were recorded by two clerks separately, and entered only when the two agreed; a round on which they disagreed was discarded and cast again. In twenty years some forty rounds were so discarded, most of them in storm; when they are restored, each with the mean of what the two clerks recorded, no number in Table I changes by more than one, and the conclusions are unchanged.

The trials by storm and by locking were made both at White Tower and at Lataki, and the two places agree (Table V); White Tower tried about twice as many rounds as Lataki, so that the combined numbers of Table I mostly agree with those of White Tower. The trial by tide must await the tide years. In the first, C was tried for forty rounds only, and that as the height was passing; a crystal gave on the mean 19 castings, against 21 in comparison that year, only two fewer, which is within the variation of the comparisons, and nothing could be decided. In the second tide year eighty rounds were cast while the height was full (the nine days within four of the height, some nine rounds a day), giving on the mean 13 castings against 22 in comparison, and the difference was settled. The daily counts of C in the second tide year are shown in Fig. 2. A and B were tried in full during the height in both years: in the first, whose tide was the weaker, A gave 31 castings and B 32; in the second, A 29 and B 30. The column of the tide in Table I combines the two years; for C, the 19 castings of forty rounds and the 13 of eighty make 15.

**§ 10. Objections**

If the differences of Table I did not arise from the places whence power is drawn, they might arise from other causes. I have thought of six, and remove them in turn.

First, the expectation of the caster. A caster who knew in which disturbance he stood might, without intending it, put forth more or less strength. Storm and tide cannot be hidden from him, but the array can; the trials by locking were therefore opened by lot, the casters not knowing. The most important place in Table II — that B is disturbed by locking and C is not — was obtained in trials in which the casters did not know. By not knowing I mean not knowing before the array was opened, nor as it was opened (§ 8); whether the array, once open, made any change that the caster could feel in the course of casting, these trials cannot wholly exclude.

Second, a difference among the crystals. Crystals of one vein do not all hold the same charge. The crystals used for each working in each surrounding were assigned by lot from the same batch. To measure the time a crystal takes to go dark it must be spent, and a crystal so measured cannot be used again; from each batch, therefore, twenty were first drawn, and in a calm place a caster held one and the same small light upon each until it went dark, and the time was recorded. A batch in which these times differed too much was not used at all, and two batches were set aside in this way.

Third, the single caster of the string-spell. Since C came from one hand only, it might be thought that this fellow was himself peculiarly proof against locking. He was versed also in law-workings, and we asked him to cast B within the array. He was disturbed like the rest: a crystal gave him 30 castings against 41 in comparison, as with the other three casters of B. It is not, then, because he is himself proof against every locking that he was undisturbed by it. Whether it is so only when he casts this one string-spell, these trials cannot tell: one caster only cast the string-spell, and one string-spell only was tried (§ 6).

Fourth, an action of the disturbance upon what is produced. This was removed in § 1: the flames were formed in the chamber closed against wind and barred against the elements, whose own action was also tried; and the object perceived by C, changed each time by lot, was always set at the same distance, in the same casket.

Fifth, an action of the disturbance upon the crystals themselves. If storm, locking or tide made a crystal leak its charge, the castings would be fewer for that reason. We set unused crystals of the same batch, uncast, in each of the three disturbances and in the places of comparison for a day, and then measured, in a calm place and in the manner just described, the time each took to go dark: it was the same in all. At the height the tide is everywhere, and there is no calm place outside it; for the tide, therefore, half of a batch was set in the height and measured after the ebb, and compared with the other half measured before the tide, and again they agreed; and in an ordinary year the same days of rest did not change the time to go dark. A hindrance arising only while power is drawn could not be found by setting crystals aside; the trial of the point of darkness in § 7 makes good some part of this.

Sixth, the changes of twenty years. In twenty years casters grow old and instruments wear. But the comparison for each disturbance was always cast in the same year, at the same place, by the same caster, and never compared across years; the changes of many years fall alike upon both sides. Within a year, weariness and practice were evened out in the storm by sailing in and out by turns, and in the tide by the comparisons before and after, between which the height lay. The array could be opened by lot, but storm and tide must be waited for, and the comparisons for these two are never so clean as those for the array.

**§ 11. That the Doctrine of Two Layers cannot account for the Table**

By the doctrine of two layers the lines of law are Tiamat, and B and C both draw from them. Then the locking that disturbs B must disturb C; but C is not disturbed.

If it be said that C draws from another part of the lines, which the array happened not to lock, then locking other lines ought sometimes to disturb C. We changed the locked lines seven times (Table IV): C was never disturbed, and B always was. If it be said that the tide disturbs C by way of the lines of law, then an array that locks the lines ought to stop, or at least to alter, the action of the tide upon C. In the second tide year we cast C within the array, and it was disturbed by the tide exactly as outside it (Table III).

Without further assumptions the doctrine of two layers cannot yield a result in which B is disturbed by locking and C is not; and the two assumptions just considered both disagree with the trials. The doctrine of two layers, in the form that what draws from one place is disturbed alike, therefore cannot stand, at least as regards string-spells. Seven groups of locked lines do not exhaust the lines of law; should it be further assumed that the array can never lock the part the string-spell uses, these trials cannot exclude it. But such an assumption is made for this one place in the table, whereas the doctrine of layers accounts for the whole table by one account, and foretold Table III besides.

**§ 12. The Three Premisses**

I take the following three as premisses, and inquire whether they account for Table II.

First, the source of mana lies lowest. All the power that any working draws comes in the end from the Sea of Mana, the sea upon which, by the fragments, Masha wove her web; and the tide of mana rises there.

Second, between the source and the caster there are several layers lying one upon another. A working that takes hold at a certain layer must draw its power through all the layers beneath it; this I call its path. The higher it takes hold, the more layers it passes.

Third, each disturbance reaches one layer only. A working whose path passes a disturbed layer shows a discernible change in expenditure and steadiness; one whose path does not, is not affected. The storm reaches the uppermost layer, where the elements are; the locking reaches the layer woven of the lines of law; the tide rises from the source and reaches the lowest layer, next the source.

The premisses do not prescribe beforehand at which layer each working takes hold; that is for the trials to determine. By the method of § 4: C is least disturbed, by the tide alone, so that the tide reaches the lowest layer and C takes hold there; B is disturbed besides by locking, so that the locking reaches the layer above, and B takes hold there; A is disturbed besides by storm, so that the storm reaches the uppermost layer, and A takes hold there. The manner of disturbance of every working is a continuous stretch from the lowest layer upward, none passing over a middle layer; and this is the test of the premisses by Table II. The three layers spoken of are the three that these trials can distinguish; whether any of them may be divided further I do not consider.

The compounding of losses of § 5 may be tested by Table III. In the second tide year A gives under locking alone 31/40 of its comparison, under tide alone 29/40, so that under both it should give 40 × 31/40 × 29/40, or about 22.5 castings; B should give about 21.2; C does not pass the layer of the lines, locking takes nothing from it, and under both it should give what it gives under tide alone, namely 13. The observed numbers in Table III are 22, 21 and 13, each within one casting of the product. Were each layer merely to add a fixed amount to the expenditure, A should give about 24 and B about 23, two more than observed, at the edge of the variation; Table III lies nearer the product. Since the compounding of losses was not used to fix the layers at which the workings take hold, this is a test apart.

**§ 13. The Three Words of the Fragments**

I return now to the three words of the fragments (Table VI). The first word the fragments use where they speak of what was woven upon the sea: it denotes the whole that Masha wove, which is Tiamat. The second they join with “silver lines” and “that may be plucked”: it denotes the layer woven of the lines the law-mage plucks, which I call the Web of Laws. The third they join with words of straining and clearing: it seems to denote a layer between the sea and the web, which strains the wild mana clear. Former interpreters rendered all three as Tiamat, and so took the lines of law to be Tiamat. Compared with Table II, what the second word denotes is just the layer the locking reaches and B takes hold of; what the third denotes is just the layer the tide reaches and C takes hold of. In Table VI a passage repeated in several copies of the fragments is counted once only. That the three words denote three things is my interpretation, not a consequence of their number; they might be synonyms, or differ only as figures of speech. That the interpretation agrees with Table II is a further witness for the doctrine of layers.

| First | eleven | weave, sea, Masha | Tiamat entire |

The world, then, should be thus (Fig. 3). Lowest is the Sea of Mana; upon it Tiamat is laid, whose lower part strains the mana clear and divides its streams, and whose upper part is the Web of Laws; and above the web lies the layer of the elements. The three workings take hold, in order, at the layer of the elements, at the Web of Laws, and at the lower part of Tiamat. How the lower part of Tiamat strains and divides, this paper cannot say. What it has found is the order of the layers, not their make.

**§ 14. Consequences**

First. If every string-spell takes hold at the same layer as C, then by the three premisses no ward made by locking lines or by unbalancing the elements can keep a string-spell from being cast, of whatever ring. This is a prediction; only one string-spell of a low ring has been tried. Sealed places and stores guarded by line-locking arrays alone, therefore, cannot be relied upon to keep string-spells from being cast; whether their walls and doors keep out the caster and what he would take is another matter.

Second. By the three premisses, the path of every working passes the lowest layer, and the tide of mana reaches every one. The three workings tried here all spent more and deviated more at the height of the tide; the casters’ old saying, that in a tide year the hand is heavy, may have this reason. Whether other workings, when disturbed by the tide, likewise spend more and deviate more, I have not tried.

Third. Under the three premisses, and within what these trials can distinguish, the layer at which any working takes hold may be found by the three disturbances: one need only see which disturb it. Witchcraft, runes, holy words and the other kinds of working I have not tried, and I commend this method to those who come after. Should any working be disturbed by storm and not by locking, the table of disturbance would cease to be nested, and the doctrine of layers would have to be amended.

**Conclusion**

The doctrine of two layers was not without ground. As regards elemental workings and law-workings alone it is broadly right: both are disturbed by locking and by the tide, and no difference between them appears. Its error lies only in taking the Web of Laws for the whole of Tiamat.

The trials could not have been made but for the leave of the Tower, which allowed a fellow to cast string-spells, whose name by the custom of the Tower is not given; but for an old shipmaster, who twice a year for twenty years carried us into the storms; and but for my master Atherton. My master has never accepted the conclusion of this paper, yet every year he read my records with care and pointed out their faults; and the greater part of the comparisons here described were devised to answer his objections.

## On Decidable Matters, with an Answer to Severin’s Fifth Question

**ON DECIDABLE MATTERS, WITH AN ANSWER TO SEVERIN’S FIFTH QUESTION**

By CUMMINGS, Kanaich Institute of Technology.

[Received in the Month of Reawakening, F.E. 318, the Year of Dense Fog.]

**Summary.** The reckonable numbers are defined as the numbers whose figures can be written out, one after another, by a rune chain, and reasons are given for holding that every number which can be calculated by rule is of this kind. Three matters are then shown to be undecidable: whether an arbitrary rune chain is inexhaustible; whether it will come to a stop on an arbitrary input; and whether it will ever write a given mark. In the sense here given to the word “means”, the answer to Severin’s Fifth Question is negative. It follows further that no non-trivial property of the behaviour of chains admits a total judge; that chains confined to a fixed number of cells can be judged in principle; and that every system whose proofs can be listed by rule, and whose assertions that a chain never stops are sound, leaves some such assertion unproved. Section 12 treats of insight. An appendix shows that “reckonable” in the present sense and “recursively definable” in the sense of Prescott describe the same functions.

**Introduction**

The “reckonable” numbers may be described, shortly, as the numbers whose figures can be worked out one after another by finite means. Though this is a paper on decidable matters, it begins with reckonable numbers, for the two are handled by one method. It is equally easy to define reckonable functions of an integral variable, and decidable classes of matters, each member of which can be settled by finite means as holding or not holding. By my definition, a number is reckonable if some rune chain will write down its figures one by one.

In §9 I give reasons for supposing that every number which can be calculated by rule is reckonable, and in §10 I show that the numbers actually met with in star-tables, tide-tables and arithmetic — the real roots of algebraic equations, the values of the smooth counting formula, the zeros of the density function — are all reckonable. Numerous as they are, the reckonable numbers can be numbered off one by one, and so do not include all real numbers; in §8 a definite number is exhibited which is not reckonable.

Finally I apply these results to Severin’s Fifth Question, whose words are: “Is there a means by which, before any formula is cast, its outcome may be known?” [1]. When the question was put, a formula meant a spell or an array, and its outcome meant whether it would come to rest or go on working for ever — whether, for instance, a self-adjusting ward would adjust itself without end. The difficulty lies in the word “means”. To show that there is a means one need only produce it; to show that there is none, one must first say what sort of thing a means is.

About F.E. 200 Ashby laid down the universal rune units and the three rules by which they are joined: concatenation, branching and looping [2]. Since then every engine formula can be written as a rune chain in universal units, and every engine type which realises all the units can carry out any rune chain whatever, given enough cells and time. The question may therefore be asked more narrowly: is there a rune chain which, given any rune chain and an input to it, will decide whether the latter will ever come to a stop? Such a chain I shall call a total judge.

The question is not an idle one. Since the universal units became the industrial standard for formulae, an error in the common library of formulae spreads to every city; with a total judge, each formula could be judged before it entered the library. Finley’s proofs of safety for finite-state control formulae in 213 [3], and Morton’s self-checking chains of 288 [4], were steps in that direction. A total judge would also turn many hard questions into a single judgment. Make a chain which, starting from four, tests each even number in turn to see whether it is the sum of two primes, and stops at the first that is not: whether it stops is precisely Severin’s Second Question. Nolan’s proof that it never stops cost him three years merely to mend a gap in the first draft, and the revised draft was examined for ten years more; with a total judge one judgment would have sufficed. Aldin’s conjecture, too, can be turned into the question whether a certain chain stops. A total judge would be too good to be true, and I shall show that it does not exist. Severin himself said that to learn which roads lead nowhere is part of the proper business of learning.

Prescott, of White Tower University, has lately defined by quite different means the “recursively definable functions” [8]. The two definitions differ in appearance but pick out the same functions; the proof is given in the Appendix.

Sections 1–5 define rune chains, reckonable numbers and chain numbers, and give two examples of chains; §§6–7 describe the universal chain; §8 uses the diagonal method to prove three matters undecidable; §§9–10 discuss the extent of what can be done by rule; §11 answers the Fifth Question and draws some consequences; §12 treats of insight.

**1. Rune chains**

A rune chain works upon a storage band divided into cells, each of which can hold one mark taken from a finite table of marks; the band has a beginning, and may be extended to the right as required, without limit. At each step the chain is reading one cell of the band, which I call the cell under reading, and the mark in it, the mark under reading; at the same time the chain is in one of its own units, which I call the present unit. What it does next is fixed by the present unit and the mark under reading alone: it may write a mark in the cell under reading, or erase the mark there; it may move one cell to the left or to the right, or not at all, a move to the left from the first cell leaving it where it is; and it then passes into some unit.

Ashby’s universal units [2] are units of exactly this kind: each prescribes, for each mark that may be read, what is to be written, which way to move, and which unit to pass into. Concatenation is the passage from one unit to the next when it has done its work; branching, the passage to different units according to the mark read; looping, the return to a unit already passed through. Every rune chain written in universal units may therefore be regarded as a chain in the present sense. What is taken here is only the part of the universal units that does the reckoning; where a unit is joined to a mana engine or an array — its supply, its warrant and its coupling — is treated in this paper only as a command the chain writes down, however it may be carried out in the engine.

A chain has a start, the unit in which it begins, and a stop. To pass into the stop is to come to a stop, and the contents of the band are then the output. A chain may also never reach the stop, going round some loops for ever; it is then said never to stop.

Actual calculating engines have a finite number of cells: when these are used up the engine either halts or asks to be furnished with more. I consider abstract chains whose band may be extended at need, because my subject is everything that can be done by rule, and it would be wrong to fix in advance how many cells such doings may use. The case of a fixed number of cells is treated in §11, Theorem 5.

**2. Definitions**

**Self-moving and awaiting chains.** If at each step a chain’s next move is fixed by the present unit and the mark under reading alone, I call it self-moving. Some engines of old needed, at certain branchings, a command from the keeper of the engine before they could tell which unit to pass into; this was called awaiting command, and such chains I call awaiting chains. Only self-moving chains are considered here, and “chain” will mean a self-moving chain.

**Figure-writing chains.** Some chains write two kinds of marks upon the band: figures, which are 0 and 1 only, and scratch marks, which the chain uses for its own working. Figures are written in the odd cells and, once written, are never altered; scratch marks are written in the even cells and may be written and erased at will. Figures must be written in the odd cells in order from the first, from left to right, none being passed over; a chain that passes one over also fails to keep the rules of figure-writing.

**Inexhaustible and exhaustible chains.** A figure-writing chain started on a blank band is inexhaustible if it writes infinitely many figures; if it writes only finitely many, whether it stops or not, it is exhaustible. A chain that does not keep the rules of figure-writing — that alters or erases a figure once written, for instance — I count as exhaustible, so that “inexhaustible” is defined for every chain number.

**Reckonable sequences and numbers.** The figures written by an inexhaustible chain, taken in order, form an infinite sequence of 0s and 1s, which I call the sequence reckoned by the chain. Any sequence reckoned by some inexhaustible chain is a reckonable sequence. If “0.” is set before a reckonable sequence and the whole is read as a binary fraction, the number obtained is a reckonable number; an integer added to a reckonable number is also called a reckonable number.

**Reckonable functions.** A function $f$ is reckonable if there is a chain which, given the written form of any integer $n$, always comes to a stop with the written form of $f(n)$ on its band.

**Decidable matters.** A class of matters is decidable if each matter of the class can be written as a string of marks, and there is a chain which, given the written form of any one of them, always stops, writing “yes” if the matter holds and “no” if it does not.

**3. Examples of chains**

Of the two chains below one is inexhaustible and the other comes to a stop. Each is written as a table, every row of which gives a present unit and a mark under reading, the action then taken, and the next unit; “blank” means that the cell holds no mark.

**Example 1.** A chain writing 0 1 0 1 …. It has four units and starts in $a$. Figures are written in the odd cells; the even cells are left blank, since this chain needs no scratch marks.

Table 1. Example 1.

| Present unit | Mark read | Action | Next unit |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $a$ | blank | write 0, move right | $b$ |
| $b$ | blank | move right | $c$ |
| $c$ | blank | write 1, move right | $d$ |
| $d$ | blank | move right | $a$ |

Fig. 1. The four units of Example 1. Each arrow is labelled with the mark read and the action taken.

This chain never stops and writes infinitely many figures; it is inexhaustible. The sequence it reckons is 0 1 0 1 …, and the number it reckons, read as a binary fraction, is $0.0101 dots = 1 slash 3$. A star-reckoning array unfolding a star-table by a fixed rule does work of just this kind.

**Example 2.** Addition in single strokes. The band bears a string of strokes, a plus sign and a second string of strokes, for instance “111+11”, and the chain begins at the leftmost cell. It turns the plus sign into a stroke, runs to the right-hand end, erases the last stroke, and stops; the number of strokes left is the sum of the two numbers.

Table 2. Example 2.

| Present unit | Mark read | Action | Next unit |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $a$ | 1 | move right | $a$ |
| $a$ | + | write 1, move right | $b$ |
| $b$ | 1 | move right | $b$ |
| $b$ | blank | move left | $c$ |
| $c$ | 1 | erase | stop |

When the second number is nought, as in “111+”, unit $b$ reads a blank at once, moves left on to the stroke that was the plus sign, and erases it, which is again right. Cases not listed, such as unit $c$ reading a blank, never arise with a proper input; for completeness they are all to be understood as passing into the stop.

**4. Sub-chains**

When a chain grows long its table becomes hard to read. Steps much used may be written as sub-chains with parameters, like functions in a formula: when the parameters are given, a definite table results. Four are used below. They all work within a bounded stretch of the band: a chain that uses them first writes a mark of beginning in the first cell, and an end mark after the last cell it has written, moving the end mark to the right whenever the band is extended.

⟨Seek $a$, $b$, $m$⟩. From the cell under reading go left to the mark of beginning, then right, cell by cell, to the first cell marked $m$; if one is found, stop there and pass into $a$; if the end mark is reached without finding one, pass into $b$.

⟨Erase $a$, $b$, $m$⟩. Find, as with ⟨Seek⟩, the first $m$, erase it and pass into $a$; if there is none, pass into $b$.

⟨Copy $a$, $b$, $m$, $n$⟩. Find the stretch beside which $m$ is marked, and copy its marks cell by cell into the blank stretch marked $n$, keeping the place with a further scratch mark that is erased when the copying is done; then pass into $a$, leaving $m$ where it was. If there is none, pass into $b$. A stretch of marks ends at a mark of division.

⟨Compare $a$, $b$, $c$, $m$, $n$⟩. Compare the two stretches of marks beside which $m$ and $n$ are marked; if they agree pass into $a$, if not into $b$, and if either $m$ or $n$ is not found, into $c$. Each stretch ends at a mark of division.

Each sub-chain uses only finitely many units and, when expanded, is still a finite table. To use a sub-chain within a chain is to copy its table in, putting actual units and marks for the parameters. The universal chain of §7 is written with them.

**5. Chain numbers**

The table of a chain can be put into a standard form. Let the units be called $u_1, u_2, u_3, dots$, the start being $u_1$ and the stop being marked separately; let the marks be called $m_0, m_1, m_2, dots$, $m_0$ being the blank, $m_1$ the figure 0 and $m_2$ the figure 1, each mark keeping the same number throughout, so that if a chain does not use some mark no other mark moves up to take its number; and let each row of the table be written as five items — present unit, mark read, mark written, move (left, right or none), next unit — the rows following one another with a separating sign between them. Every chain then has a standard form, written with only a few letters.

Write the standard form in the marks of the Great Library’s Code Table [7]. Every mark in the Code Table has a code of the same number of figures, short codes being filled out with noughts in front; write the codes of the marks one after another and put a 1 in front of the whole. The number obtained I call the chain number of the chain. Because the codes are all of one length, a chain number can be cut back, piece by piece, into the marks it came from, so that different standard forms have different chain numbers. A pair of numbers $(c, x)$, a pairing sign being set between the two, is made into a single number in the same way, to serve as an input. Not every number is a chain number, but whether a number is one can be tested by a chain; a number which is not a chain number shall be taken to stand for a chain that stops at once. All chains can therefore be arranged in a single list in the order of their chain numbers: chain 1, chain 2, chain 3, ….

It follows that the reckonable sequences can be numbered off as well, since every reckonable sequence is reckoned by at least one inexhaustible chain and the chains can be numbered off. The real numbers cannot be so numbered, as classical arithmetic proved long ago. There are therefore numbers which are not reckonable, and in §8 I shall exhibit a definite one by the same diagonal method.

**6. The universal chain**

LEMMA 1. There is a chain, ⟨Universal⟩, such that for any chain number $c$ and any input $x$: if chain $c$ stops on $x$, ⟨Universal⟩ stops on $(c, x)$ and writes the output of chain $c$ (the marks of chain $c$ being written in their standard form of § 5, and the figures 0 and 1 as they are); if chain $c$ never stops on $x$, neither does ⟨Universal⟩ on $(c, x)$.

⟨Universal⟩ need not be made afresh for each chain: it reads the chain number and follows, step by step, the table that the number spells out. This is just what Ashby’s transcription tables do. A transcription table realises every universal unit upon one engine type; ⟨Universal⟩ takes the rune chain itself for its engine type and realises every rune chain. An engine type which can carry out ⟨Universal⟩ can carry out every rune chain, given enough cells and time. The details of the construction follow.

**7. Details of the universal chain**

⟨Universal⟩ divides its band into three regions (Fig. 2). On the left is the standard form of chain $c$, spelled out from its chain number; this is the text region. Next comes the marker region, which records the unit chain $c$ is in. On the right is the simulation region, which holds chain $c$’s own band. The table of marks of ⟨Universal⟩ is fixed and finite; the units and marks of chain $c$, in whatever region, are written in the standard form of § 5. In the simulation region every mark of chain $c$ is filled out to one length, that of the longest mark of chain $c$, with noughts in front; so that one cell of chain $c$ is, on the band of ⟨Universal⟩, a stretch of fixed length, which I call a simulated cell. The simulated cell chain $c$ is reading has the scratch mark $R$ beside its first cell on the right. $R$, like the mark $L$ below, is a scratch mark and is written in an even cell. The regions are parted by marks of division, and the marks of beginning and end are as in § 4. ⟨Universal⟩ keeps the arrangement of figure-writing chains: the codes of all three regions are written in the odd cells, and the even cells hold only scratch marks such as $R$ and $L$, so that $R$, set beside the first cell of a simulated cell, covers no code. The units and marks of chain $c$ in the text and marker regions are filled out to the same length as in the simulation region, so that the comparison of step (ii) can be made.

Fig. 2. The band of ⟨Universal⟩: the text region on the left, the marker region in the middle, the simulation region on the right. The simulation region is extended only to the right.

At the outset ⟨Universal⟩ tests whether the $c$ of its input is a chain number, stopping at once if it is not; if it is, it spells $c$ out into its standard form as the text region, finds the length of the longest mark of chain $c$, writes the start $u_1$ in the marker region, writes $x$ into the simulation region in simulated cells, and marks the first of them with $R$.

⟨Universal⟩ does one thing over and over, each round in four steps.

(i) By ⟨Copy⟩, copy the mark in the simulated cell marked $R$, cell by cell, into the marker region, after the present unit, so as to form the pair “present unit, mark read”; $R$ stays where it was.

(ii) By ⟨Compare⟩, compare this pair with the first two items of each row of the text region in turn, and mark with $L$ the row that agrees. The text is a complete table, so such a row is always found; should none be found, the convention of §3 applies and the stop is entered.

(iii) Following the last three items of the row marked $L$, write the new mark in the simulated cell marked $R$, and move $R$ to the simulated cell on the left or on the right; if the move is none, $R$ does not move, nor does it when the move is to the left from the leftmost simulated cell. When $R$ is moved beyond the rightmost simulated cell, a blank simulated cell is added at the right, and the end mark moves with it.

(iv) Rewrite the marker region as the next unit of that row, and erase $L$. If the next unit is the stop, first erase the text and marker regions, strip the simulated cells of the noughts that filled them out, move the contents of the simulation region, in the standard form of § 5, to the beginning of the band, and then stop.

For each step of chain $c$, ⟨Universal⟩ performs one round of these four steps, each round being finished in finitely many steps; and when it is finished the simulation region still holds the band of chain $c$, $R$ still marks the simulated cell chain $c$ is reading, and the marker region still records the unit chain $c$ is in. If chain $c$ stops on $x$, therefore, ⟨Universal⟩ stops and writes the output of chain $c$; and if chain $c$ never stops, neither does ⟨Universal⟩. This proves Lemma 1.

**8. Application of the diagonal method**

THEOREM 1. There is no chain which, given any chain number, will decide whether that chain is inexhaustible.

Proof. Suppose there were such a chain, ⟨Discern⟩, which given any chain number always stops, writing “yes” if the chain is inexhaustible and “no” otherwise. With it construct a figure-writing chain ⟨Diagonal⟩ which works in rounds, the $n$-th round being as follows. By ⟨Discern⟩, test the chains in order from chain 1 until the $n$-th inexhaustible chain is found; by ⟨Universal⟩, run that chain step by step, counting after each step the figures it has written, and break off when it has written $n$; and write on the band the opposite figure, 1 if it was 0 and 0 if it was 1. The workings of ⟨Discern⟩ and ⟨Universal⟩ are carried on by ⟨Diagonal⟩ in answering scratch marks in the even cells; in the odd cells it writes only the figure reversed in each round, which is never altered.

Every round is completed in finitely many steps, for ⟨Discern⟩ always stops, and the $n$-th inexhaustible chain, being inexhaustible, always writes its $n$-th figure. ⟨Diagonal⟩ therefore writes infinitely many figures and is itself inexhaustible, so that it stands somewhere in the list of all inexhaustible chains, say as the $k$-th. But then its $k$-th figure is, by its construction, the opposite of the $k$-th figure written by the $k$-th inexhaustible chain — that is, by itself — which is absurd. Hence there is no ⟨Discern⟩.

The sequence obtained by reversing, for each $n$, the $n$-th figure of the $n$-th reckonable sequence differs from every reckonable sequence in at least one place, and is therefore not reckonable. With “0.” set before it, it gives the definite number, not reckonable, promised in §5. By the $n$-th reckonable sequence I mean that reckoned by the $n$-th inexhaustible chain in the order of chain numbers; if one sequence is reckoned by several chains it appears several times, which does no harm. Only a terminating binary fraction has two forms, the second ending in an endless run of 1s; both forms are in the end periodic, and both are reckonable sequences. The sequence obtained above, not being reckonable, is neither form of any terminating fraction; the number it gives therefore has one form only, and is not reckonable.

THEOREM 2. There is no total judge. By a total judge I mean a chain ⟨Judge⟩ which, for every pair $(c, x)$, stops, writing “stops” if chain $c$ stops on $x$ and “never stops” if it does not.

Proof. Suppose ⟨Judge⟩ exists. Construct a chain ⟨Contrary⟩ which, given a number $c$, (i) copies $c$, with the pairing sign between, so as to have $(c, c)$; (ii) concatenates ⟨Judge⟩ and obtains its output on $(c, c)$, which by hypothesis it surely does; (iii) branches on that output, entering a loop it never leaves on “stops”, and passing into the stop on “never stops”. ⟨Contrary⟩ is made from ⟨Judge⟩ by the three rules of combination and is therefore a chain; let its chain number be $r$.

Consider ⟨Contrary⟩ on the input $r$. If it stops on $r$, the output seen at step (iii) was “never stops”: ⟨Judge⟩ has declared that chain $r$ never stops on $r$, and chain $r$ is ⟨Contrary⟩, which is a contradiction. If it never stops on $r$, the output seen at step (iii) was “stops”: ⟨Judge⟩ has declared that ⟨Contrary⟩ stops on $r$, which is again a contradiction. Hence ⟨Judge⟩ does not exist.

The astrologers below are often confounded by a single remark: “You say I shall go out tomorrow; then I shall stay at home.” Whatever the astrologer foretells, a hearer who does the opposite will make the prophecy fail. ⟨Contrary⟩ is just such a hearer, who listens to the verdict and does the reverse.

Theorem 2 does not say that whether a chain stops can never be known. If a chain will stop on some input, one has only to let it run, and sooner or later one sees it stop: stopping is something that can be waited for. What cannot be waited for is the other side. If a chain never stops, no length of running will show that it will not stop later; and the whole difficulty of a total judge lies on that side. Theorems 1 and 2 are two faces of one obstacle.

THEOREM 3. There is no chain which, given any chain number, will decide whether that chain, started on a blank band, will ever write a given mark, say “0”.

Proof. For any pair $(c, x)$ construct a chain $B$ which, starting on a blank band, first writes $x$ and then carries out the working of chain $c$ on $x$. The marks $B$ uses are kept wholly apart from “0”: $x$ and every mark of chain $c$ are written by $B$ in scratch marks of their own, and where chain $c$ reads or writes a mark $B$ reads or writes the answering scratch mark, so that in the course of the working $B$ never writes “0”; only if that working stops does $B$ write “0”. The table of $B$ is obtained from $(c, x)$ by rule. Then $B$ will at some time write “0” if and only if chain $c$ stops on $x$. A chain deciding whether “0” is ever written would thus give a total judge, contrary to Theorem 2.

**9. The extent of what can be done by rule**

I now ask whether everything that can be calculated by rule can be calculated by a rune chain. This cannot be proved, since “by rule” is not a notion of arithmetic; I can only give reasons. They are three: a direct look at what a person does who calculates by rule; a comparison with other exact definitions; and the facts of practice.

**(I) The direct argument.** Of old the Tower of All Things reckoned its star-tables by the Ring Rite. The presiding Grand Master stood at the station of the chief star, and some scores of wizards at the star stations of the array; each did one fixed step only, taking the mana and the token passed to him by the one before, changing them as prescribed, and passing them on. A reckoning might last for months, yet no wizard needed to understand the whole of it; he needed only to understand his own step.

So it is with a reckoner working alone by rule. Let us consider him point by point.

(a) He uses only finitely many kinds of mark. A mark is written within a cell and is of bounded size, and his eye can distinguish only so finely; were there infinitely many kinds, two of them within these bounds would differ by as little as one pleased, and he could not tell them apart. The marks he can use are therefore finite in number; longer writings may be regarded as strings of these marks.

(b) At any moment he looks at only finitely many cells. However much he can take in at a glance, there is a limit, and beyond it he must look more than once.

(c) The states of his mind relevant to the work are finite in number. Since the rules are fixed in writing, what he keeps in mind is only which step of the rules he has reached, and of such steps there are finitely many. Whatever must be remembered that grows as the work goes on — how far he has counted, where he has got to — is written on the paper, not kept in mind (see (e)).

(d) At each step he alters only finitely many cells, moves his eyes only a little way, and passes to the next step of the rules. Any larger action may be broken into several such steps.

(e) The state of his mind may itself be written down. When a reckoner of the Tower goes off duty he leaves a note saying which step he has reached, and the one who comes on carries on from the note, with no need to know what passed through the other’s mind. The state of mind can thus be exchanged for marks on paper: a reckoning may be carried on by many people in turn, each working only from the note and the rules.

Taken together, these show that each step a reckoner takes by rule is to read a few cells, to alter and move according to the rules, and to pass to the next step; and this is just what a chain of §1 does at each step. Everything that can be done by rule can therefore be written as a rune chain.

**(II) Agreement with other definitions.** The “system whose rules can be listed” of the Babel proof of the Fourth Question [5] is one whose proofs can be listed by a chain: write out strings of marks in order of length, test each to see whether it is a correct derivation by the rules, and output it if it is. This requires that whether a derivation is correct can always be settled in finitely many steps; and the system of the Babel proof is of this kind. Prescott’s recursively definable functions [8] are, as the Appendix shows, the same as the reckonable functions. That definitions starting from such different places should meet is evidence that they have caught the same thing.

**(III) Practice.** Every method of judgment the Alliance has so far used — the cross-checking of star-tables, the reconciling of accounts, Finley’s checker [3] — has been written as a rune chain; and I can think of no judgment by rule which could not be broken into fixed steps like those at the star stations.

Insight is not included in this; see §12.

**10. Large classes of reckonable numbers**

The reckonable numbers include almost every number used in actual calculation. I mention some large classes.

(i) Integers and fractions. The binary expansion of a fraction is in the end periodic, and a chain need only repeat the period.

(ii) Real roots of algebraic equations. Let an equation with integral coefficients first be freed of repeated factors, so that its roots are all simple; the number of real roots between two fractions can be counted by the old method of algebra, and the roots can thus be isolated one by one. A root once isolated is sought on the lattice of binary fractions. Each binary fraction met with is first substituted: if it gives nought, it is the root, and is written as in (i); if not, the root lies between two neighbouring binary fractions of $k$ places, and its first $k$ figures are fixed. The middle point is then substituted, to see in which half the root lies, and each halving fixes one figure more.

(iii) Limits obtained by the calculus of flux, provided the speed of convergence can be reckoned. Let the steps of an approximation be fractions given uniformly in $n$ by a chain, and let it be possible, for any $p$, to reckon a step beyond which every step differs from the limit by no more than $2^(-p)$. Then enclosures of any accuracy can be found, and the figures fixed as at the end of (v). The value of Crossley’s smooth counting formula at any integer is of this kind. So is the solution of Aldridge’s tide equations over a stretch of time, provided the initial state and the constants are reckonable, the existence and uniqueness of the solution over that stretch have been proved, and its errors can be bounded by reckoning; whether the solution exists and remains smooth over every finite time is the fourth of the Millennium Seven, which this paper does not touch.

(iv) The zeros of the density function. The density function can be evaluated to any accuracy, with a reckonable bound to the error; and by the counting of zeros round a contour the number of zeros within a small rectangle of the complex plane can be found, and found in finitely many steps whenever no zero lies on its edge; in dividing a rectangle one takes several lines of division very close together near the point of division and reckons them by turns, and since the zeros are isolated, one of these lines has no zero on it and is settled in finitely many steps. Every non-trivial zero can therefore be enclosed in smaller and smaller rectangles, so that its real and imaginary parts are reckonable. Aldin’s conjecture says that all these zeros lie on one line. Were there a zero off the line, it would lie within some rectangle that does not touch the line and has no zero on its edge; a chain may count the zeros in all such rectangles by turns, advancing each a little at a time and waiting long on none, and stop when it finds a count that is not nought. The conjecture fails, then, if and only if this chain stops, as was said in the Introduction. On the other hand, no number of zeros found on the line can prove that all lie there.

(v) The sum, difference, product and quotient (the divisor not being nought) of reckonable numbers are reckonable. In writing out the figures one first calculates to sufficient accuracy before fixing each figure, lest a later carry alter figures already written. But if the result lies exactly upon a point of division, an enclosure however narrow may always straddle the point, and that figure cannot be fixed. What is asserted here, then, is asserted of each number singly: if the result is not a binary fraction, a narrow enough enclosure will always avoid the points of division; if it is, its terminating expansion is written as in (i). Whether the result lies exactly upon a point of division no single chain can decide.

The numbers reckoned by star-reckoning arrays and calculating engines are thus all reckonable, and the limits found here put no hindrance in the way of the calculations of these classes. Section 8 gives one way of describing a number that is not reckonable; such numbers are far more numerous than the reckonable, and need not all be made on purpose.

**11. The answer to Severin’s Fifth Question, with consequences**

The Fifth Question asks about any formula whatever. A rune chain is itself a formula, being exactly the formula that an engine executes; so a means of knowing the outcome of any formula before it is cast would decide, for every rune chain and every input, whether the chain stops. By §9 such a means, if it works by rule, can be written as a rune chain, and would then be a total judge, which by Theorem 2 does not exist. In the sense here given to the word “means”, the answer to Severin’s Fifth Question is therefore: there is no such means.

Some further consequences follow.

THEOREM 4. Let $P$ be a property of the behaviour of chains; that is, whether a chain has $P$ depends only on the inputs on which it stops and the outputs it then gives, not on the way it is written. If some chains have $P$ and some do not, there is no chain which decides, for every chain, whether it has $P$.

Proof. Suppose there were such a chain ⟨Judge-P⟩. The chain that never stops on any input either has $P$ or has not; suppose it has not (otherwise use “not $P$” in place of $P$), and take a chain $A$ that has $P$. For any pair $(c, x)$, construct a chain $B$ which, given $y$, first stores $y$ in scratch marks of its own at the beginning of the band and carries out the working of chain $c$ on $x$ to the right of it; and, if that stops, erases all that chain $c$ has written, restores $y$, and then does what $A$ does on $y$. If chain $c$ stops on $x$, $B$ behaves exactly as $A$ and has $P$; if not, $B$ never stops on any input and has not $P$. Applying ⟨Judge-P⟩ to $B$ would thus decide whether chain $c$ stops on $x$, contrary to Theorem 2.

“Stops on every input”, “on some input outputs the command to open a valve”, and “behaves exactly as a certain chain already passed by the inspectors” are all properties of this kind. Actions taken in the course of the working — whether a command to open a valve, or to drain the mana core attached, is ever issued along the way — are not properties of behaviour in the sense of Theorem 4, yet they too admit no total judge: make a chain which first carries out the working of chain $c$ on $x$, issuing neither command meanwhile, and only after it stops issues one; to judge whether it ever does so is to judge whether chain $c$ stops on $x$. What is spoken of here is whether the chain issues the command; whether the valve in fact opens, or the core is in fact drained, depends on the engine and its supply, and lies outside the model of this paper. There is, therefore, no program which, for every formula whatever that may be sent to it, answers rightly “passed” or “not passed”. An inspection may answer “not proved”; it may require a formula to carry its own proof; it may confine formulae to a class that can be completely judged; or it may make honest trial. Where a proof is carried, the proof assures the conclusion; where the class is confined, the judgment assures it; where trial is made, only the cases tried are assured.

THEOREM 5. If a chain may use only a fixed number $n$ of cells — its input being written within the first $n$, and a move to the right from the $n$-th cell leaving it where it is — then whether it stops on a given input can be decided.

Proof. The situation of such a chain is fixed by three things — the contents of the band, the present unit, and the cell under reading — each of which can be in only finitely many states: if the chain has $q$ units besides the stop and the table has $k$ marks, there are at most $N = q n k^n$ situations short of the stop. Let the chain run $N$ steps, passing through $N + 1$ moments counting the first. If it has not stopped, some situation has occurred twice, and from then on it can only repeat the same course for ever.

$N$ grows very fast with $n$: if the table of marks has $k$ marks, the contents of the band alone can be in $k^n$ states. “Decidable in principle” is therefore not “decidable in practice”. In practice three classes are commonly met with that can be completely judged: chains without loops, which always stop; chains in which the number of passes round each loop is bounded beforehand by an amount reckonable from the length of the input, which also always stop; and chains with a fixed number of cells, which can be judged by Theorem 5. Most control formulae of the workshops can be written in the first two classes. Finley’s method works because it uses the structure of control formulae rather than enumerating situations one by one. The sixth of the Millennium Seven [6] asks whether problems whose answers can be quickly checked once given can also have their answers quickly found. That is a question of speed; this paper is concerned only with possibility, and does not touch it.

THEOREM 6. Let there be a system all of whose proofs can be listed one by one by a chain, in which propositions of the form “chain $c$ never stops on $x$” can be recognised mechanically, and all of whose proved propositions of this form are true. Then there is a pair $(c, x)$ such that chain $c$ in fact never stops on $x$, but the system cannot prove it.

Proof. If not, then for every pair $(c, x)$ for which chain $c$ never stops, the system proves “chain $c$ never stops on $x$”. Any pair could then be decided thus: run chain $c$ on $x$, and at the same time list the proofs of the system one by one; if the former stops, answer “stops”; if a proof of “chain $c$ never stops on $x$” appears, answer “never stops”. One of the two must happen after finitely many steps, and since what the system proves is true, the answer is right. This would be a total judge, contrary to Theorem 2.

The Babel fragment released in F.E. 290 [5] proves that a system which does not contradict itself, can express the arithmetic of the natural numbers, and has rules that can be listed, cannot prove by its own means that it does not contradict itself. The fragment starts from within the system, this paper from the one who judges, and the two roads lead to neighbouring places. It should be said that Theorem 6 requires the system’s assertions that a chain never stops to be sound, while the fragment requires the system to be free of contradiction and able to express arithmetic; the two sets of conditions are different, and this paper does not derive the fragment’s result.

**12. On insight**

It may be said that the seers of the White Tower sometimes see at a glance how a formula will end, and not by rule. On this the theorems have nothing to say, for they concern judgment by rule. Two things may nevertheless be said.

First, by the rule laid down by the White Tower Society about C.R. 1090, that insight must be reckoned, whatever is gained by insight becomes learning only when it is written in formulae and checked. Checking is of two kinds: the examining of a written proof, which is a matter of rule, and the checking by trial of casting, which can only go case by case. What the theorems of this paper govern, then, is the examination that follows reckoning, not insight itself; how insight arises I do not discuss.

Secondly, if there were a seer who could see, for every rune chain, whether it stops, and were never wrong, his gift, taken as a uniform method, could not be written as rules, for written as rules it would be a total judge. His conclusions might still be proved or checked one by one, so far as proof or trial could reach; but by Theorem 6 his judgments that a chain never stops could not all be proved in one sound system, and the gift itself, that of seeing it for every chain, could by the rule never be reckoned. Whether there is any such seer is not a question that arithmetic can answer.

**13. Conclusion**

The Fifth Question was put one thousand and eleven years ago, and its answer is negative. This does not hinder the craftsmen in their work. It only shows where their labour is best spent: in confining the formulae to be judged to classes that can be decided, in writing a proof for each formula, or in honest trial.

Earlier drafts of this paper were read by colleagues at White Tower University and the University of Kanaich. The construction of Theorem 2 came from a conversation with an old rune-cutter of the Guild of Rune Mages, who told me that the apprentices’ oldest trick for teasing an inspector is to hide in a formula a passage which first reads the mark the inspector has stamped, and then does the opposite.

**Appendix. Reckonable and recursively definable functions**

The recursively definable functions of Prescott [8] are those obtained from three initial functions — the function that is always nought, the function that adds one, and the function that picks out the $i$-th argument — by three operations: composition, recursion and least search. Least search means this: given a function $g$, for each set of arguments try $y = 0, 1, 2, dots$ in turn: if $g$ is defined at $y$ and is nought, take this $y$; if it is defined and not nought, try the next; if a $y$ is met at which $g$ is undefined, or if none is ever found, the function is undefined for those arguments. The recursion meant is primitive recursion, the arguments are natural numbers, and signed integers are coded as natural numbers. I show that a function is recursively definable if and only if it is reckonable, “reckonable” being here widened to include functions undefined at some arguments, at which the chain does not stop. Restricted to functions defined everywhere, the two definitions again describe the same class.

(i) Every recursively definable function is reckonable. The proof is by induction on the number of steps in the definition. The three initial functions are evidently reckonable. If $g$ and $h$ are reckonable (each chain first copying the arguments and the step count to a safe place on the band, lest its working erase them), so is their composition, by concatenating the two chains; so is a function defined from them by recursion, by a loop that works up from nought to the place required; and so is least search on $g$, by a loop that tries one value after another, stopping when $g$ is nought and going on for ever if it never is, which answers exactly to being undefined.

(ii) Every reckonable function is recursively definable. Code the situation of a chain at a given moment — the contents of the band, the present unit and the cell under reading — as a number, in the manner of §5. Division, remainder, the taking out of a stretch of figures, the joining of two stretches and the looking up of an entry in a finite table are all recursively definable, as Prescott [8] has shown; looking up the table, rewriting one cell and moving one cell are compounded of them, so that the transformation by which one step of the chain carries one situation into the next is recursively definable. If a situation already in the stop is taken to pass by one step into itself, the transformation is defined for every situation. The situation after $t$ steps from the input is defined by recursion on $t$; the number of steps needed to stop is found by least search, as the first $t$ at which the chain is in the stop; and the output is extracted from the situation at stopping.

The two definitions therefore describe the same functions, and the second reason of §9 receives an exact illustration.

## On the Least Share in the Exchange of Mana

**ON THE LEAST SHARE IN THE EXCHANGE OF MANA**

By SELBY, of the University of Kanaich, and ORMES, of Tumen University, Stormthrone; the order of the names was decided by the toss of a coin.

[Received in the Month of Spring Dawn, F.E. 338, the Year of Fine Rain.]

**Summary.** Classical nomological algebra treats the exchange of mana between the Web of Laws and the depths of Tiamat as a quantity divisible without end. From more than four centuries of comparison registers we recompute the “order residual”, and find that within the precision of the registers it does not vary with instrument, environment or epoch. Writing the exchange as two operations, raising and lowering, and supposing the difference between their two orders of application to be a constant greater than nought, we prove under some general rules of the algebra that a definite amount of exchange can only be a whole multiple of a least share; we compute its value from the registers and, following the casters’ old usage, call it a wisp. Three predictions are offered for testing by others with different instruments.

**§ 1. Introduction**

Severin’s Eighth Question [1] reads: “Is mana continuous?” In F.E. 307 the Millennium Foundation set it as the fifth of the Millennium Seven [7], asking that a least, indivisible share in the exchange of mana be proved, its value computed, and the result confirmed by independent measurement.

Casters have always said that mana comes “wisp by wisp”. We borrow the name, but do not take the intuition as evidence. Besides intuition, three approaches have been tried before, without result. The first was to ask casters skilled in fine control to divide mana again and again and to see where it would divide no further; but whether the limit lay in the mana or in the caster’s hand, there was no telling. The second was to consult the station mana-meters, whose readings have been smooth for centuries since Fanshawe drew up the rules for stations about C.R. 950; but the finest division of these meters is far coarser than the share in question, and smoothness proves nothing of continuity. The third was to argue from classical nomological algebra; but there the amount exchanged is written as a continuous quantity from the start, so that continuity is not concluded but assumed.

Classical nomological algebra, since Elworthy [3], writes the exchange between the Web of Laws and the depths of Tiamat as a continuous quantity. Successive application in that algebra mostly depends on order; yet the two operations of exchange, raising and lowering, have always been treated as a pair that may be interchanged, raising then lowering giving the same as lowering then raising. Classical learning, treating mana as continuous, computed wards and tides in agreement with observation, and continuity came to be taken for granted.

Our point of departure is a very small number. When the Seventh Division compares its standard crystals, receiving before releasing gives a slightly different result from releasing before receiving; the difference is entered in the registers as the “order residual”, and has always been taken for instrumental error. In Part I (§§ 2–5) we show that it is not instrumental error: within what the registers can distinguish, it does not vary with instrument, environment or epoch. In Part II (§§ 6–10) we deduce from it that the exchange is not continuous, compute the least share, and show why the classical theory nevertheless holds. In Part III (§§ 11–13) we propose tests, and discuss the bearing of the result upon the standards of measure and the questions it leaves open.

**PART I. THE REGISTERS**

**§ 2. The procedure of comparison**

Under the classical procedure, when a city’s secondary standard is compared with the primary, two sets of standard crystals [2] are placed side by side on the comparing bench and joined through a single conducting crystal to the Web of Laws. The comparison proceeds by small shares received and released alternately: at one opening and closing of the conducting crystal each side, primary and secondary, receives a share of mana, and at the next each releases one; or the reverse, releasing first and receiving after. One receiving and one releasing make an alternation; after an alternation both sides return to their former charge. Alternations are of two orders, receiving before releasing and releasing before receiving; the procedure takes them by turns, half of each, so as to cancel the drift of the crystals during the comparison, and one alternation of each order makes a pair. A comparison runs to some thousands of alternations. The bench has two counting dials: the difference between the two sides in the alternations receiving first runs on to one, and in those releasing first on to the other, neither being cleared during the comparison; at its end each is read once and entered in the register as a matter of routine. The procedure regards the difference between the two orders as due to drift and dissipation, averages it away at the end, and takes only the averaged result as the calibration of the secondary.

The order residual has thus stood in the registers for centuries without anyone treating it as a quantity. In a single comparison it amounts to no more than a fraction of the smallest division, and to those making the comparison it was not to be distinguished from an error of reading.

The registers of the Seventh Division were for the most part burned in the Great Tide, and those that survive begin about C.R. 1450; the registers of secondary standards kept by seven cities begin and end at various dates. We have recomputed the whole of these four centuries of records. In recomputing we have used only the original readings, never the results calculated at the time; from each comparison the supply, the exchange with the surroundings and the dissipation have been deducted, the dissipation being calculated by the general account established since Finch [2]. The records of the First Era have all been reduced, by the numbers and calibration dates of the standards entered in the registers, to the master standard fixed by the Seventh Division. We found in all some 21,900 comparisons; the 2,100 and more that cannot be so reduced, about one in ten, are not used for the value, but only to see the form of the residual, which is the same as in the rest. They lie mostly in the first century of the First Era and in the cities of Tallin and Onais. The comparisons admitted number 19,762: 612 of the Seventh Division before the Great Tide, used for the comparison across the Tide in § 5, and 19,150 of the seven cities in the First Era, used for the value of the wisp.

The recomputation followed the old rule of great reckonings, that two places reckon apart. The University of Kanaich and Tumen University each, with their own hands and without communication, copied from the original registers, reduced and combined; only results that agreed group by group between the two were admitted. The three hundred and more comparisons on which they disagreed were copied afresh from the registers, and those that still disagreed were not used; they are counted within the one in ten above.

**§ 3. The order residual**

The order residual of a comparison is the sum of what is recorded for all its alternations receiving before releasing, less the sum for all those releasing before receiving, and is reckoned positive when the former is the greater; the number of reversals is the number of pairs into which the two orders are joined in the comparison. The recomputation shows three things.

(i) The residual always lies to one side, and is not positive and negative at random: of more than nineteen thousand comparisons fewer than twenty give a negative residual, all of them comparisons of very few pairs, whose residual is no greater than the error of estimating the reading.

(ii) The residual is proportional to the number of reversals of order in a comparison: each reversal adds the same amount (Fig. 1). The duration of a comparison, the number of its alternations and the total passed through the conducting crystal all increase with the number of pairs; but the dissipation and drift they bring are the same for both orders, and cancel in the residual. The two procedures of the First Era differ threefold in the duration of an alternation, yet the amount added by one reversal is the same in both (Table 2, § 4), so that the residual does not depend on duration.

(iii) The amount added by one reversal does not vary with the city, with tide years or ordinary years, or with the manner of coupling (Table 1); grouped by the three materials of the standard crystals, the three groups lie between 3.46 and 3.48, each with an error of about 0.02, and show no difference either.

Several further possibilities must be excluded one by one (Table 2).

First, an asymmetry in the procedure itself. Were the two orders carried out differently in duration or in manner, the residual might arise from the procedure. The procedure of comparison has been written in four forms in the classical age and the First Era, differing in the duration of an alternation, the number of pairs in a comparison and the manner of alternation; recomputed form by form, the two procedures of the First Era give the same amount for one reversal, and the two classical ones a slightly higher, for which see the note below the table.

Secondly, the habits of hands. Comparisons are mostly conducted by officers sent by the Seventh Division, and a habit common to them might produce the residual. Recomputed by the officer in charge, the three hundred and more officers show no difference among themselves; Table 2 gives only three groups by length of service.

Thirdly, the charge of the crystals at the time. If the residual depended on how full the crystals were, it would not be a property of the exchange itself. The charge at a comparison ranges from nearly full to nearly empty; recomputed in four classes by the charge at the start of the comparison, the amount added by one reversal is again the same.

Fourthly, an asymmetry between the two sides of the bench. In about a quarter of the comparisons the primary stood on the right of the bench, and in the rest on the left; recomputed by the two placings, the amount added by one reversal is the same.

| Charge below one quarter | 4,220 | 3.46 ± 0.03 |

The two classical procedures give slightly higher values because they were used only before the Great Tide and are reckoned in the old units of that time; see the next section. Procedure and epoch cannot therefore be separated. The classes by service and by charge contain the records of the First Era only, 19,150 in all.

So far as these groupings can distinguish, then, the residual shows no variation with instrumental error, dissipation, procedure, hands, charge or placing; a bias common to all the groups, such as the one master standard or the one kind of bench, cannot be seen by grouping. We interpret the residual as a property of the exchange itself; this interpretation still awaits the test of other colleges with other instruments (§ 11). It is consistent also with the records of the plane-gate of the White Tower, kept without interruption since about C.R. 1260: within the reach and precision of the gate, the course of the laws has scarcely varied in centuries; but the gate sees only the working of the web, and cannot by itself show that the exchange in the depths is unchanged.

**§ 5. Before and after the Great Tide**

Before and after the Great Tide the case is a little different (Fig. 2, Table 3). Read in the old units, the residual after the Great Tide is about one part in a hundred smaller than before. If the residual is a constant of the exchange, this change can only come from the unit itself; this is a conditional, and cannot be turned round to prove the residual a constant. Taunton showed about F.E. 20 [5] that the standards disturbed by the tide had suffered a lasting change in capacity and response: after the Great Tide the old unit fixed by the master standard was larger than before, the ratio of the unit after the tide to the unit before being 1.011 ± 0.002. He measured it against reference crystals kept where the tide had touched them least, independently of the registers used here. By this ratio the 3.51 before the tide becomes 3.47 in the unit after it, which agrees with the 3.47 after; and the ratio of Table 3, 1.012 ± 0.009, agrees with Taunton’s within the errors. The simplest explanation is that it was the standard that changed, not the residual. Taunton urged in his day that the unit of mana be defined by a quantity that does not change with the instrument, but no such quantity was then known; the order residual is such a quantity.

**PART II. THEORY**

**§ 6. Exchange in nomological algebra**

Nomological algebra calls the condition of the web at a place a web-state, and operations act upon web-states. We write $X Y$ for the operation of applying $Y$ first and $X$ after. In nomological algebra joint application answers to addition and successive application to multiplication; successive application is associative and distributes over joint application; a number and an operation may be multiplied in either order; and the identity operation changes nothing. These are general rules of the algebra since Elworthy [3], holding among exchanges that are compatible with one another, and the raising, lowering and amount of this paper lie within that range. They are all we shall need, together with the rules of the null at the end of this section.

Nomological algebra writes the exchange by two operations: raising, $U$, which raises one share of mana from the depths of Tiamat into the Web of Laws; and lowering, $D$, which lowers one share from the web back into the depths. When a crystal receives or releases mana in comparison, the exchange always passes between the Web of Laws and the depths of Tiamat: in receiving, mana from the depths is raised into the web; in releasing, mana in the web is lowered into the depths. Receiving before releasing is therefore raising before lowering, written $D U$; releasing before receiving is lowering before raising, written $U D$.

We take further a general rule of nomological algebra since Elworthy [3]: $U$ and $D$ are dual to each other, and an operation and its dual applied in succession, in either order, yield an amount that is never negative.

The mana a web-state has drawn from the depths and not yet returned we call the amount of exchange of that web-state, or simply its amount, $Q$. In the usual way of writing amounts of exchange in nomological algebra, it is lowering followed by raising:

$$ Q = U D $$ (1)

Two things must also be kept apart. A web-state whose amount is nought is still a web-state; an operation which yields nothing at all we say yields the null. The null is not a web-state, and is not the same as a web-state of amount nought. Any operation applied to the null yields the null; the null applied jointly with any web-state yields that web-state; and a number multiplied into a web-state yields the null only if the number is nought. Raising or lowering a web-state yields, if not the null, again a web-state; and a joint application acts upon a web-state term by term.

That a web-state has the definite amount $q$ means that $Q$ applied to it yields $q$ times the web-state itself. How the readings of the registers answer to these operations we take as follows. One opening and closing of the conducting crystal is, in nomological algebra, one application: receiving is one application of $U$, releasing one of $D$. The difference between the two sides recorded on the bench for an alternation, apart from the difference between secondary and primary themselves, is by the usage of reading since Elworthy [3] the excess of the pair of operations over the amount: if the pair, applied to a web-state of definite amount $q$, multiplies it by a number exceeding $q$ by so much, so much is recorded. Both sides being reset after each alternation, the two alternations of a pair are made upon the same web-state; the difference between secondary and primary does not depend on the order, and cancels in the difference of the two. The difference recorded within a pair, receiving before releasing less releasing before receiving, is therefore the difference between the numbers by which $D U$ and $U D$ multiply that web-state; and the residual is the sum of these differences over the pairs. That one opening and closing is one application, and this manner of reading, are hypotheses of this paper; the tests of § 11 are tests of them also. On this reading one opening and closing exchanges a single wisp; the “share” of the classical procedure was always far below a division, and no one knew how small it was.

**§ 7. The relation of successive application**

The result of § 3, written as a formula, is

$$ D U - U D = epsilon $$ (2)

where $epsilon$ is the amount added by one reversal, and the right-hand side is the product of $epsilon$ with the identity operation, written simply $epsilon$. What the registers record are readings made under a particular procedure; to extend them to a relation (2) holding for every web-state is an hypothesis of this paper. It is supported by two findings of §§ 3–4: the residual shows no variation with the charge of the crystals at the time, nor with the procedure, the city or the epoch. By § 3 (i) the residual lies always to one side, receiving before releasing recording more than releasing before receiving, so that $epsilon$ is greater than nought. The classical theory, in assuming that the two operations may be interchanged, assumed in effect that $epsilon$ is nought.

**§ 8. The theorem of shares**

From (2) it follows that

$$ Q U = U (Q + epsilon), quad Q D = D (Q - epsilon) $$ (3)

For $Q U = U D U = U (D U) = U (U D + epsilon) = U (Q + epsilon)$; and $D (Q - epsilon) = D U D - epsilon D = (U D + epsilon) D - epsilon D = U D D = Q D$.

The meaning of (3) is this: if a web-state has amount $q$, the web-state obtained by applying $U$ once has amount $q + epsilon$, and that obtained by applying $D$ once has amount $q - epsilon$, unless it is the null.

THEOREM 1. If the amount of a web-state is a definite number, it can only be one of $0, epsilon, 2 epsilon, 3 epsilon, dots$; and if there is any web-state of definite amount, all of these values occur.

Proof. Take a web-state of definite amount $q$, $q$ being finite, and apply $D$ to it repeatedly. By (3), so long as a web-state results, its amounts are $q - epsilon$, $q - 2 epsilon$, …. Since $epsilon$ is greater than nought, and by the rule of duality the amount is never negative, $D$ cannot be applied without end: at some step the result is no longer a web-state but the null. Let the web-state before that step have amount $q_0$. For this web-state $Q = U D$; lowering gives the null, and raising the null gives the null again; so $q_0 = 0$. Hence $q$, less some number of $epsilon$, is exactly nought; that is, $q$ is a whole multiple of $epsilon$. Conversely, apply $U$ once to the web-state of amount nought just reached. The result is not the null: for by (2), raising it and then lowering is $(U D + epsilon)$ applied to it, which gives $epsilon$ times the web-state itself, and is not the null; whereas had raising given the null, lowering would give the null again. By (3) its amount is $epsilon$. In the same way, raising and then lowering a web-state of amount $q$ gives $q + epsilon$ times the web-state, which is not the null, so that $U$ may always be applied again, giving $2 epsilon, 3 epsilon, dots$ in turn.

Fig. 3. The ladder of the theorem of shares (a diagram). Each rung is a definite amount; $U$ raises by one rung and $D$ lowers by one; lowering the web-state of amount nought gives the null.

The ladder of the algebra has no top; how far an actual instrument can climb it is another matter. The theorem concerns only web-states whose amount is a definite number. We assume further that before and after a single exchange the web-state joined to the conducting crystal is of this kind, so that the amount of a single exchange is a whole number of wisps. The mean of many exchanges need not be: if half are nought and half one wisp, the mean is half a wisp. This assumption is put to the test by the counting of jumps in a weak field (§ 11).

Under these hypotheses, therefore, the exchange of mana between the Web of Laws and the depths of Tiamat is not continuous. It proceeds share by share, each share being $epsilon$, and there is no half-share: $epsilon$ is the least share in the exchange of mana. Casters have always said that mana comes wisp by wisp, and following their usage we call $epsilon$ a wisp.

Severin’s Eighth Question can thus be answered: as regards the exchange between the Web of Laws and the depths of Tiamat, mana is not continuous. Of other quantities we say nothing.

**§ 9. The value of the wisp**

By §§ 3 and 7, a wisp is the amount added by one reversal. The calculation is this. The residual of each comparison equals the number of reversals multiplied by $epsilon$, together with the bias left by dissipation and instrumental error; all the records are combined once by the doctrine of errors into a weighted mean, the weights being set by the number of pairs and the error of estimating the reading in each comparison, and the errors of reduction from the old units being included. The relation (2) is exact; the uncertainty of the value arises from the old units and the records of past ages (Table 4). Combining all the comparisons of the seven cities in the First Era, we find

$$ "one wisp" = (3.47 plus.minus 0.01) times 10^(-11) $$

old units of exchange (after the Great Tide, as fixed by the master standard).

Table 4. Sources of error in the value of the wisp (× 10⁻¹¹).

| Source | Error |
| --- | --- |
| Scatter of the readings of single comparisons | 0.005 |
| Reduction of the cities’ secondary standards to the master standard | 0.006 |
| Deduction of dissipation | 0.004 |
| Differences between procedures | 0.003 |
| Total | 0.009 |

The total is the square root of the sum of the squares, and is here taken as 0.01. So to combine them is to treat the four sources as independent; the correlation arising from the one master standard shared by the cities has been put into the second.

The instruments of the comparing procedure have a smallest division of about ten thousand wisps, and the finest weak-field sensing piece now made resolves no better than some three wisps at a reading. This is why a wisp has never been read directly.

How a wisp is to be got from divisions of ten thousand wisps may be shown by a single comparison. A comparison has commonly two or three thousand pairs; with two thousand five hundred, the residual is some two thousand five hundred wisps, a quarter of a division; and those comparing estimate the reading, by custom, to a twentieth of a division, some five hundred wisps. A wisp found from this one comparison has an error of about one fifth. Combined over nineteen thousand comparisons, the error falls to about one part in seven hundred, or 0.005 × 10⁻¹¹, the first entry of Table 4. What no single comparison can show, four centuries of registers show together.

**§ 10. The macroscopic limit**

An exchange on the large scale amounts to thousands of millions of wisps; a difference of one wisp lies far below the error of measurement, $U D$ and $D U$ are scarcely to be told apart, and to treat the exchange as a continuous quantity does no harm. If the amount of a web-state is one old unit, the difference of one reversal is about one part in thirty thousand millions of it. In general, if the amount at a place is $q$, the ratio of the difference of order to the amount is $epsilon \/ q$; when $q$ is many wisps this ratio tends to nought, and (2) returns to the classical form, in which the two operations may be interchanged.

Classical nomological algebra and the tide equations are therefore not overthrown by this paper. The difference of one reversal bears to the amount the ratio $epsilon \/ q$, which may be neglected when the amount is many wisps. How the tide equations as a whole should be amended would require a passage from the exchange wisp by wisp to the continuous equations, which this paper has not made; we can say only that observation on the large scale still agrees with the continuous theory. Only where mana is extremely weak, or gathers and disperses violently within very small regions, may the continuous theory fail; where the limits of its failure lie, we do not venture to predict.

**PART III. PREDICTIONS AND REMARKS**

**§ 11. Predictions that may be tested**

Our deduction used the registers and a general rule of nomological algebra. Whether the problem of the least mana has been solved must be settled by other colleges with other instruments. The three methods of measurement below are all independent of the registers used here.

(i) Counting jumps in a weak field. In a still room far from any place of casting, out of reach of the interference of casting nearby, let the mana from without be reduced to extreme weakness; no room keeps out the tide, and the rise and fall of the tide are to be recorded at the same time by a second sensing piece not joined to the web, and deducted. Let a sensing piece resolving better than half a wisp be joined to the Web of Laws and the exchange be read continuously. After calibration, and when the response of the sensing piece has been allowed for, the readings should jump step by step, each step being one wisp or a whole multiple of one (Fig. 4). With other crystal materials, other manners of coupling and other supplies the height of the steps should not change; the finer the sensing piece, the clearer the steps, but their height should still not change.

Fig. 4. Predicted readings in a weak field (a diagram, not an observation). With a sensing piece resolving half a wisp the readings should jump between whole multiples of a wisp; the broken lines mark those multiples.

(ii) Resonance in a cavity. Students of arrays have long calibrated the resonant frequency of an array cavity by the mana stored in it: with large amounts the shift of frequency is proportional to the amount stored, the proportion depending on the dimensions and material of the cavity. Whether the proportion still holds at the scale of a single wisp we cannot warrant; this method tests the law of the cavity as well, and the proportion should first be calibrated with amounts lowered step by step, to see whether it changes. If it does not, then when mana is let into the cavity very slowly the frequency should shift step by step, each step answering to one wisp or a whole multiple of one, the interval being this proportion multiplied by a wisp.

(iii) Slow discharge of a mana core. A mana core discharged very slowly through a very fine mana engine should yield its energy in steps, each answering to a wisp or a whole multiple of one. The procedure of Northcote [4] gives only the energy a core delivers when drained; between core capacity and total exchange there is as yet no common reckoning. For this method the relation between the two must first be calibrated afresh for one mana engine under conditions set down, the losses within the engine being deducted, before the core capacity answering to one step can be found.

There is also a negative prediction: after calibration, and when the response of the instrument has been allowed for, the net amount of every single exchange should be a whole multiple of a wisp, and there should be no half-wisp. The raw readings of an instrument may show changes smaller than a wisp, by reason of the duration of a reading, the response of the sensing piece or a divided path; these are no counter-instance. Only an event that is certainly an exchange of the kind measured, and is not a whole multiple of a wisp, is a counter-instance. If any method, after calibration and the exclusion of missed and merged steps, should find steps whose height varies with the instrument, or does not agree with a wisp, or should find exchanges certainly smaller than a wisp, the doctrine of this paper cannot stand.

**§ 12. The wisp and the standards**

Taunton urged about F.E. 20 that the unit of mana should be defined by a quantity that does not change with the instrument. Should this paper be confirmed by independent measurement, the unit of total exchange might be defined as a fixed number of wisps, and the concentration of free mana and the capacity of mana cores might both be traced back to it, so that the three standards would at last rest on a common ground. The old records need not be discarded: provided the standard used and the date of its calibration are entered, they can be reduced in the manner of § 2. Whether this should be done we submit to the Seventh Division for preparation, and to the Academic Council of the Silver Society for decision.

**§ 13. Remarks**

First. This paper treats only of the exchange between the Web of Laws and the depths of Tiamat. Whether time, length or any other quantity is continuous we do not consider. Some may wish to explain the boundary of the tenth ring by a counting of wisps; for that we have no evidence.

Secondly. Why the difference of successive application is not nought, we cannot say. Westphal’s doctrine of layers [6] gives the order of the layers, and the doctrine of seven layers supposes that the depths of Tiamat contain layers that strain, sort and divide; the wisp may arise in one of these, but that is conjecture, and this paper has no means of testing it.

Thirdly. The data of this paper are all taken from the registers of those who went before. Without readings entered as a matter of routine for centuries and never thrown away, we could not have begun; those who made the comparisons took this small difference for instrumental error, yet they entered it, item by item.
