# 《联盟学术名篇》英文原刊（第三批）

## The Twenty-Four Questions

**THE TWENTY-FOUR QUESTIONS**

By SEVERIN, of Chaen.

[Read before the First General Assembly of the Silver Society on its last day, in the Year of the Return of Radiance, C.R. 824, the year of the League’s founding.]

Friends and colleagues,

The Assembly rises today. Before it met, the Society charged me to gather into one place the hard questions that have passed these many years in letters between the cities and the schools, to set them in order, and to read them to you before we part. I have spent half a year upon the gathering. I struck out what was repeated, and where several letters asked the same thing I made of them one question; there remain twenty-four. Before I read them I would say a few words on why they are to be read at all, and why in this manner.

Our generation has done its learning for the most part upon books recovered. The War of the Saints lasted a hundred years; the libraries of many cities were burned and many copies scattered. In the twelve years since the war, you and I have gone from city to city seeking them, have pieced them together volume by volume, and have collated the fragments after the order that Babel left us. That work is not finished, and it must be finished. Yet I have often thought: our forebears kept the books that Babel left behind, but the old books we recover must one day all have been read through, and the blank pages where those before us never set pen must be filled by those who come after. What I shall read to you today are the places where we have looked in the old books and found no answer.

A learning is alive so long as it has questions to ask. When its questions are all answered, or when no one asks any more, nothing is left of it but copying. The use of a good question does not lie in its answer alone. To answer it men must devise new methods, and the new methods may be taken to answer other questions; the longer a question stands unanswered, the more methods, as a rule, grow up around it. There are many methods in the old books of Babel which we can use to this day without knowing for what question they were first devised.

Questions come by two roads. Some arise from what we see in the world: why the tides rise and fall, how a mana core is formed in the sea of cloud, why the plagues come most in the tide years. Others arise from the learning itself: whoever counts the primes is led, as he counts, to ask how they are spread further on; whoever writes down the laws is led, as he writes, to ask whether this manner of writing can write down every law. The first keep learning close to the things of the world; the second make learning grow new methods of its own. Both must be asked. Among the twenty-four there are both, and often a question of the first kind, pursued far enough, becomes one of the second.

As to what questions deserve a place in the list, I have held to five rules.

The first is that a question must be asked clearly; so clearly that, when one day a man brings an answer, we shall all be able to judge whether he has answered it. Each question is therefore first set down as a plain question. Some of them are still roughly put, because I could put them no better. If those who come after can put them more finely, that is good; to put a question finely is already a step toward its answer.

The second is that a question must be hard, so hard that it cannot be answered without some new method; but not so hard that there is no way in. A question of which no one can even see where to begin serves only to dismay. To most of the questions, therefore, I have added a few words on how much we now know, and where one might begin. One or two have only their sentence, because beyond asking I have nothing to say.

The third is that “it can be done” is an answer, and “it cannot be done” is an answer also. To find out which roads are impassable is itself part of the work of learning. Among the books of Babel there is an old proof that there is no greatest prime. Once that proof was made, no one in the world went looking for the greatest prime again; this one “there is none” has saved those after us how much labour, and opened how many other questions! It may be that the answer to some of today’s questions is “it cannot be done”. If so, I pray you not to think the labour wasted: to prove that a thing cannot be done is, in the eyes of the Society, as much an answer as to prove that it can.

The fourth is that an answer must be written out, so that a scholar of another city can check it. A proof must be written so that it can be followed step by step; a proof that only its author can follow is not yet an answer. Where we ask about the things of the world, the answer must state the conditions and the errors of its trials, and men of another city, doing as it says, must obtain results that agree; a method that has worked every time it was tried, but of which no one can say under what conditions it works or how well, is still only a clue. A reading of a script must agree with the other fragments. In law, in number, and in casting, this rule is the same. Some say that a strict proof is always a long one, and that insight reaches the end sooner. I do not think so. He who writes strictly must make clear to himself by what right each step stands; when that is clear, the needless steps fall away of themselves, and the strict proof is often the simple one. Insight is a good thing, but it becomes learning only when it is written in words that others can check.

The fifth is that it is better to divide one question into several than to knead several matters into one. If a question hides several things within it, then when one of them has been answered others will suppose that all have been. Matters that belong together may stand in one question, but the parts that must be judged separately are to be marked.

When a question cannot be answered, two roads remain. One is to ask it more narrowly, and first answer some special case; it is in this sense that I ask the Third Question first of planes of three dimensions. The other is to ask it more widely: a question that cannot be answered by itself will sometimes show its way in when it is looked at among a whole kind of questions. If both roads fail, it is most often because some method is lacking, or some manner of writing. Our way of writing numbers has come down to us through thousands of years, and many matters of number become simple when they are written otherwise; it is the writing of the laws that the Sixth Question asks after.

I have besides a conviction, which I lay before you to weigh: that every question clearly asked has an answer, whether “it can be done”, or “it cannot be done”, or “it can be done under these conditions”. That we cannot answer a question today does not mean that it can never be answered. This conviction is my own; the Society does not depend upon it in listing these questions. Only, he who says “it cannot be done” must say clearly what it is that cannot be done, and under what conditions; to be unable to answer for a time is no proof that the thing cannot be done.

The order of the twenty-four runs, roughly, from the most abstract to what touches us most nearly. The first to the fifth ask about number, shape and proof; the sixth to the eleventh, about law and casting; the twelfth to the fourteenth, about the elements, the tides and the mana pivots; the fifteenth to the twentieth, about the cities, the crystals, the cores, translation and seals; the twenty-first to the twenty-third, about plague and the mana pool; the twenty-fourth, about the books of Babel. I do not mean by this order that the earlier are of more account. One learning has often to borrow the methods of another; I read them together that each of you may look at the questions of the others.

**THE FIRST QUESTION. — OF THE DENSITY OF THE PRIMES**

**Question.** As the primes grow sparser, is there an exact law of their density?

Between one and a hundred there are twenty-five primes; between a hundred and two hundred only twenty-one; and by ten thousand there remain on the average some twelve in every hundred numbers. The books of Babel prove that there is no greatest prime, but they do not say how the primes grow sparse. The copyists of Chaen have counted the primes below a hundred thousand for me, noting the count at every ten thousand; the counts are given in Table I.

The primes added in each ten thousand grow fewer, but they grow fewer very slowly, and not steadily: the eighth ten thousand has some twenty more than the seventh. Looking at these numbers I judge that the primes thin out, on the whole, evenly; so evenly that it seems there must be a law, while the small rises and falls seem to have none. The primes are like raindrops falling on the ground: watch a small patch, and the drops fall without any order; look at a wide field, and they fall evenly.

I ask: given a number, how many primes are there below it, and is there an exact law? If no exact law can be written, how near can an approximate law come, and by how much at most can it be wrong? This question may begin with what is approximate, but it should not stop there. What I seek is not a greater table, for a table can always be counted further one number at a time; it is a law by which, without counting one by one, the density may be told as a whole, together with the most by which it may be wrong. I guess that the exact law will not be a simple formula; it may be that one must count in another manner, counting not the primes themselves but something else joined with them, and reasoning back from that.

The copyists told me one thing more: the primes sometimes crowd together, and sometimes stand far apart; pairs of primes that differ by two, such as eleven and thirteen, seventeen and nineteen, are still met with often as far as a hundred thousand. Whether such pairs go on without end I do not make a question of its own, but set it down here for those who count the primes to bear in mind.

**THE SECOND QUESTION. — OF EVEN NUMBERS AND PRIMES**

**Question.** Is every even number greater than two the sum of two primes?

This question I copied from the margin of an old manuscript in Chaen. It is a book of number; who copied it no one knows. Between his pages of copying he amused himself by breaking even numbers into parts, and at the corner of the leaf he left this line in a small hand. I have tried it for him, and it holds for every even number below ten thousand; many even numbers can be broken in more than one way, and the larger numbers, on the whole, tend to have more ways. But I do not know why it holds, nor can I see how it might be proved. A question that can be asked so plainly in a single line, and that we cannot answer, is the very kind the Society should list.

This question looks near to the first, but it is different. The first asks how many primes there are on the whole; this asks whether every single even number can be broken exactly. To know roughly how many primes there are on the whole is not to know that every even number can be broken: a single even number that cannot be broken would make the line false, and what holds on the whole says nothing of any one number. That the larger numbers tend to have more ways makes one believe it true; but “more ways” holds on the average, and the average does not reach to every number.

**THE THIRD QUESTION. — OF THE SHAPES OF CLOSED PLANES**

**Question.** What shapes may a closed plane have?

There are demiplanes in which a man who walks straight on comes back, after a while, to the place he started from; they have no edge, and there is no walking out of them. I ask first of the simplest kind: planes finite in extent, all of one piece, without edge, and like ordinary space in the neighbourhood of every place. That a man comes back where he started is only one sign of such a plane, and not a test of it. What shapes may such a plane have? Given two closed planes, is there a way to judge whether they are of the same shape, and so to list all the shapes there can be? By shape I mean here not size but manner of joining: if one plane can be changed slowly into another without tearing, without joining, and without bringing two places into one, they are of one shape. Planes of three dimensions touch us most nearly, and I ask first of these; of more dimensions we cannot yet even say how we ought to think, but I list them here as well.

At present we know only one method: to draw a cord round some circuit in the plane and see whether it can be drawn together to a point without being cut. A plane in which every cord can be drawn together, and a plane in which some cord cannot, are surely not of the same shape. That method tells some shapes apart, but not all: whether two planes in each of which every cord can be drawn together must therefore be of one shape, I do not know.

**THE FOURTH QUESTION. — OF THE FOUNDATIONS OF NUMBER**

**Question.** Can the rules of derivation prove of themselves that they do not contradict themselves?

From accepted truths we derive, by a set of rules, the many conclusions of the science of number. Suppose that these rules can be listed one by one in due form, that they suffice to express the arithmetic of whole numbers, and that they do not in fact contradict themselves: can it then be proved, by these rules alone, that they will never lead both to a statement and to its contrary? We believe that they will not contradict themselves; but belief is not proof.

What this question asks is not that more conclusions be derived, but that the rules by which we derive them be given surety. A set of rules that has once led to a contradiction can lead to anything whatever, and all that it leads to is then worth nothing. It may be that this question requires first that derivation itself be written as something that can be checked by rule: which step is a derivation and which is not must be checked as an account is checked.

**THE FIFTH QUESTION. — OF JUDGING A FORMULA TO ITS END**

**Question.** Is there a means by which, before any formula is cast, its outcome may be known?

A formula, once unfolded, comes to rest or goes on working without end; whether a self-adjusting ward will go on adjusting itself for ever, we can now only cast it and watch; and that it is slow to stop is no proof that it will never stop. If there is such a means, many dangerous formulae need not be tried upon men; if there is not, we ought to know why not, and where the bounds of the “not” lie.

**THE SIXTH QUESTION. — OF A RECKONING FOR THE LAWS**

**Question.** Can the lines of law be written in a strict reckoning?

A law-mage sees the lines of law by what he grasped in ascending the Tower and by the divisions handed down in the manuscripts; when two of them speak of a line, they must often gesture at length, and when they have done they may not mean the same line. I ask whether the laws can be written as number is written, so that different men, following the reckoning, shall reach the same result. If they can, much that today can only be passed by word of mouth may be written down and checked.

The thirty-six thousand characters write how a working is to be cast, not how it is to be reckoned: with them we can carve a formula, but we cannot set two formulae side by side and reckon with them. The science of number travels far because it has a manner of writing, and what one man has derived another can derive again by following it. If the laws had such a manner of writing, they need not be handed down by insight alone.

**THE SEVENTH QUESTION. — OF THE LAYERS OF THE WORLD**

**Question.** By what way does a formula draw its power?

That elemental workings draw their power through the elements no one doubts. Whence law-workings and string-spells draw theirs, opinions differ. Some hold that the world has two layers only, the elements and Tiamat, and that the lines of law are Tiamat itself; then both kinds draw from the lines of law. Yet casters say often that within a ward against magic a law-working cannot be cast, while a string-spell often can. How many layers lie between a formula and Tiamat, and what are they? Where the Babel fragments speak of a web, they use more than one word; I think there may be a clue there.

If this question can be answered, many strange things of casting may find their account: why the same formula cannot be cast in some places, and why some wards hold back one kind of formula and not another.

**THE EIGHTH QUESTION. — WHETHER MANA IS CONTINUOUS**

**Question.** Is mana continuous?

**THE NINTH QUESTION. — OF THE BOUNDARY AT THE TENTH RING**

**Question.** Why does casting harm the upper limit of the mana pool from the tenth ring on?

At the ninth ring and below it does not; only the Silver Folk take no such harm from any working below the thirteenth ring. Why does the common boundary lie at the tenth ring precisely, and that of the Silver Folk at the thirteenth? Is there a measure that relates the harm to the ring? Some say that it is the laws punishing those who exceed their right; that is a figure, not a reason. This question asks about the body of the caster and also about the frame of the lines of law, and I think the answer must be sought from both ends together.

**THE TENTH QUESTION. — OF THE THIRTY-SIX THOUSAND CHARACTERS**

**Question.** Do the thirty-six thousand characters suffice to write every law that can be written? And are there among them characters which, in every lawful use, can be spelt by others, and so are superfluous?

These characters came down to us from Babel. Here are two questions, which I have joined, because whoever answers the one will most likely have to think at the same time of the other. The two must be judged separately. If the characters do not suffice, there are laws that cannot be written in these characters, and more characters, or another manner of writing, will be needed; if some are superfluous, the carving may be made simpler, and less liable to error, by striking them out.

**THE ELEVENTH QUESTION. — OF CAUSE IN WORKINGS OF TIME**

**Question.** Can a working of time alter what had already happened before it was cast?

If it can, cause is no longer a line that runs forward, the records it touches must all be checked again, and how far its effects reach must first be made out; if it cannot, what is it that bounds it? This question may not be tried at the hazard of men; it must first be argued from the workings and the laws, and then sought in such observations as harm no one.

**THE TWELFTH QUESTION. — OF THE TURNING OF THE ELEMENTS**

**Question.** When the four elements are turned one into another, what is it that does not change?

Fire into air, water into earth: casters do it every day. Is there an account in which, entering the elements before and after, together with the vessels that hold them and all that lies about, the sums always agree? Today we know only that every turning has its loss; we do not know where the loss goes. If there is such an account, the turning of the elements may be reckoned by it, and need not rest on experience alone.

**THE THIRTEENTH QUESTION. — OF THE TIDES OF MANA**

**Question.** Have the risings and fallings of the tides a fixed law?

The tides come once in ten years, and a great tide once in a hundred; this our forebears wrote down. Of the other risings and fallings, great and small, there seems no order. Have they to do with the twelve mana moons? Can they be reckoned beforehand, as the sun and moons are reckoned? This question wants the records of many generations. I ask every city to keep a record of its tides, and to keep it, as far as may be, in one manner: at what hours of the day to read, how many times, and with what instrument, should all be fixed; let each record also note the place of the reading, the day on which the instrument was last set true, and any unusual supply of mana at the time. Only when the records of the cities can be compared one with another can anything be reckoned from them.

**THE FOURTEENTH QUESTION. — OF STEADY OUTPUT IN THE TIDES**

**Question.** Can a mana pivot, under the shock of the tides, hold its own output steady?

When the tide comes, the output of a pivot rises and falls with it. Today all depends on the craftsmen who stand by the pivot, watching and adjusting. When the craftsman adjusts well, the city feels nothing; when he is slow, or adjusts too far, the supply comes now strong and now weak. The strength of men has its end: as the cities are built greater and the engines more numerous, a day will come when they cannot all be watched. So long as the tide does not exceed what the pivot can bear, can the pivot be made to hold itself steady, without a man to stand by it?

**THE FIFTEENTH QUESTION. — OF THE KEEPING ALOFT OF THE CITIES**

**Question.** When its levitation arrays are damaged in part, can a city keep from falling without the help of hands?

A city is borne upon several groups of arrays. If one group is damaged, the others must bear more; whether they can bear it rests today on the experience of the craftsmen, and if the repair is slow the city will lean. I ask whether, when the remaining arrays still have lift enough and the supply and the main ward hold, a city can keep itself steady and aloft without the help of hands.

**THE SIXTEENTH QUESTION. — OF THE ELEMENTAL CRYSTALS**

**Question.** When an elemental crystal has spent its mana, can it be renewed?

A spent crystal is a dead stone. If it can be renewed, by what?

**THE SEVENTEENTH QUESTION. — OF THE MANA CORES**

**Question.** When a mana core is spent, can it be charged anew?

If it can, is there loss in the charging, and how many times may it be charged?

**THE EIGHTEENTH QUESTION. — OF DEFLECTED TRANSLATION**

**Question.** Can the landing of deflected translation be reckoned beforehand?

Deflected translation has saved the lives of many law-mages, yet no one has yet reckoned its landing exactly, and sometimes it falls far off, into a place more perilous than the one it fled. I ask whether the exact landing can be reckoned from what is known before the casting alone; and if it cannot, how small at least the error can be made.

**THE NINETEENTH QUESTION. — OF THE BOUNDS OF SEALS**

**Question.** Can the seals now in use be freed at last from tending?

All the seals now in use must be tended; every few years they must be searched and mended. Men are careless, and when a seal is lost it is the affair of a whole city. If they cannot be freed, by what law do they wear, and how often ought they to be searched?

**THE TWENTIETH QUESTION. — OF THE MAKING OF MANA CORES**

**Question.** How are the mana cores formed in the sea of cloud?

They are gathered from the sea of cloud, and each one gathered is one fewer. How long does a core take to form? Do we gather them faster than they grow? The sixteenth and seventeenth questions and this one are, as I see them, three faces of one matter: whether what is spent can be renewed, whether what is emptied can be filled again, and whether what is not yet there can grow. Together they ask how long the strength of the League can last.

**THE TWENTY-FIRST QUESTION. — OF THE SOURCES OF PLAGUE**

**Question.** Whence come the plagues?

There are several fierce plagues which strike the mortals of the cities, the people of mixed blood, the herds and the crops, while the Silver Folk do not take them. The old people say that the plague years are mostly tide years. Why do they come with the tide? Why do the Silver Folk not take them? That itself is a clue. When the source is known, a defence can be founded upon it; until it is known, the defences the cities have already found by experience should be recorded too. I ask every city to record together its tide years, its cases of sickness, and the traffic of its routes.

**THE TWENTY-SECOND QUESTION. — OF THE NATURE OF THE MANA POOL**

**Question.** What is the mana pool?

Where in the body does it lie, how does it grow, and how is it harmed? We know only that it may be drained and may recover, and that when its upper limit is harmed the harm is sometimes long in mending; but at present we cannot even tell a passing exhaustion from a lasting harm. So long as we cannot, we do not know which caster need only rest, and which must cast less and be treated.

**THE TWENTY-THIRD QUESTION. — OF HARM TO THE MANA POOL**

**Question.** Can damage to the mana pool be repaired safely, swiftly and for all?

I ask for a repairing that all may use, and not for the lucky recovery of one or two. This question can hardly be taken in hand until the twenty-second shows some light; I list it by itself none the less, because those who are harmed cannot wait.

**THE TWENTY-FOURTH QUESTION. — OF THE UNKNOWN SCRIPT OF BABEL**

**Question.** Can the third script in the scrolls of the Babel Relic be read?

In the scrolls of the Babel Relic, beside the two scripts that we can read, there is a third, which no one can read. The copyists copy it stroke by stroke as they copy the rest, only they do not know what they copy. What does it say? I list this question because it may lead us back into the old books; but this time we must read them by methods of our own.

The twenty-four questions have been read. I have only a few words more.

These questions belong to several learnings, but they do not stand each alone. The methods by which the primes are counted may perhaps be used to count the risings of the tides; the judging of formulae and the foundations of number are two faces of one matter; and how the mana cores are formed and whether they can be charged again will most likely be answered from the same place. As learning is divided ever more finely, and each city and each school keeps its own field, this cannot be helped; but a question must often be answered by a man of another field.

Some fear that as learning grows ever greater a day must come when no one man can know the whole of it, and that learning will then fall apart. I do not see it so. Each new method makes many old things simple, and what once wanted ten volumes to understand will afterwards want one. Learning will grow ever greater, but it need not grow ever more confused.

The Society asks you to carry this list home, to copy it for the men of your city and your school, and to copy it also for those of other cities and other schools. Whoever answers a question, or proves that a question cannot be answered, is asked to send it to the Society, which will record it, and read it at the next Assembly.

This list is not sealed. Of the twenty-four, some will one day be asked more finely, some will be shown to have been asked amiss, and there will always be new questions to add. I hope only that at the next Assembly, and the one after, someone will stand here and read not questions but answers; and if what he reads is “it cannot be done”, that too will be an answer.

I have one request more. When a question is answered, let the answer be copied twice, and kept in two cities. We have only just brought our books back from a war, and you all know how easily a manuscript is lost.

## On the Density of the Primes

**ON THE DENSITY OF THE PRIMES**

By ALDIN.

The Society has done me the honour, in the past year, of recognizing me as an Academician. I think I cannot better show my thanks than by making early use of the leave it has thereby given me, and laying before the Society an investigation. The subject I have chosen is the density of the primes. Severin placed it first among his twenty-four questions; Crossley, nearly two hundred years ago, proved its approximate law; and many have studied it since. It may not, perhaps, be unworthy of such a purpose.

What Crossley proved is that the number of primes up to a large number $x$ is nearly $x$ divided by its logarithm; and that a closer approximation is the smooth counting formula, which adds up, piece by piece, the density $1 / (log t)$ at every place. How far the true number differs from the smooth counting formula he left to those who should come after. I shall show here that this difference is governed by the roots of a certain function, and that its size depends upon where those roots lie.

**I. THE DENSITY FUNCTION**

Crossley’s proof makes use of an equation which joins all the primes with all the whole numbers:

$$ product_p 1/(1 - p^(-s)) = sum_(n=1)^oo 1/n^s $$ (1)

The product on the left is taken over all primes $p$, and the sum on the right over all positive whole numbers $n$. The equation holds because every whole number is the product of primes in one way only. Crossley allowed $s$ to take real values greater than one alone. I let $s$ be a complex number, $s = sigma + t i$; so long as its real part $sigma$ exceeds one, both sides converge and are equal as before. I denote it by $zeta(s)$ and call it the density function, since the whole density of the primes is written in it.

Equation (1) gives $zeta(s)$ only where $sigma > 1$. To learn its form elsewhere we first write it otherwise. Let $Gamma$ be the function that extends the factorial to complex values, $Gamma(n) = (n - 1)!$. When $sigma > 0$, from

$$ integral_0^oo e^(-n x) x^(s-1) d x = Gamma(s) / n^s $$ (2)

we obtain, by summing over $n$ when $sigma > 1$,

$$ Gamma(s) zeta(s) = integral_0^oo x^(s-1) / (e^x - 1) d x $$ (3)

**II. EXTENSION TO THE WHOLE PLANE**

Consider now the integral $integral_oo^oo (-x)^(s-1) / (e^x - 1) d x$, taken along this path: from positive infinity, close above the positive real axis, to the neighbourhood of the origin; once round the origin in the positive sense; and back, close below the positive axis, to positive infinity. The circuit is to be small, so as not to enclose the other zeros $plus.minus 2 pi i, plus.minus 4 pi i, dots$ of $e^x - 1$. By $(-x)^(s-1)$ is meant $e^((s - 1) log(-x))$, the logarithm of $-x$ being real when $x$ is negative. This integral has a definite value for every complex $s$, and may everywhere be expanded term by term. When $sigma > 1$, the path may be drawn down upon the axis: the part round the small circuit then vanishes, and the parts above and below the axis together give $-2 i sin(pi s)$ times the integral in (3); and we find

$$ 2 sin(pi s) Gamma(s) zeta(s) = i integral_oo^oo (-x)^(s-1) / (e^x - 1) d x $$ (4)

The integral on the right, once the radius of the circuit is fixed at less than $2 pi$, has a definite value for every $s$ and is everywhere smooth. The factor $sin(pi s) Gamma(s)$ on the left, however, has its zeros, and the division must be made with care. Since $Gamma(s) Gamma(1 - s) = pi / sin(pi s)$, equation (4) may be written $zeta(s) = (i Gamma(1 - s)) / (2 pi) integral_oo^oo (-x)^(s-1) / (e^x - 1) d x$, so that $zeta(s)$ can become infinite only where $Gamma(1 - s)$ does, at $s = 1, 2, 3, dots$. At $s = 2, 3, dots$, however, $zeta(s)$ is already given finite by (1), and there the integral vanishes; only at $s = 1$ does it truly become infinite, as $1 / (s - 1)$ does. Thus $zeta(s)$ is extended over the whole complex plane.

**III. THE SYMMETRY**

The function $zeta(s)$ has besides a symmetry, which joins $s$ with $1 - s$. Let

$$ psi(x) = sum_(n=1)^oo e^(-n^2 pi x) $$

The tide-reckoners of the Tower of All Things, in parting a record into its constituent tides, make frequent use of an equation which here becomes, for positive $x$,

$$ 2 psi(x) + 1 = x^(-1/2) (2 psi(1/x) + 1) $$ (5)

Putting $s/2$ for $s$ and $n^2 pi x$ for $x$ in (2), we have $integral_0^oo e^(-n^2 pi x) x^(s/2 - 1) d x = pi^(-s/2) n^(-s) Gamma(s/2)$; and summing over $n$ when $sigma > 1$, $pi^(-s/2) Gamma(s/2) zeta(s) = integral_0^oo psi(x) x^(s/2 - 1) d x$. Divide this integral at $x = 1$, and carry the first part beyond one by means of (5); there results

$$ pi^(-s/2) Gamma(s/2) zeta(s) = 1/(s (s - 1)) + integral_1^oo psi(x) (x^(s/2 - 1) + x^((1 - s)/2 - 1)) d x $$ (6)

The right side is unchanged when $1 - s$ is put for $s$; therefore

$$ pi^(-s/2) Gamma(s/2) zeta(s) = pi^(-(1-s)/2) Gamma((1-s)/2) zeta(1-s) $$ (7)

Equations (6) and (7) are first derived where $sigma > 1$; since the right side of (6) has a meaning for every $s$ other than 0 and 1, both hold everywhere. By (7), when $s$ is $-2, -4, -6, dots$, the factor $Gamma(s/2)$ on the left becomes infinite while the right side is finite; therefore $zeta(s)$ vanishes at these places. These I call its trivial roots, and the others its non-trivial roots.

**IV. THE ROOTS**

Put $s = 1/2 + t i$, and let

$$ xi(t) = 1/2 s (s - 1) pi^(-s/2) Gamma(s/2) zeta(s) $$ (8)

By (7), $xi(t)$ is unchanged when $-t$ is put for $t$; by (6), it has a definite value everywhere in the plane and nowhere becomes infinite, and it may be written as a series in powers of $t^2$ which converges very rapidly; and for real $t$ it takes real values. The non-trivial roots of $zeta(s)$ are exactly the roots of $xi(t)$. The imaginary part of $t$ at each of these roots lies between $-1/2$ and $1/2$; in other words, the non-trivial roots all lie in the band between the lines $sigma = 0$ and $sigma = 1$, and lie symmetrically on either side of the line $sigma = 1/2$. There is no non-trivial root outside the band: where $sigma > 1$ the logarithm of the product (1) converges absolutely, and $zeta$ does not vanish; where $sigma < 0$ there is none, by the symmetry (7). Whether there is a root upon the two edges of the band requires another argument. I believe that there being no root upon the edges is the same thing as Crossley’s approximate law, that is, that the ratio of the number of primes to $x$ divided by $log x$ tends to one; but this must be proved separately, and I shall not go into it here.

The number of non-trivial roots, counted according to their multiplicity, whose $t$ has its real part between $0$ and $T$ is approximately

$$ T/(2 pi) log(T/(2 pi)) - T/(2 pi) $$ (9)

and the error is of no greater order than $log T$. The number may be found by carrying $xi$ once round the boundary of a rectangle and observing how far its angle has turned: the principal term comes from the angle of $pi^(-s/2) Gamma(s/2)$ along the line, and on the other sides the angle of $zeta$ changes by no more than of the order of $log T$. Where a side passes through a root, the side is moved a little.

I have reckoned the roots with $T$ up to 30. I first prepared from (6) the series for $xi$, with bounds for its remainders, and then asked the star-reckoning arrays of the Tower of All Things to unfold the tables by differences. Since $xi(t)$ is real for real $t$, every change of its sign encloses at least one real root of odd multiplicity, lying upon the line $sigma = 1/2$; a root of even multiplicity, however, makes no change of sign, so that the changes of sign alone do not suffice. Between $t = 0$ and $t = 30$ I found three changes of sign, between 14.1 and 14.2, between 21.0 and 21.1, and between 25.0 and 25.1; at both ends of each the values far exceed the bound of the remainder, so that the signs cannot be wrong. Then, by the turning of the angle described above, I carried $xi$ round the rectangle in which the real part of $t$ runs from 0 to 30 and its imaginary part from $-1$ to $1$, taking a point at every twentieth part along the sides; the value of $xi$ at every point lies far from zero, and between neighbouring points its angle turns by less than a quarter of a circuit, so that no whole turn can be missed. The angle turned three times in all: this stretch contains three roots, counted by multiplicity. The three changes of sign therefore each enclose a single root, and there is no other root, on the line or off it. Their places are given in Table I. Formula (9) gives nearly three at $T = 30$, in agreement.

**V. THE NUMBER OF PRIMES**

Let $F(x)$ be the number of primes less than $x$; when $x$ is itself a prime, let $F(x)$ be the mean of its values just before and just after the step. Let further

$$ f(x) = F(x) + 1/2 F(x^(1/2)) + 1/3 F(x^(1/3)) + dots $$ (10)

so that every prime is counted as one, every square of a prime as one half, every cube as one third, and so on. Taking logarithms of both sides of (1) we find

$$ log zeta(s) = sum_p sum_(n=1)^oo 1/n p^(-n s) = s integral_1^oo f(x) x^(-s-1) d x $$ (11)

This equation gives $log zeta$ from $f$; it may also be turned about, to give $f$ from $log zeta$:

$$ f(x) = 1/(2 pi i) integral_(a - i oo)^(a + i oo) log zeta(s) x^s (d s)/s $$ (12)

the integral being taken along the line $sigma = a$, with $a$ and $x$ greater than one, between the limits $a plus.minus i T$, $T$ being then made to grow without end. Here $log zeta$ is defined where $sigma > 1$ by the series in (11), and is real upon the positive real axis. At the powers of primes the integral gives the mean of the values just before and just after the step, in agreement with the convention of (10). When the tide-reckoners put a record together again from its constituent tides, they use such a pair of reckonings as this; only here it is the primes that are put together.

Now $xi(t)$ may be written as $xi(0)$ multiplied by the product of all the factors $(1 - t^2 / alpha^2)$, where $alpha$ runs over the roots of $xi$, $alpha$ and $-alpha$ being taken once only, and repeated roots according to their multiplicity. Two things are needed for this: by (6), $xi(t)$ grows no faster than of the order of $e^(|t| log |t|)$, so that the product converges; and since $xi$ is even, and grows so slowly, there can be no exponential factor besides the product. Accordingly $log zeta(s)$ falls into several parts: one arising from the infinity at $s = 1$, one from $Gamma$, and one from each of the non-trivial roots. These are put one by one into (12). The integral is first cut off at $a plus.minus i T$, with $T$ taken between the heights of two roots, and the path pushed to the left; each root then gives a term, $s = 1$ gives $L(x)$, the part from $Gamma$ gives the last integral, and the constant gives $-log 2$; and it is shown that the integrals along the upper and lower sides of the moved path vanish as $T$ grows without end. We obtain

$$ f(x) = L(x) - sum_rho L(x^rho) - log 2 + integral_x^oo (d t)/(t (t^2 - 1) log t) $$ (13)

Here $L(x)$ is Crossley’s smooth counting formula,

$$ L(x) = integral_0^x (d t)/(log t) $$

the integral being taken, where it passes $t = 1$, as the limit of equal parts on either side. Crossley began his integral at 2; the two forms differ only by a constant, about 1.045, which may be neglected in the comparisons below. And $rho$ runs over the non-trivial roots, counted according to their multiplicity. $L(x^rho)$ is to be understood as $integral_(-oo)^(rho log x) e^u / u d u$, the path coming round from the left to $rho log x$, and not by first reckoning $x^rho$ and then taking its logarithm; the terms from the upper and lower halves of the plane are conjugate. The terms are taken up to the roots whose imaginary parts do not exceed $T$ in magnitude, and $T$ is then made to grow without end. Lastly, inverting (10), we have

$$ F(x) = sum_(m=1)^oo mu(m)/m f(x^(1/m)) $$ (14)

where $mu(1)$ is one, $mu(m)$ is zero when $m$ has a square factor, and otherwise is one or minus one according as the number of prime factors of $m$ is even or odd. For a given $x$ the terms with $m$ greater than $(log x) / (log 2)$ are all nothing, so that (14) has in truth only finitely many terms. Equations (13) and (14) together are an exact formula for the number of primes; I call them the density formula.

**VI. THE ERROR**

If the smooth principal term of the density formula is inverted by (14), there results

$$ A(x) = sum_(m=1)^oo mu(m)/m L(x^(1/m)) $$ (15)

$$ = L(x) - 1/2 L(x^(1/2)) - 1/3 L(x^(1/3)) - 1/5 L(x^(1/5)) + 1/6 L(x^(1/6)) - dots $$

which I call the estimate of the density formula. The series (15) converges slowly, and for reckoning I use instead this equivalent form, which converges fast:

$$ A(x) = 1 + sum_(k=1)^oo (log x)^k / (k dot k! dot zeta(k + 1)) $$

When $x$ is large, the estimate differs from the smooth counting formula only by $1/2 L(sqrt(x))$ and a few further terms, together of the order of $sqrt(x) / (log x)$.

The difference between $F(x)$ and the estimate comes, apart from a few small terms inverted from $-log 2$ and from the last integral, which within the range of the table together amount to less than one, wholly from the non-trivial roots. Each root $rho = beta + gamma i$ contributes a term whose envelope is about $x^beta / (|rho| log x)$, and whose phase turns with $log x$ in the period $2 pi / gamma$; and the terms of different roots partly cancel one another. The roots go in groups: $rho$ and $1 - overline(rho)$ lie symmetrically on either side of $sigma = 1/2$, and the one of a group that lies to the right has real part $1/2 + |beta - 1/2|$, which fixes how fast the swing of that group may grow. Thus the farther a group of roots lies from the line $sigma = 1/2$, the greater the swing it may give; how far the true number departs from the smooth counting formula depends wholly upon where the roots lie.

If all the non-trivial roots lie upon $sigma = 1/2$, the envelope of each term is $sqrt(x) / (|rho| log x)$ only. To add them up it is convenient first to count otherwise, reckoning each power $p^k$ of a prime as $log p$, so long as $p^k$ is less than $x$, and taking the mean at each step as before; call this count $H(x)$. Its exact formula is derived as (13) is, but is cleaner: $H(x) = x - sum_rho x^rho / rho - log 2 pi - 1/2 log(1 - x^(-2))$. If the roots are cut off where their imaginary parts exceed $T$ in magnitude, the part cut off is of no greater order than $x (log x T)^2 / T$, and by (9) the part kept is of no greater order than $sqrt(x) (log T)^2$. Taking $T$ comparable with $sqrt(x)$, both parts are of no greater order than $sqrt(x) (log x)^2$; and in passing back from $H$ to the number of primes one logarithm is removed. Hence:

When $x$ is large enough, the true number of primes up to $x$ differs from the estimate of the density formula by no more than a constant multiple of $sqrt(x) log x$; and from the smooth counting formula by an amount of the same order.

I conjecture that this is so. But I can derive it only on the supposition that all the roots lie upon the line, and that supposition I cannot prove. The converse holds also: if for all large $x$ the error is no more than a constant multiple of $sqrt(x) log x$, then $H(x) - x$ is of no greater order than $sqrt(x) (log x)^2$, so that $-(zeta'(s)) / zeta(s) - s / (s - 1) = s integral_1^oo (H(x) - x) x^(-s-1) d x$ has a definite value everywhere where $sigma > 1/2$, and $zeta$ has no root there; and by the symmetry the non-trivial roots all lie upon the line. If a single root lies off the line, then no constant can hold the error for all large $x$. The two matters are therefore one: to ask how great the error is, is to ask where the roots lie. The estimates above I have given only in outline, not in full.

## On Steady Flow

**ON STEADY FLOW**

By LOWEN, of White Tower University.

[Received the Month of Autumn Dusk, F.E. 26.]

**Summary.** A mana pivot that adjusts its intake against the deviation of its own output, in proportion to that deviation, may hold that output steady in the tides; yet on the trial benches some such returns steady the output, and others make it swing ever wider. Without entering into the carving of the return-pattern, this paper treats the dynamics of the return. While the deviations are small and the return does not exceed the capacity of the pivot, the motion of the deviation may be resolved into parts, each of which either increases continually, or diminishes continually, or swings with increasing amplitude, or swings with diminishing amplitude; the condition of steadiness is that every part be of the second or the fourth kind. When the pivot’s own response, the delay of the reading and the delay in changing the intake are taken together, the deviation satisfies an equation of the third degree, and the condition of steadiness is that the product of the two middle coefficients exceed the product of the two extreme ones; when the three delays are equal, the strength of the return must be less than eight. The stronger the return, the smaller the deviation the tide leaves behind, but the nearer the bound. To remove even this deviation an accumulating part must be added; if it accumulates too fast, the output again swings ever wider, and its bound is given. These reckonings agree in the main with the records of six trial benches at Tallin, Kanaich and White Tower. At the end a method is given by which craftsmen may measure the delays and fix the strength, and the places the reckoning does not reach are stated.

**1. The question**

Severin’s Fourteenth Question asks: “Can a mana pivot, under the shock of the tides, hold its own output steady?” [1] For seven hundred years every city has answered it with men: when the tide comes, craftsmen stand by the pivot, watching the output and adjusting the intake. In the year of the Great Tide the engines of several cities were very nearly not held; I was then at White Tower, and saw craftsmen who did not leave the pivot for three days and nights. The year F.E. 24 was the third tide year since the Great Tide, and in its Month of Scrolls the city engine of Tallin twice ran now strong and now weak upon the crest of the tide; the craftsmen on watch took two days to bring it level [9].

The thought of letting the pivot change its own intake according to the deviation of its output is not new. Since the year 24 the craftsmen of Tallin and Kanaich have tried several carvings upon trial benches, and the workshop of White Tower University has made two benches for me [10]. The results are perplexing. Some pivots, once given a return, are left with only a small deviation of output by the push of the tide; others swing ever wider, so that the return must be cut off; and the same carving, cut a little stronger, passes from the first sort to the second. The craftsmen say that the return must be “just right”, but no one can say how much that is.

I propose, without entering into the manner in which a return-pattern is carved, to treat only of how the pivot moves when it has one. This is the heart of Severin’s question: whether a pivot can hold itself steady depends not upon whether a return can be carved, but upon whether, once carved, it steadies.

Canning reckoned how the lift is shared out anew among the groups of arrays when a city leans [3]. He asked where the city comes to rest, not how it moves before it rests; and the second is what is asked here. That a machine can rest at a certain place, and that it will return to that place when disturbed, are two different things.

**2. The pivot and its return**

Let the output of the pivot exceed its set value by $y$, which I call the deviation of output, and let the intake exceed its set value by $u$. When the intake changes, the output does not follow at once but arrives after a little while; and the tide also pushes the output. Let $u$ be measured in such units that a change $u$ of intake changes the output in the end by $u$, and let the push of the tide be $w$, that is, the change the tide would make in the output if the intake were held fixed. The tide for the most part changes slowly: the ten-year tide, the several constituent tides and the rise and fall within a day are all far slower than the delays of a pivot [4]; only when the head of a tide comes suddenly does it act like a push suddenly applied. The pivot’s own response is written

$$ tau_0 (d y)/(d t) + y = u + w $$ (1)

and $tau_0$ is called the pivot’s own time of response. The return-pattern reads the output, and the reading too cannot follow at once, but follows by degrees according to (2). Let the reading be $z$:

$$ tau_1 (d z)/(d t) + z = y $$ (2)

where $tau_1$ is the delay of the reading. By a delay I mean throughout such a time of response, in which a quantity follows by degrees, and not a waiting after which it leaps at once. When the deviation has been read, the intake is changed, and this too cannot be done in an instant:

$$ tau_2 (d u)/(d t) + u = -K z $$ (3)

where $tau_2$ is the delay in changing the intake, and $K$ the strength of the return: for each part added to the reading, the intake is in the end diminished by $K$ parts. The pivot, the reading-pattern and the intake-pattern need not follow in exactly this manner; I write them so because it is the simplest manner, because it can be measured on the bench, and because the following measured there agrees with it (Section 7). The three are supposed besides to act independently and to remain unchanged during a trial, and the tide to enter only as a push added to the output.

These three equations suppose two things more. First, that the deviations are small, so that everything is proportional to them. Secondly, that the intake the return calls for lies, at every moment of the passing motion, within the capacity of the pivot. By capacity I mean here the upper and lower bounds of what the pivot can take in, in a unit of time: $u$ may be positive or negative, and a greater intake meets the upper bound, a lesser the lower. The capacity depends upon the fuel and upon what is left in the cores, and is not the same thing as core capacity [5], which is the whole energy a core delivers when it is spent; in the trials the cores of every bench were more than half full. If the tide is too violent, and the return calls for an intake beyond these bounds, the intake stands at the bound and no longer follows the reading; equation (3) then fails. Everything reckoned below holds within these two conditions only.

The return-pattern reads the output by a sensing piece such as that of a mana meter [2], set however upon the conduit of the output, so that it reads the mana delivered to the load and not the free mana round about. On the bench it is first set true against known loads, and a second piece, not joined to the conduit, is set beside it, so that the direct effect of the mana round about may be taken out of the reading. The reading must further be compared with a standard. Taunton has shown that standards disturbed by the tide are lastingly changed, in capacity and in response; for the set he compared, the ratio of after to before was 1.011 [8]. The “steadiness” of this paper is therefore steadiness with respect to the standard: if the standard changes, the output changes with it; and a sensing piece that has passed through a tide may have changed its time of response, and must be measured again.

**3. The four kinds of motion**

Let $w$ be constant. When the fixed deviation it leaves has been taken away, the solution of equations (1) to (3) is a sum of terms, each of the form $e^(n t)$, where $n$ is a root of a certain equation (where a root is repeated, the term is multiplied by $t$, $t^2$ and so on, but so long as the real part of the root is negative such terms die away as well). The roots may be real, or they may be complex in pairs. The motion of the deviation may therefore be resolved into parts, the amplitude of each being of one of these four kinds:

First, it may increase continually; secondly, it may diminish continually; thirdly, it may swing to and fro with increasing amplitude; fourthly, it may swing to and fro with diminishing amplitude. At the boundary there is a fifth: it may swing to and fro, neither growing nor dying (Section 5).

The first and third kinds are inconsistent with steady flow; a good return admits only the second and fourth. In the language of reckoning: every real root must be negative, and every complex root must have its real part negative.

**4. When there are only one or two delays**

If there is no return, $K = 0$, the deviation tends to $w$ in a time of the order of $tau_0$: as far as the tide pushes, so far the output departs.

If the intake can be changed without any delay, $tau_2 = 0$, and $n$ satisfies $(tau_0 n + 1)(tau_1 n + 1) + K = 0$. The sum of its two roots is $-(tau_0 + tau_1) / (tau_0 tau_1)$, which is negative, and their product is $(1 + K) / (tau_0 tau_1)$, which is positive; so the two roots are either both negative and real or a pair of complex roots with negative real part. However strong the return, the output will not swing ever wider. Once the two roots are complex, their real part is always $-(tau_0 + tau_1) / (2 tau_0 tau_1)$: a stronger return makes the swinging closer, but the rate at which the swinging dies away is unchanged.

Yet the pivots on the benches do swing ever wider. The reason is that the intake cannot be changed in an instant, and the reading too takes time to follow; the delay in changing the intake is never nothing. The returns treated in this paper have at least three delays, and to the case of three I now turn.

**5. Three delays**

Putting $e^(n t)$ in equations (1) to (3), we find that $n$ must satisfy

$$ (tau_0 n + 1)(tau_1 n + 1)(tau_2 n + 1) + K = 0 $$ (4)

which, when expanded, is

$$ a n^3 + b n^2 + c n + d = 0 $$ (5)

where $a = tau_0 tau_1 tau_2$, $b = tau_0 tau_1 + tau_0 tau_2 + tau_1 tau_2$, $c = tau_0 + tau_1 + tau_2$ and $d = 1 + K$; all four coefficients are positive.

The question is how great $K$ must be before (5) has a root with positive real part. Let $K$ increase from nothing. When $K = 0$ the three roots are $-1/tau_0$, $-1/tau_1$ and $-1/tau_2$, all negative. As $K$ increases the roots move continuously; a real root cannot pass through zero, because at $n = 0$ the left side of (5) is $d$, which is positive. If a root is to acquire a positive real part, therefore, a pair of complex roots must cross the imaginary axis. At the moment of crossing $n$ is purely imaginary, $n = omega i$, and the deviation neither grows nor dies but swings to and fro with the period $2 pi / omega$. Putting $n = omega i$ in (5), the real and the imaginary parts must vanish separately:

$$ b omega^2 = d, quad a omega^2 = c $$

whence, dividing the one by the other, $b c = a d$ at the boundary; (5) then falls exactly into the factors $(a n + b)(n^2 + c/a) = 0$, which give, besides a negative real root, the pair of purely imaginary roots $plus.minus i sqrt(c/a)$. Below the boundary the real parts of all three roots are negative; and as the boundary is passed, the real part of this pair increases with $K$, for at $n = omega i$ the real part of the rate at which $n$ changes with $K$ is $c / (2 (c^2 + b^2 omega^2))$, which is positive. Thus:

**The condition of steadiness.** The product of the two middle coefficients must exceed the product of the two extreme ones,

$$ b c > a d $$ (6)

that is, the strength of the return must be less than

$$ K^* = (b c) / a - 1 = ((tau_0 tau_1 + tau_0 tau_2 + tau_1 tau_2)(tau_0 + tau_1 + tau_2)) / (tau_0 tau_1 tau_2) - 1 $$ (7)

When the three delays are equal, $K^* = 8$ (the delays are here supposed greater than nothing, and $K$ not less than nothing). When one delay is far less than the other two, $K^*$ is very great, which is the case of Section 4. The more unequal the delays, the wider the bound; the more nearly equal, the narrower.

The good of the return is that the deviation the tide leaves behind is made smaller. While $w$ is constant, and (6) holds, the deviation comes at length to rest at

$$ y = w / (1 + K) $$ (8)

The stronger the return, the smaller the deviation left; but the stronger it is, the nearer the bound; near the bound the swinging dies away slowly, and beyond it the output swings ever wider. Between these two lies what the craftsmen call “just right”.

**6. The period at the boundary, and how to measure the delays**

Upon the boundary the deviation swings to and fro, neither growing nor dying, with the period

$$ P = 2 pi sqrt(a / c) $$ (9)

Near the boundary the period of the swinging is not far from this; farther from it, both the period and how much of each swing remains after the next must be found from the complex roots of (5), and where there are no complex roots there is no swinging.

A craftsman who would use this condition must first measure the three delays. It may be done thus, the return being first cut off and the other parts held fast, so that each part is measured by itself. Change the intake suddenly by a little, and note how long the output takes to cover sixty-three parts in a hundred of its whole change, that is, one less the reciprocal of $e$: that is $tau_0$. Let the output change suddenly by a little, and note how long the reading takes to cover the same part: that is $tau_1$. Hold the reading, change it suddenly by a little, and note how long the intake takes to cover the same part: that is $tau_2$. The strength $K$ is measured besides at rest: hold the reading, change it by a little, and note by how much the intake changes in the end. Reckon $K^*$ by (7), and make the strength of the return about one half of it; there is then room to spare.

A craftsman standing by a pivot is himself a kind of return. He sees the output, considers, and then changes the intake with his hand, and each of these three steps has its delay. The old craftsmen say “better slow than violent”: a violent craftsman adjusts too far, and the supply comes now strong and now weak, which is just what Severin described in his Fourteenth Question. The condition of this paper agrees with the old saying: whether the return is a man’s hand or a return-pattern, if its strength passes the bound the output swings ever wider. Why a man’s hand should be harder to keep within the bound than a pattern, I cannot say, for this paper has written no equation for a man’s hand.

**7. Comparison with the trial benches**

Table I gives the records of six trial benches. The two of Tallin were made in the workshop of Arundel, and are one pivot, the return-pattern of the second being cut deeper; the two of Kanaich are two pivots made by craftsmen of Kanaich; the two of White Tower were made by the workshop of White Tower University for this paper, and are again one pivot with the strength of the return changed [10]. The three delays were measured by the method of the last section, the error of measurement being within about a tenth at each bench; $K$ was measured at rest by the method of the last section, and differed from what the carving gives by less than one part in twenty. At every bench the push applied was two parts in a hundred of the set output and the load six tenths of the full load, and each trial was made three times; the table gives the middle of the three. The reckoned columns are found from (7) and from the roots of (5). The swinging is counted as at rest when its amplitude has fallen below a twentieth of its first.

**8. The accumulating part**

By (8), a return always leaves a deviation $w / (1 + K)$. To remove this too, a further part may be added to the return: so long as a deviation remains, it is accumulated, and the intake is changed further according to what has been accumulated. I call this the accumulating part. Equation (3) becomes

$$ tau_2 (d u)/(d t) + u = -K z - K/T integral z d t $$ (10)

where $T$ is the time of accumulation; the smaller $T$, the faster the accumulation. For a constant push, so long as (12) below holds and the intake the compensation calls for lies always within the capacity, the deviation now comes at length to nothing; if the tide changes slowly, the deviation only follows the change, and cannot be said to be wholly removed. The number $n$ must satisfy

$$ a n^4 + b n^3 + c n^2 + (1 + K) n + K/T = 0 $$ (11)

Seeking the boundary as in Section 5, by putting $n = omega i$, we find from the imaginary part $omega^2 = (1 + K) / b$, and from the real part $a omega^4 - c omega^2 + K/T = 0$. When $T$ is very great, the added root is about $-K / (T (1 + K))$ and the other three are the roots of (5); so long as (6) holds, then, the motion is steady when $T$ is great, and as $T$ is made smaller the first crossing of the imaginary axis falls exactly where the following becomes an equality. The condition of steadiness, $K$ and $T$ being greater than nothing, thus consists of two parts: first, that (6) still hold; secondly, that

$$ (b c - a (1 + K))(1 + K) > b^2 K / T $$ (12)

that is, the accumulation may not be too fast, and $T$ must exceed $b^2 K / ((b c - a(1 + K))(1 + K))$. When the three delays are each five seconds and $K = 4$, $T$ must exceed nine seconds. If the accumulation is too fast, it presses past before the deviation has come back, and the output swings ever wider; if it is slow, the deviation is slow to come back to nothing. The fourth line of Fig. 1 is the case $K = 4$, $T = 30$ seconds, in which the deviation comes at length to nothing. The accumulating part has not yet been tried upon a bench; what is said of it here is reckoning only.

**9. Two regulations together**

When Oldham made trial of the joint tuning of the mana net in a quarter of Kanaich, the regulation of the arrays and the regulation of the engines for a time worked against each other, and the arrays swung to and fro; it was afterwards arranged in layers, the arrays first levelling themselves where they stood and the engines then adjusting the whole output slowly, and so it became steady [7]. Two regulations acting upon the same thing may each lie within its own bound, and the two together pass beyond it. The simplest case is this: if two returns read the same output and move alike, together they are one return of strength $K_1 + K_2$; $K_1$ and $K_2$ may each be less than $K^*$, and their sum greater. Where the speeds differ, or they act upon different places, the equations of their joining must be written out, and this paper does not treat them. When the regulations were arranged in layers, the arrays came to rest first and the engines were then adjusted slowly; the reason is akin to that of Section 8, that the accumulation must be slow, but the joining is different, and (12) cannot be carried over to it: why Oldham’s layers are steady remains to be reckoned. So also with parallel pivots. Brandon carved a pattern of even flow between parallel pivots, so that they share the load in proportion [6]. If the pivots are alike and move alike, and the returns of all read the same whole output, the strength of them together is the sum of their strengths, and must be fixed for that sum. The general case wants its own equations; and that the whole output does not swing does not mean that no pivot swings, for the pivots may swing one against another, one rising as another falls, and it is just this that the pattern of even flow governs.

**10. What the reckoning does not reach**

This paper asks only after the motion when the deviations are small and the return does not exceed the capacity of the pivot. If the tide is too violent and the return calls for more intake than the capacity, the intake stands at its capacity, and the equations above no longer hold. While it so stands the return is in effect weakened, but the accumulating part goes on accumulating, and when the intake leaves the bound what has been accumulated may be too much. I guess that it is then as if the delays were lengthened; but this is a guess only, and what then follows I cannot yet reckon.

For the equation of the third degree the condition of steadiness can be judged from the coefficients alone, namely (6); for the equation (11) of the fourth degree it can be found by the method of Section 5. For equations of higher degree the boundary may be found in the same way, but each equation must be taken by itself; a general method by which one may judge from the coefficients alone whether every root has its real part negative I have not found. When several regulations and several pivots are joined together, the degree of the equation soon grows high. This I leave to the reckoners.

The return-pattern is carved at the intake of the pivot; how it is carved this paper does not treat. As things now stand, it must be carved anew for each kind of engine.
