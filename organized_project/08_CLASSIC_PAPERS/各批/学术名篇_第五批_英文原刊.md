# 《联盟学术名篇》英文原刊（第五批）

## The Ledger of the Turning of the Elements

**THE LEDGER OF THE TURNING OF THE ELEMENTS**

By Finch, of Onais.

[Communicated to the Silver Society, C.R. 981.]

Severin’s Twelfth Question asks: “When the four elements are turned one into another, what is it that does not change?” Every turning, he said, has its loss, and we do not know where the loss goes. For more than a hundred and fifty years the workshops of the cities have kept accounts of turnings without number, and not one of them agrees: some turnings seem to lose mana, and some workshops say they have made furnaces that bring forth mana of themselves. At Onais I have kept the accounts of turnings for thirty years: for the first eight in the old manner, and they did not agree either; afterwards I built an array chamber, and in twenty-two years made in it three hundred and eighty-six turnings, and kept the whole account of every one. I shall show three things in this paper.

First, the accounts do not agree for two reasons: in a turning some part of the mana disperses into what lies about, and has never been gathered back and measured; and those who keep the accounts take the reading of the mana meter for the whole, whereas the meter reads the density at one place, not how much was exchanged in a turning. Secondly, if in a sealed array chamber the dispersed mana is gathered back, if all mana to be entered is received into crystals of one kind and measured by discharging them through one apparatus, and if the gathering ward’s own use and its leakage are measured first, then in all three hundred and eighty-six turnings the receipts and the payments agree within the error of measurement. Thirdly, the mana that the “ever-burning furnace” of Parin gives over and above its fuel comes from the crystal of its lining; when the lining is first discharged, the excess is gone.

**I. WHY THE ACCOUNTS DO NOT AGREE**

At first I kept the account in the old manner of the workshops: before the turning I measured the elements and the storing crystals that drive it; after it, the element obtained; and what had dispersed about I estimated from a mana meter hung in the middle of the room, read once before the turning and once after: the divisions it had risen were multiplied by the size of the room, and turned into a sum of exchange by the custom of the workshops. In this manner I kept the accounts for eight years, a hundred and six turnings, and the receipts always exceeded the payments, by some six in a hundred on the mean, and the excess was now great and now small.

Afterwards I understood that the meter cannot be so used. Fanshawe’s meter reads the density of the free mana where its needle hangs. The dispersed mana does not stay evenly in the room: some runs along the walls, some passes through them, some after a while spreads elsewhere. What runs along the walls or passes through them the needle never reads, and it was left out of the account, so that the receipts were always the greater; and how dense the mana is at that moment and at that place depends on how fast it spreads, so that the excess was now great and now small. That the density has risen by so many divisions tells how dense it is at that moment and at that place, not how much this turning has dispersed in all. What is to be entered is the whole amount exchanged in a turning, and for that all the dispersed mana must be gathered back, and received into something that can be discharged and measured.

**II. THE ARRAY CHAMBER**

In 958 I built an array chamber in the north of the city of Onais, twelve feet square and ten feet high, with wards for gathering dispersed mana laid upon the four walls, the ceiling and the floor. The wards lead the free mana dispersed in the chamber into the troughs of crystal upon the walls. The mana meter within hangs upon a stand that can be moved, and at every turning it is read at nine places of the room in order. Before a turning the free mana in the room is first gathered down until all nine places stand at a fixed reading, differing by no more than half a division, and after it is gathered back to the same readings, so that the room holds as much free mana before as after. The meter without hangs outside the door, and is first read for half an hour to fix its background; if during a turning it rises a division or more above the background, the ward is broken and mana has dispersed out of the room, and that turning is void, whether its account agrees or not. In twenty-two years seventeen turnings were made void, and they are not among the three hundred and eighty-six. While a turning is made, no casting is allowed within thirty paces of the chamber, lest the readings be drawn aside.

Whether the body of the chamber, the vessels and the members of the ward store up mana, I also tried: with no turning, a fixed amount of mana was let go into the room and gathered back ten times over, and what was gathered back did not grow or diminish with the number of times; and after every turning fresh crystals are set in the troughs, and the ward, fed now by a feeding crystal, goes on gathering for two hours more, and they receive nothing. The difference between what the body of the chamber, the vessels and the members of the ward hold before and after is therefore within the error of measurement, and is entered as nought.

All mana to be entered is received into crystals of one specification, not yet spent: from one vein, and of one cut, each holding some eight units when full. To measure it, the crystal is joined to a discharging apparatus and discharged entirely, and the whole amount discharged is entered; what is more than one crystal will hold is divided among several, each discharged in its turn, and the readings added. A crystal so discharged can hold no mana again, and is cast away, so that every measurement uses up several crystals. A new crystal holds a little mana of its own, and this must be taken off: from every batch ten crystals are first discharged without receiving anything, and the mean of what they give is what each holds of itself; the ten differ among themselves by no more than 0.02 unit.

The apparatus drives a lifting-wheel, which raises a standard weight; the whole height raised is the amount discharged. The apparatus has its own waste, and I have tried that it is in proportion to what is discharged: one portion of mana discharged from one crystal, and the same portion divided between two and discharged one after the other, give readings that differ within the error; discharging fast or slow gives the same reading; and each starting and stopping wastes besides some 0.01 unit, far below the error of a reading. Every portion of mana being discharged through the same apparatus, the waste is the same proportion of each, and in comparing receipts with payments it cancels.

For the unit I take a set of standard crystals kept by the Silver Society: twelve from one vein, filled at a fixed warmth of the room and by a fixed manner of receiving, and discharged through this apparatus down to a fixed floor; the mean of what the twelve give I fix as ten units of exchange. The apparatus stops of itself at this floor, and below it a little still remains, so that a standard crystal is never spent, and after use is filled again in the fixed manner. Every month I check the apparatus again with three of them, discharging each once; and the mean of each crystal’s readings in the first ten years differs from the mean in the last ten by no more than four parts in a thousand. This is the steadiness of the apparatus and the crystals together: were both to change alike, I could not tell it, of which more in Section VII. The error of one discharge is about a tenth of a unit.

**III. THE BLANK TRIALS**

Before the turnings proper I made blank trials: in the chamber no turning was made, and only the gathering ward was kept at work, to see how much it uses of itself and how much it fails to gather.

First, its own use. The working of the ward itself takes mana, which in ordinary use it draws from the mana gathered into the troughs upon the walls, so that what is stored in the troughs is already less this portion. What the ward uses is spent upon its own working, and does not return into the room: with no mana let go, and the ward fed by a feeding crystal of its own, six hours of ordinary working put nothing into the troughs. In the blank trials the ward was fed in this way, and what the feeding crystal gave was measured: in forty trials, on the mean 0.42 unit an hour, the rate of the several trials differing by less than 0.03; and while twenty, forty or sixty units were let go into the room and the ward was busy gathering them, the rate still lay between 0.40 and 0.44. The error of the mean of the forty is some 0.005 unit an hour.

Secondly, its leakage. Ten crystals of one batch were filled in the fixed manner; five were discharged and measured, to fix what each holds, the five differing among themselves by no more than the error of reading; the mana held in the other five was released into the chamber until they would give no more, with no turning, and gathered back by the ward and measured; joined afterwards to the apparatus, the five gave nothing more. The ward was then still fed by its own crystal, and drew nothing from the troughs, so that what was gathered back contains none of its own use. Of 40.00 units let go, 39.28 were gathered back on the mean, a leakage of 1.8 in a hundred; in eight trials, letting go from twenty to sixty units, in the middle of the room, in its corners and near the ceiling, the mean of the eight was likewise 1.8 in a hundred, and no one of them strayed from it by more than the error of reading. The portion lost is scattered out of doors, far less than a division, and the meter without cannot read it.

Both of these must be entered among the payments: the ward’s own use is taken out of the mana gathered back, and the leakage is never gathered back at all; if they are not entered, the receipts will seem the greater. In the hundred and six turnings of the first eight years the dispersed mana was estimated from the meter, and these items cannot be made up; those turnings are therefore not entered in the table below, and are shown in Fig. 2 for comparison only.

**IV. THE RULES OF THE LEDGER**

From then on, every turning was entered by the following rules.

Receipts: the mana contained in the elements before the turning; the mana given by the storing crystals that drive the turning. The mana contained in an element is measured by taking three other portions of the same batch and the same weight, turning them back into free mana in the chamber by a working for the dispersing of elements, and gathering it by the ward; the mean of the three is taken, and they seldom differ by more than half a unit. This I call the method of dispersal. The working of dispersal is itself driven by storing crystals, and what drives it disperses into the room likewise and is gathered with the rest, so that what these crystals give must be taken off; and the residue, dispersed a second time, yields nothing, which shows that the dispersal was complete. What the storing crystals give is found as a difference: what they held before the turning is fixed by five filled crystals of the same batch, and what remains after it is discharged and measured. The turnings are all driven by storing crystals, and never by the caster’s own mana, so that the caster shall not himself be entered in the account.

Payments: the mana contained in the element obtained after the turning, also measured by the method of dispersal; the dispersed mana gathered back into the troughs; the ward’s own use in this turning, reckoned at 0.42 unit an hour; the leakage: what is gathered back, together with the ward’s own use, is ninety-eight and two-tenths in a hundred of what was let go, and the leakage is that sum multiplied by 1.8 and divided by 98.2. What is gathered in the method of dispersal is made up for its own use and leakage in the same way, and only then is what the driving crystals gave taken off.

Since the free mana in the room is the same before and after, and the difference of what the body of the chamber, the vessels and the members of the ward hold is entered as nought, the item of what lies about is nought.

Here is the account of one turning of water into air, in units of exchange.

Five things were measured in this turning: the portions of water, what the storing crystals held before by their fellows of the batch and what remained after, the portions of air, and the dispersed mana gathered back. Each has an error of about a tenth of a unit, greater where several crystals must be filled; this is the common scatter of many repeated measurements, not a bound. The five being independent, together they come to about a quarter of a unit. The ward’s own use and the leakage, reckoned by the proportions of the blank trials, bring an error of less than 0.03. 0.16 lies within it.

**V. THREE HUNDRED AND EIGHTY-SIX TURNINGS**

From 958, when the chamber was finished and the blank trials made, to 980, I made by these rules three hundred and eighty-six turnings. Their workings, vessels and quantities were various, some two hundred and forty manners in all; by their elements they fall into twelve kinds, set out in Table I. The four elements are turned each into each, in both directions. All the turnings were of the ninth ring or lower.

Of the three hundred and eighty-six turnings, a hundred and ninety-four differ on the side of the receipts, a hundred and eighty-five on the side of the payments, and seven not at all; only three differ by more than three-quarters of a unit, where such a spread should give some two, and all three are entered as they were found. That is to say, within the error estimated, no kind of turning shows a constant gain or loss: there is none that always gives over, and none that always falls short.

**VI. THE EVER-BURNING FURNACE OF PARIN**

In 978 a workshop of alchemy at Parin made a furnace of which it said that, given one portion of fuel, it burned longer than that fuel should burn, and gave more mana than the fuel held; they called it the ever-burning furnace. Were this so, there would be nothing unchanging for the Twelfth Question to find. The workshop lent me one, and I kept its account in the chamber as usual.

At a fixed strength of fire the furnace burned one portion of fuel for forty hours before it went out, and gave 186.4 units; this is what was gathered back into the troughs, made up for the ward’s own use in forty hours, 16.8 units, and for the leakage. The fuel, by the method of dispersal, held 151.8 units, so that 34.6 units were given over. The workshop said that the excess was what the furnace brought forth. But the lining of the hearth is a layer of crystal of the elements. The workshop had made four linings in one batch, one of them in the furnace; I discharged the other three and measured them: they held 35.6, 35.9 and 36.2 units, on the mean 35.9; after the burning, the lining in the furnace held only 1.2. The lining had lost 34.7 units, which agrees with the 34.6 given over: what the furnace gave had to be divided among some twenty crystals to be measured, and made up for forty hours of the ward’s own use, the fuel too had to be divided among many crystals by the method of dispersal, and the lining could be measured only by its fellows of the batch, so that each of the two numbers has an error of about half a unit, and their difference of 0.1 lies well within it.

I then asked the workshop to put in a lining discharged beforehand, and to burn the same portion of fuel at the same strength of fire. This time the furnace went out at thirty-two hours and a half, and gave 151.5 units, 0.3 less than the fuel, within the error of this measurement, which is about half a unit, and not the least part over. The excess, then, was given out by the lining: while the furnace burned, the lining gave out its store little by little and fed the fire together with the fuel, so that the same portion of fuel burned seven hours and a half the longer. When the lining had come down to 1.2 units the furnace went out; that remainder gives itself out too slowly, I suppose, to feed the same strength of fire, but I measured only before and after the burning, and did not see whether this is what put the fire out. The 34.6 units had been stored in the lining; the furnace did not bring them forth.

**VII. CONCLUSION**

From the sections above I conclude: in the turnings that I have tried, if the elements, the media that store mana, what lies about and the dispersal are entered together, the ledger of mana before and after the turning agrees within the error of measurement. The loss does not vanish; it disperses into what lies about, and can be gathered back and measured.

From this one thing more may be said, gathered from the turnings that I have tried and from this one furnace, and not proved: no instrument can bring forth mana out of nothing. A furnace, a crystal, an array can only move mana from one place to another and turn it from one form into another; whenever it is said of an instrument that it gives more than it receives, one should look first for a store that has not been entered, or for a supply from without that has not been entered.

There are some things I could not do. First, why the ledger always agrees I cannot say; I know only that in three hundred and eighty-six turnings there has not been one exception. Secondly, turnings of the tenth ring and higher are too dangerous, and I have not tried them; nor do I enter the caster’s own mana, so that how the elemental pool of a caster gives and takes in a turning this paper does not treat. Thirdly, the unit of exchange hangs upon the Society’s set of standard crystals and upon this one apparatus; should both one day change alike, the unit changes with them, and I could not tell it. I ask the Society to seal the standard crystals, and once a year to compare them with a few copies.

## Nomological Algebra

**NOMOLOGICAL ALGEBRA**

By Elworthy, of the White Tower Society.

[Communicated to the White Tower Society, C.R. 1061.]

Severin’s Sixth Question asks: “Can the lines of law be written in a strict reckoning?” When two law-mages speak of a line, he said, they must often gesture at length, and when they have done they may not mean the same line. More than two hundred years have passed, and the case is not much altered: the same line is written in one manner and another in the manuscripts of those who have grasped it; and the craftsmen, in building their arrays and wards, rely upon the rules of experience in Pendleton’s Laws of the Craftsmen, rules which tell what to do but not why, so that where the book is silent nothing remains but to try. In forty years, upon the many scattered manners of writing left by those before me, I have settled a manner of writing and of reckoning, which I call nomological algebra. I shall show four things in this paper.

First, every elementary line of law is written as a sign; lines are combined in two ways only, by joint application, in which they are applied together, and by successive application, in which they are applied one after the other; these keep a few simple rules, and successive application in general depends upon the order. Secondly, the two lines of the old wards, the warp and the weft, applied in succession, bring forth a third line; only the three together are complete in themselves, and from this it may be explained why the old wards tear where the load is gathered, and the weave that mends it may be derived. Thirdly, whether several lines can be jointly applied may be decided by a second reckoning. Fourthly, when the supply from without, the exchange with what lies about and the dispersal are all entered in the account, lines that keep the whole amount, however they are combined, leave the whole amount the same before and after; and why the ledger of Finch, eighty years ago, still agrees under combination may be shown from this. Lastly I set out three trials.

**I. THE SIGNS**

The condition of the Web of Laws at a place I call a web-state, and write $w$. A line of law applied to the web changes one web-state into another, so that a line is an operation upon web-states, and I write it with a capital letter. The result of applying $X$ to the web-state $w$ is written $X w$.

Two web-states may be laid together at one place, which I write as adding them, $w + v$; and a web-state may be enlarged, $a w$ being the web-state with its rising and falling enlarged $a$ times. An operation applied to web-states added together acts upon them term by term: $X (w + v) = X w + X v$, and $X (a w) = a (X w)$. These two are general rules, and no line that I have measured has failed of them.

The operation that changes nothing is called the identity operation, and written $1$: $1 w = w$. When an operation yields nothing at all, not even a web-state, I say that it yields the null, and write $0$. The null is not a web-state: the web at rest, holding no mana, is still a web-state, and is not the null. Any operation applied to the null yields the null; the null added to a web-state yields that web-state; and a web-state multiplied by a number yields the null only if the number is nought. The measure in which a line is applied is written by a number: $a X$ is $X$ applied $a$ times over; the measures are common numbers, positive or negative, whole or broken. $-X$ is the reverse of $X$, whose rising and falling is just contrary to that of $X$; a negative measure only applies the line the contrary way, and is no negative mana.

**II. JOINT AND SUCCESSIVE APPLICATION**

When two lines are applied together, I call it joint application, and write $X + Y$; when $Y$ is applied first and $X$ after, I call it successive application, and write $X Y$. Of the web-state obtained, joint application gives the sum of what the two give, and successive application hands what $Y$ gives to $X$:

$$ (X + Y) w = X w + Y w, quad (X Y) w = X (Y w) $$ (1)

(1) defines the two manners of application. From it and the two general rules of Section I the following rules may be derived:

$$ X + Y = Y + X, quad (X + Y) + Z = X + (Y + Z) $$ (2)

$$ (X Y) Z = X (Y Z), quad X (Y + Z) = X Y + X Z, quad (Y + Z) X = Y X + Z X $$ (3)

$$ a (X Y) = (a X) Y = X (a Y), quad 1 X = X 1 = X, quad X + (-X) = 0 $$ (4)

(2) says that joint application does not depend on the order; (3) that successive application may be grouped as one pleases, and is distributed over joint application; (4) that the measures may be moved before or after, that the identity operation changes nothing, and that a line applied jointly with its reverse cancels its rising and falling and yields the null. When these had been derived, I had the law-mages of White Tower test each of them by casting, and not once has one failed.

Wherever a joint application stands in an equation, the lines in it must all be applicable together, at one place and under the same conditions, before either side has a meaning; which lines can be applied together is reckoned apart, in Section V. An equation of successive application alone is under no such restriction.

One thing only is unlike the reckoning of number: successive application in general depends upon the order, and $X Y$ and $Y X$ are often not the same. The law-mages have long known that two workings applied in the other order give another result; but without a manner of writing they could not say wherein the result differs, or by how much. The difference of the two orders I write

$$ [X, Y] = X Y - Y X $$ (5)

and call the difference of reversal. It compares what the two orders give; it does not mean that both are applied at once. When the difference of reversal of two lines is the null, I say that they may be interchanged.

**III. THE WARP, THE WEFT AND THE THIRD LINE**

The old wards are all woven of two lines, which the craftsmen call the warp and the weft. I write the warp $A$ and the weft $B$. Of all the work of forty years, the successive application of $A$ and $B$ cost the most labour.

When $B$ is applied first and $A$ after, there arises at that place of the web a kind of twist, whose direction is neither that of the warp nor that of the weft; when $A$ is applied first and $B$ after, the twist is just contrary. This twist can also be applied by itself, and is another line, which I call the bias, and write $C$. Measured thus by casting, one by one, the successive applications of the three lines make up Table I.

Table I. The successive application of the warp, the weft and the bias (rows applied after, columns applied first)

| After \ First | $A$ (warp) | $B$ (weft) | $C$ (bias) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $A$ (warp) | $-1$ | $C$ | $-B$ |
| $B$ (weft) | $-C$ | $-1$ | $A$ |
| $C$ (bias) | $B$ | $-A$ | $-1$ |

The $-1$ in the table is the reverse of the identity operation: when the same line is applied twice in succession, the web-state is turned over, its rising and falling just contrary to what it was; applying $A$ twice, $w$ becomes $-w$. And $-w$ is still a web-state, neither the null nor any negative mana.

From Table I, the differences of reversal of the three lines are

$$ [A, B] = 2 C, quad [B, C] = 2 A, quad [C, A] = 2 B $$ (6)

Any two of the lines applied in succession give again a joint application of $1$, $A$, $B$ and $C$, each in its own measure. Whatever operation can be written $a + b A + c B + d C$ gives, under joint and successive application, what can again be so written, so that this family is complete in itself. The warp and the weft alone make no such family: $A$ and $B$ applied in succession must bring forth $C$.

From this it may be explained why the old wards tear. An old ward bears its load always in the same order: the load as it falls is taken first by the warp, and the warp hands it on to the weft. When the load is gathered at one place of a ward, the web there has the warp applied first and the weft after, which by Table I is $B A = -C$, and a twist arises contrary to the bias. In an old ward there is no line of bias, and this portion has nowhere to go, but gathers at that place; when enough has gathered, the ward tears there. Many of the wards that Pendleton recorded tore just where the load was gathered. If a line of bias is woven in besides the warp and the weft, the bias bears a twist in either direction, and the portion spreads along the lines of bias over the whole ward. This much is my explanation: Table I says only that the warp and the weft in succession bring forth a portion of the bias; how that portion gathers and how it spreads the reckoning does not contain, and I have conjectured it from the places where Pendleton’s wards tore. But it yields a prediction that can be tried, for which see the third trial of Section VII. That the three sets of lines cross at sixty degrees is so that at every place all three cross equally close; this is the craftsmen’s rule of weaving, and is not derived from the algebra.

**IV. THE DUAL AND THE STRENGTH**

The dual of an operation $X$, written $macron(X)$, does what $X$ does in the contrary way. The dual keeps these rules: the dual of a joint application is the joint application of the duals; the dual of a measure is that same measure, so that the identity operation and its multiples are their own duals; the dual of a successive application is the successive application of the duals in the reverse order, $macron(X Y) = macron(Y) macron(X)$; and the dual of the dual is the operation itself. The dual is not the reverse: the reverse of twice the identity operation is twice its reverse, but its dual is twice the identity operation still.

The lines I have measured keep one general rule: an operation and its dual applied in succession, in whichever order, yield what is never negative; that is, if what they yield merely multiplies some web-state by a number, that number is never less than nought.

The duals of $A$, $B$ and $C$ are their reverses. For $X = a + b A + c B + d C$ (where $a$ stands for the identity operation in the measure $a$),

$$ macron(X) = a - b A - c B - d C, quad macron(X) X = X macron(X) = a^2 + b^2 + c^2 + d^2 $$ (7)

What is obtained is a number only, multiplying the identity operation, and not less than nought; an operation of this family and its dual applied in succession, in whichever order, give this number. I call it the strength of $X$, and write it $|X|^2$. When $X$ and $Y$ both belong to this family,

$$ |X Y|^2 = |X|^2 |Y|^2 $$ (8)

The proof needs, besides (3), (4) and (7), only the rule that the dual reverses the order: $macron(X Y) X Y = macron(Y) macron(X) X Y = |X|^2 macron(Y) Y = |X|^2 |Y|^2$, the middle step because $|X|^2$ is a number, and may be moved before or after. When two operations are applied in succession, the strength is the product of the two strengths. This the craftsmen can use: how strong a compound line is need not be found by casting it, but can be reckoned from the lines that compose it. It holds only within the family of the warp, the weft and the bias; raising and lowering are not of this family, and it may not be carried over to them.

**V. COMPATIBLE AND INCOMPATIBLE LINES**

Joint application requires that two lines can be applied at one place together. Some cannot: no law-mage can make a cold fire, and upon one and the same fire the lines of fire and of cold, however they are woven, admit no construction that satisfies the conditions of both.

First the constructions to be considered are fixed: all that are possible with the given instrument, material, supply and object of the working. The condition of a line is those of these constructions upon which the line can be applied, and the material, the supply and the object must all be written into it. The condition of an elementary line is found by casting, one construction after another; that of a compound line is the product of the conditions of the lines that compose it. I write a condition by a small letter: the condition of fire $f$, of cold $g$. That both conditions are to be satisfied I write $f g$, which is the constructions common to both; a condition said twice is the same condition:

$$ f f = f, quad f g = g f $$ (9)

A condition that no construction satisfies I write $0$; that within which every construction lies, $1$. These $0$ and $1$ are those of the conditions, and not the null and the identity operation of Section I. “Not satisfying $f$” I write $1 - f$, and then

$$ f (1 - f) = 0 $$ (10)

no construction can both satisfy a condition and not satisfy it. The conditions having been written whole, two lines are compatible if, and only if, the product of their conditions is not $0$; and so for three or more, the product of all their conditions must not be $0$. Whether the supply will serve compatible lines all together is reckoned apart, by the account of Section VI. Fire and cold cannot be jointly applied, because

$$ f g = 0 $$ (11)

This reckoning is another thing from that of joint and successive application above: that one reckons how lines act upon web-states, this one whether several lines can act together. Its use is that it can reckon what words cannot make plain. For instance, three lines may each be jointly applied with each, and yet not all three together: $p q$, $q r$ and $p r$ are none of them $0$, but $p q r$ is $0$. To take a small example: let there be only three constructions, the first, the second and the third; let $p$ be satisfied by the first and second, $q$ by the second and third, and $r$ by the first and third; any two have one construction in common, but all three have none. Pendleton recorded three such overlayings, in which two layers did well and the third spoiled all, and he could only write “not to be laid three deep”. I applied the line of each layer in these three by itself upon the same kind of instrument, and found its condition so, without regard to how the overlaying had fared; multiplied by (9) to (11), in all three the products two by two are not $0$, and the product of all three is $0$. The conditions and the reckoning of each case are kept by the White Tower Society.

**VI. THE WHOLE AMOUNT UNCHANGED**

Eighty years ago Finch showed that in the turning of the elements, if the elements, the media that store mana, what lies about and the dispersal are entered together, the ledger of mana agrees always within the error of measurement. The combination of lines of law has its ledger also, and it may be written in the algebra.

The mana contained in a web-state is measured by an operation, which I call the amount, and write $Q$: if $Q w = q w$, the amount of $w$ is said to be the definite number $q$. The operation that raises one share of mana from the depths of Tiamat up into the web is called raising, and written $U$; that which sends one share back into the depths, lowering, and written $D$. Each application of raising adds one share to the amount at that place, and each application of lowering takes one away. The share here is the portion of exchange taken by the usage of reading; how much it is I cannot measure, and know only that it is far below one division of the mana meter; it is not a least quantity that has been proved. Raising and lowering are dual to each other, and by the general rule of Section IV, applied in succession, in whichever order, they yield what is never negative. So far as I can measure, raising-then-lowering and lowering-then-raising do not differ: the one order gives the same as the other.

A line that may be interchanged with the amount, $Q X = X Q$, I call a line that keeps the amount: applied to a web-state of definite amount $q$, it yields, unless it yields the null, a web-state whose amount is still $q$, since $Q (X w) = X (Q w) = q (X w)$. Conversely, a line that leaves unchanged the amount of every web-state of definite amount may be interchanged with $Q$, since every web-state that I have measured can be taken apart into several such web-states added together. Lines that keep the amount have one property of consequence:

$$ Q X = X Q, quad Q Y = Y Q $$

$$ arrow.r.double quad Q (X + Y) = (X + Y) Q, quad Q (X Y) = (X Y) Q $$ (12)

Lines that keep the amount, however they are applied jointly and successively, give again what keeps it. The proof needs only (1) to (3): $Q (X Y) = (Q X) Y = (X Q) Y = X (Q Y) = X (Y Q) = (X Y) Q$; and so for joint application. A combination must of course be applicable, and must yield something other than the null, before there is any amount for it to keep; (12) says that every combination that can be written keeps the amount, not that every one that can be written can be applied.

Looking at one place of the web alone, many lines do not keep the amount: raising draws mana up from the depths, and dispersal sends it out beyond the bounds. But if the supply from without, the exchange with what lies about and the dispersal are all entered in the account, the account holds. It has five items: first, the amount upon the web at that place; secondly, what the feeding crystal still holds; thirdly, the item of the depths, in which the shares raised from the depths and not yet lowered back are entered as paid out, with the negative sign; fourthly, what has passed into what lies about; fifthly, the item of dispersal, in which what has been dispersed beyond the bounds is entered as it accumulates. When one share is raised, the web has one share more and the item of the depths one less; when the feeding crystal gives one share, the crystal has one less and the web one more; when one share is dispersed, the web has one less and the item of dispersal one more. The operation that measures the whole of the five items together I write $T$, to keep it apart from $Q$, which measures one place alone; the web-state is then taken to hold the other four items also. Every elementary line that I have measured may be interchanged with $T$. With $T$ in place of $Q$, (12) holds as before, so that all the arrays and wards combined from these lines, so far as they can be applied, keep this whole amount also. This agrees with the ledger of Finch: when the supply from without, the exchange with what lies about and the dispersal are entered together, the whole mana is the same before and after the combination.

Each elementary line that I have measured I have entered by Finch’s rules, and its receipts and payments differ within the error of measurement; the account of each line is kept by the White Tower Society. From this it may be seen also that the ledger still agrees under combination by no chance: so long as every elementary line keeps the whole amount, every combination however intricate keeps it, and there is no need to measure them one by one. Why the elementary lines keep the whole amount I cannot say; Finch said that he could not tell why the ledger always agrees, and here the question has only been carried back to the elementary lines.

**VII. THREE TRIALS**

A manner of writing is not enough because it is orderly; it must reckon what the casters can measure. I have made three trials.

First, reckoning against casting. After Table I was fixed, I reckoned beforehand by the rules above what two hundred and sixteen combinations should give, and wrote the reckonings on sealed sheets; then I asked twelve law-mages of White Tower to test them by casting. Each combination was cast once by each of two law-mages working apart, so that each of the twelve cast thirty-six times; those casting did not see the reckoning, and the sheets were opened only when the casting was done. The fifty-four combinations of the first kind are new measures and orders beyond Table I, and not the old records from which the table was fixed. A result was counted as agreeing in this way: for successive application, the direction of the rising and falling and the measure of each term; for strength, the number measured; for joint application, whether it could be applied or not; each the same within what the casting and the instruments could distinguish. The results are in Table II; all agree, which is to say that within the precision used no difference was seen. The sheets and the records of casting are kept by the White Tower Society.

Thirdly, the weave of three lines. If the conjecture of Section III is right, a ward of three lines should not tear under a prescribed load, duration and disturbance. This no one had said before, and it can be tried. I asked the craftsmen of White Tower to weave, of one batch of materials and in like frames, twenty-four trial pieces six feet square. Twelve were woven of three lines: the warp and the weft at the common spacing, with a set of lines of bias added. Twelve were woven of two lines in the old manner: six at the common spacing, and six with the warp and the weft drawn closer, until the whole length engraved was the same as in the weave of three, to see whether more lines of the old kind alone would serve as well as the bias. The supply of each piece was given in proportion to the whole length engraved. The working load that the craftsmen allow to the common weave of two lines I count as once, and both kinds were reckoned by it. The trial laid a gathered load upon the centre of each piece by an array, beginning at once the working load and adding a tenth of it evenly in every quarter of an hour, with a disturbance of the same size added at the same place from first to last, until the piece tore, or the load reached three times; at three times it was held for two hours more. **VIII. WHAT CANNOT BE WRITTEN IN THE RECKONING**

Nomological algebra is not all. The law-mages who hold most by what is grasped in ascending the Tower say that what is written in the reckoning is only the shadow of what is grasped; and this I do not wholly deny: the reckoning keeps how lines are added and applied in succession, but not all that is seen at the moment of grasping. For many workings of the witches I find no reckoning that answers; whether they do not go by the Web of Laws at all, or go by lines I have not written, I do not know. Nor can I say why the lines of law keep these rules; I know only that in forty years they have not once failed to keep them.

I write the manner of writing here in the hope that those who grasp a line may henceforth, when they speak of it, write it down and compare, and need no longer gesture at length.
