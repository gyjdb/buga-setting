# 《联盟学术名篇》英文原刊（第二批）

## The General Theory of Resumption, Discount and Silver Notes

**THE GENERAL THEORY OF RESUMPTION, DISCOUNT AND SILVER NOTES**

By STANHOPE, of the University of Kanaich.

**PREFACE**

This book is addressed to my fellow students of commerce, and also to those who keep the money of the Reserve, of the Treasury and of the counting-houses of the cities. Its argument is not abstruse. If the reader finds it hard going, the reason will seldom be that the new ideas are difficult; it will be that the old ones are rooted so deep. The maxims of the counting-house we heard as children and have used all our lives, and we have never asked whether they hold in more than one case. In writing this book I have spent more labour in putting old notions away than in finding new ones.

For more than sixty years I kept accounts at the Kanaich Branch of the Reserve, and I had three stoppages through my hands. Each time, after the node had been repaired, the stoppage of work in the books dragged on for months; and the city with most work stopped was seldom the city whose supply had failed. The old doctrine could not explain this, and set it down to the people’s alarm. The people were alarmed; but alarm, too, leaves its entries in the books. This book is the result of reading them.

After the stoppage at Tallin in F.E. 234 I obtained, from the registers of the branches and the counterfoils of the acceptance houses, every bill, every wage ticket and every payment falling due before and after the stoppage, and checked them one against another; and I went back over the old accounts of four earlier stoppages and of the Great Tide. These accounts kept me travelling between the branches of the cities for three years. Had the keepers of accounts everywhere not entered every item as a matter of routine, I could not have begun.

When the first draft was done, Glover of the University of Kanaich read the whole of it, and Chapter 10 owes most to him; several colleagues at the branch checked the numbers in the tables for me. The errors are my own.

Whatever in this book can be said with numbers is said with them, and every number in the tables can be checked against the original registers, which are kept at the Kanaich Branch of the Reserve; the manner of consulting them is set out in the appendix. Where numbers fail I have tried to say that I am inferring. The two measures proposed here are for use only when payments have broken down, and not at other times; they cannot take the place of repairing a node, and they do not touch a single share of the city’s reserve of lift.

(Kanaich, the Month of Slumber, F.E. 237.)

**BOOK I. INTRODUCTION**

**CHAPTER 1. THE GENERAL THEORY**

I have called this book a General Theory in order to set it against the old doctrine. The old doctrine treats of a special case, and this book of the general case; and the case the old doctrine supposes happens not to be the case of a stoppage. To meet a stoppage with the old doctrine is to be wrong at every turn, and to do harm.

The old doctrine holds that work stops for one reason only: for want of mana, or for want of materials. On this view, when a city’s supply fails, the only workshops that stop should be those of that city which lack mana, together with those further down the line which therefore lack materials; and when the node is repaired and the mana returns, all the workshops start again, and the stoppage in the books disappears with it.

That is not what the books show. In F.E. 234 the supply of Tallin failed for nineteen days. The workshops of Tallin that stopped for want of mana had all started again within five days of the repair. But in the cities whose supply had not failed, more than two thousand four hundred workshops stopped one after another once the stoppage had begun, though their mana never failed for a day and they wanted no materials; and the last of them did not start again until the following spring. Reckoned by the number of workshops stopped multiplied by the days they stood, the work stopped in this stoppage for want of payment was three times the work stopped for want of mana (Chapter 6). Of this threefold the old doctrine has nothing to say.

These workshops stopped because they were not paid. A workshop that works must first pay out wages and the price of its materials, and wait until its goods are delivered before it is paid for them; how much work it undertakes depends on the payments it expects to receive. The stopped city could not meet the bills falling due, and so its suppliers were not paid; the suppliers, not being paid, could not meet their own wage tickets and bills. Once the chain of payments is broken, those beyond the break must stop, though they have mana, materials and hands. In a tide year everyone fears another stoppage; whoever holds silver notes keeps them idle, the acceptance houses will not discount, and the broken chain is not joined again.

There are, then, two kinds of stopped work: work stopped for want of mana or materials, and work stopped for want of payment. The first is the stoppage itself; the second is the breakdown of payments spreading outwards. The old doctrine sees only the first, and supposes that the second either does not happen or, if it happens, mends of itself. This book will show that the second not only happens but is commonly the larger; that it does not mend of itself; and that it can be remedied.

The old doctrine is not wrong. It is a special case of the general one, the case in which the chain of payments never breaks. In years without a stoppage that case is not far from the truth; in a year of stoppage it is very far from it.

**CHAPTER 2. THE THREE POSTULATES OF THE OLD DOCTRINE**

No single book sets out the old doctrine in full. It is scattered among the sayings of the counting-house, the old maxims of the Reserve and the lectures of the schools of commerce; its fullest statement is in the Lectures on Commerce which the late Hardwick gave at the University of Kanaich for fifty years. Gathered together, it comes to three postulates.

First, work stops only for want of power or materials. A workshop that has mana, materials and hands will work, and how much it works is fixed by how much mana and material it can obtain.

Secondly, goods find their buyers. What a workshop receives for its goods it must lay out in wages, in materials or in other things; what one man pays is what another receives, so that payment never stops half-way. What cannot be paid today will be paid in a few days, and when the stoppage is past the accounts will balance of themselves.

Thirdly, every note its silver. A silver note is a certificate of silver, and behind every note there must be a note’s worth of silver; one note more is a false note. The Reserve therefore cannot issue a single note more to meet the emergency of a stoppage.

The first two postulates are broadly true in years without a stoppage. Payments then follow one upon another, ninety-nine bills in a hundred are met on the day, and the work of a workshop is in truth limited only by its mana, its materials and its hands. The third was reasonable when it was laid down, about C.R. 1240; notes were then used only for large dealings between city and city, they were redeemed in silver on sight, and the only danger of non-payment lay on the road.

This book will show three things. First, that in a stoppage the second postulate fails: payment does stop half-way, and when it stops it stops in a string. Secondly, that once the second fails the first is incomplete: a workshop with mana, materials and hands may stop for want of payment. Thirdly, that the third ties the hands of the Reserve at the very moment when it ought to act: when the chain of payments breaks everyone wants notes, and the Reserve cannot issue one more.

Hardwick too had seen workshops stop in other cities after a stoppage. His explanation, in the Lectures, was that those workshops wanted materials supplied by the stopped city, or were the customers of such workshops, so that if one traced far enough every stopped workshop would be found to want power or materials. The accounts of Chapter 5 were traced in just the way he describes, workshop by workshop; and of the two thousand six hundred and more workshops that stopped in F.E. 234 in the cities whose supply had not failed, only sixty could be traced to a want of materials.

Not everyone who holds the old doctrine holds all three. Some believe the first alone, and think the other two of no account; some believe the third, and see no connexion between it and stopped work. But in the counting-house the three are used together. When a city’s supply fails and the clerk sees workshops stopping in other cities, he says they want materials; when he sees bills falling due unmet, he says they will be met in a few days; and when someone asks the Reserve to act, he says every note its silver. This book goes through the accounts of each in turn.

**CHAPTER 3. THE CHAIN OF PAYMENTS**

Consider first how a workshop decides how much work to undertake. Before it begins it has orders in hand, and an estimate of the season to come: when the goods already ordered will be delivered and when they will be paid for, and how much more it may expect to sell. On these expected receipts it hires so many craftsmen, buys so much material and works so many days. Wages are mostly paid in wage tickets, redeemed when they fall due; materials mostly by bills, met when they fall due by the drawer or by the acceptor. The work a workshop undertakes now is therefore fixed by its expectation of what it will receive later, and mana, materials and hands set only an upper limit. In years without a stoppage expectations are broadly met, work comes near the limit, and the first postulate of the old doctrine appears to be true.

Consider next the chain of payments. What a workshop receives is paid by those who buy from it; what it pays is received by those who sell to it and by its craftsmen. The craftsmen take their wages and pay rent and buy bread; the corn-dealer takes his money and meets his bills. A payment passes from hand to hand like the links of a chain, and the second postulate of the old doctrine amounts to saying that the chain never breaks.

Suppose that at Tallin there is a foundry whose mana comes from the node that has failed. It stands for nineteen days, cannot deliver, is not paid for its goods, and so cannot meet two bills falling due: one for materials, drawn by a crystal-grinder at Kanaich, and one for carriage, drawn by a carrier at Varen. The crystal-grinder at Kanaich has never lacked mana for a day, and meant to meet its own wage tickets out of that payment. The payment does not come, the wage tickets cannot be met, and the craftsmen will take no more tickets for their work. The grinder must stop and wait for its money. That is the second link. The craftsmen of the second link, not being paid, cannot pay their rent or their bread; the corn-dealer, receiving less, meets his own bills late. That is the third.

Most of the stopped work in the books lies at the second link and beyond. The stopping of the first link is for want of mana; the stopping of the second and later links is for want of money. These workshops have mana, materials and hands, and orders too; what they want is a payment that ought to have come on the day.

Where the chain can be joined again, and where it cannot, depends on whether each link has money in hand, or can turn the good bills it holds into money. The crystal-grinder in fact holds bills drawn by others and falling due in two months, and the drawers are sound. In ordinary times it could take them to an acceptance house to be discounted, and meet its wage tickets with the notes. But in a tide year the acceptance houses are themselves afraid; they will not discount, or will discount only at ten in the hundred a year; and the notes are lying idle in people’s houses and are not to be had. A good bill will not fetch a single note, and there the chain breaks.

Whether the chain breaks, then, depends not only on how great the stoppage is, but on whether, after it, those who hold good bills can turn them into money. This is the hinge of the book. Chapter 9 explains why people hoard notes in a tide year, Chapter 10 reckons how far the break travels, and Book IV shows how good bills may be made to fetch money.

**BOOK II. THE ACCOUNTS**

**CHAPTER 5. THE THREE CASES**

In this chapter the accounts of the stoppage at Tallin in F.E. 234 are used to show how the workshops that stopped fall into three cases.

Four kinds of account were used: the supply accounts of the nodes, which record how much mana each workshop received on each day; the accounts of delivery, which record what goods were handed over on what day; the accounts of bills met, kept at the branches and the acceptance houses; and the accounts of wage tickets met, kept by the workshops and the mutual funds. All four are kept under the registered numbers of the workshops, and can be matched workshop by workshop. Every workshop that stopped at any time within ninety days of the beginning of the stoppage was examined.

A workshop that stopped is entered under A, want of power or materials, if on the days it stood the supply account shows no mana, or the account of delivery shows that a necessary material had not come. It is entered under B, power but no money, if mana and materials were both there, but an expected payment had fallen due unpaid before it stopped, and some bill or wage ticket of its own fell due unmet while it stood. It is entered under C, insolvent, if its debts exceeded all its property and everything owing to it, so that its affairs could not be settled in full when they were wound up. Case C can only be decided when the winding-up is complete, and the C of this chapter comprises those wound up by F.E. 236 and found to have been insolvent before the stoppage. Solvency is examined first: workshops under C are counted only by number. The rest are entered under A and B by their days: a workshop that first wanted power and then money has its days of the one entered under A and of the other under B, and is counted among the workshops of whichever case held it longer. A workshop that became insolvent only after the stoppage is still entered under A or B, and is treated elsewhere in the book. Days stopped are counted to the ninetieth day from the beginning of the stoppage; the three hundred and more workshops still stopped on that day stood some eight thousand workshop-days more afterwards, which the tables leave out and Chapter 4 lists apart. Only some dozen workshops fit none of the three, mostly because their craftsmen left of their own accord, and these are not counted.

Table 1. The stoppage at Tallin, F.E. 234: workshops stopped, by case (within ninety days of the beginning of the stoppage).

| Case | Tallin | Other cities | Total | Mean days stopped | Workshop-days |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| A. Want of power or materials | 1,140 | 60 | 1,200 | 18 | 21,700 |
| B. Power but no money | 410 | 2,460 | 2,870 | 23 | 66,000 |
| C. Insolvent | 70 | 90 | 160 | — | — |

Three things may be seen in Table 1. First, the A workshops are nearly all at Tallin; the sixty A workshops in other cities were those that wanted materials supplied from Tallin. Secondly, the B workshops are far more numerous than the A, and mostly in the cities whose supply had not failed; and the four hundred and more B workshops in Tallin itself were drawing upon reserve cores or upon other nodes, and did not want power. Thirdly, there are only a hundred and sixty C workshops, fewer than one in twenty of those that stopped. The great majority of the workshops that stopped were not insolvent; what they wanted was money for a time.

The old accounts of the Great Tide tell a different story. The Great Tide of C.R. 1516 destroyed a great many nodes, and every city wanted power at once; in the accounts preserved at the branches of the cities, more than nine in ten of the workshops that stopped in the year after the Tide fall under A. What was wanting then was mana itself, and whether the chain of payments broke no longer mattered. For a time wage tickets and warehouse receipts passed as money; the workshops that had issued them stopped, the goods in the warehouses spoiled, the tickets fell heavily in value, and those who had taken them lost heavily. The rule that money is money and a bill is a bill was laid down then. The Great Tide and the later stoppages are thus the two extremes: in the one every city wanted power, in the others one city only. This book treats of the latter.

By the division of Chapter 5, the work stopped under A is the loss from the stoppage itself, and the work stopped under B the loss from the breakdown of payments spreading outwards. The greater part of B lies in the cities whose supply had not failed. And in the four tide years the ratio of B to A grows from one stoppage to the next: one and six-tenths in F.E. 124, three in F.E. 234; the ordinary year of F.E. 197 stands apart.

Why should the ratio grow? I believe there are two reasons. One is that more and more of the trade between city and city is paid by bills. When the speaking-network was completed in F.E. 191, the branches began to check their accounts with one another continually instead of monthly, and buying and selling on credit between cities became easy; by the accounts of the branches, the bills passing between cities in the 230s amounted to more than four times those of the 120s. The longer the chain, the further a break travels. The other reason is that the hoarding of notes in tide years has grown heavier; see Chapter 9.

The two losses differ not only in size but in their course. Fig. 1 shows the number of workshops stopped day by day in F.E. 234. A reaches its height on the third day of the stoppage; the node was repaired on the nineteenth day, and five days later A was gone. B does not begin to rise until the fifth day, goes on rising for ten days after the repair, reaches its height about the thirtieth, and on the ninetieth day more than three hundred workshops had still not started again. More than half the loss of a stoppage comes after the node is repaired, and those who watch only whether the node has been repaired do not see it.

**BOOK III. HOARDING AND TRANSMISSION**

**CHAPTER 9. THE HOARDING OF NOTES**

A workshop may pay out the notes it holds, or keep them idle. In ordinary times it keeps only enough to meet a few days’ expenses, and lays out the rest in payments and materials, or puts it in a mutual fund, which lends it on to others. A note kept idle earns nothing; a note lent earns interest. In ordinary times, therefore, no one keeps many.

A tide year is different. Everyone fears a stoppage, and after a stoppage good bills may not fetch notes (Chapter 3); the silver note becomes the one thing that can be relied upon, for it is redeemed in silver on sight, and the Reserve has never failed to redeem one. So the workshops keep more notes and pay less in advance; households draw their money out of the mutual funds, turn it into notes and put it at the bottom of the chest; and the acceptance houses themselves keep more notes and take fewer bills, and take them only at a high discount. The more notes lie idle, the fewer circulate; and unless new silver comes in, the Reserve cannot issue one more.

The branches register the notes they redeem and take in by their numbers. From these registers one may count what part of the notes outstanding at the beginning of the month has not come back to be checked at any branch within a month. A note that has not come back within a month is not necessarily lying idle; it may have passed through several hands. But in ordinary years this part is fairly steady, and what is added to it after a stoppage may for the most part be set down to hoarding. I call it the idle notes. Before the speaking-network the branches checked their accounts monthly, and the part could be counted only by the month; all five stoppages are counted in that manner. Table 3 gives the idle notes in the month before each of the five stoppages and in the first month after it, together with the median rate of discount of the acceptance houses, by the year.

In the four tide years the idle notes rose by half or more after the stoppage, and the rate of discount rose to between 1.4 and 2.7 times what it had been; in the ordinary year both rose very little. People hoard notes, not because a note has become worth more, but because everything else has become unreliable: bills may not be met, the mutual funds may not pay out, and only the note is sure of its silver.

For any single workshop, hoarding is no mistake: the more notes it keeps, the safer it is. The mistake lies in everyone doing it together. When all keep their notes, fewer payments are made; when fewer payments are made, more bills go unmet, and all must keep still more. In this matter the prudence of each, added together, becomes the imprudence of the city.

**CHAPTER 10. THE TRANSMISSION NUMBER**

How far a break in the chain of payments travels can be measured by a single number.

Suppose a workshop has not received a payment it expected on the day. If it has money in hand, or can discount good bills, it meets its own bills and wage tickets as before, and the chain is joined again at that link; if not, it too fails to pay on the day, and the chain breaks one link further on. One default seldom harms one workshop only: the defaulter may owe several, and each of those may owe others. What must be counted, therefore, is not what part of the workshops goes on to default, but how many further defaults, on the average, each default draws after it. This number I call the rate of default, and write $r$.

$r$ can be counted from the accounts. The branches and the acceptance houses keep the day of falling due and the day of payment of every bill. If each bill that fell due unmet is joined to the bills that its holder failed to meet within thirty days afterwards, the accounts give strings of linked defaults; the holder’s own wage tickets left unmet are counted in its own stopped work, not as further defaults; a later default joined to several earlier ones is set down to the earliest only, so that nothing is counted twice. The mean number of later defaults joined to each default is $r$. When thirty days are made sixty, $r$ changes by no more than three in the hundred in any of the five stoppages.

Write $D$ for the work stopped by the stoppage itself. The second link, drawn from $D$, can be counted separately: the bills the A workshops failed to meet, reckoned by the work they stopped among those who should have been paid, came in F.E. 234 to 16,800 workshop-days, while $r$ times $D$ is 16,500, a difference of less than two in the hundred; in the other four stoppages the difference is under a tenth. The work stopped at the second link may therefore be written $r D$. If each default costs the workshop that should have been paid the same number of workshop-days, the third link is $r^2 D$, and so on; and so long as $r$ is less than one, the whole work stopped is

$$ D + r D + r^2 D + dots = D / (1 - r) $$ (1)

The number $1 / (1 - r)$ I call the transmission number: for each workshop-day stopped by the stoppage itself, the number of workshop-days stopped in the whole Alliance.

The idea of this number goes back to an estimate made by Glover of the University of Kanaich in F.E. 229 for the common works. He asked how much payment in all is added to the market, after several changes of hands, by the wages paid to men hired to repair a stretch of seaway. He reckoned how money paid out adds to payments link by link; this chapter reckons how money not paid takes away from them link by link; the reasoning is the same. His method will be needed again in Chapter 14.

Equation (1) rests on three assumptions: that the second link is $r D$, which has just been tested; that $r$ is roughly constant through one stoppage; and that every default causes roughly the same stopped work. The last two are only approximations: in the accounts, $r$ differs by no more than a tenth from one link to the next, and the workshop-days lost by the workshop that should have been paid differ by no more than a fifth from one link to the next. What follows the ninetieth day is left out of the tables; in F.E. 234 it came to about a tenth of A and B together. The $r$ and the A and B of Table 4 are all taken from the accounts of the first ninety days; equation (1) sums the links without end and is not cut off at the ninetieth day, but the two columns differ by less than this remainder, and may still be compared.

Table 4 sets the rate of default counted in each of the five stoppages, and the transmission number computed from it, beside the ratio (A + B)/A observed in Table 2 of Chapter 6.

| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 124 (tide year) | 0.60 | 2.5 | 2.6 |

$r$ is no constant of nature. Read Table 3 beside Table 4: the more notes lie idle and the higher the rate of discount, the greater is $r$. $r$ is great because a workshop that has not been paid cannot turn its good bills into notes. If in a stoppage good bills could fetch notes at the ordinary discount, or a little above it, $r$ might fall back to its value in an ordinary year, about one half; but this is only a supposition, for only one of the five stoppages fell in an ordinary year. By (1), had $r$ been one half in F.E. 234, the whole work stopped would have been twice 21,700, or about 43,400 workshop-days, instead of the 87,700 of A and B together that were in fact stopped: some forty-four thousand fewer; had it fallen only to 0.6, some thirty-three thousand fewer. Those tens of thousands of workshop-days are what Book IV of this work sets out to save.

$D$ cannot be saved. It comes from want of mana, and can only be lessened by repairing the node and bringing reserve cores to bear. But in F.E. 234 $D$ was only a quarter of the whole.

**BOOK IV. REMEDIES**

**CHAPTER 13. DISCOUNT**

To mend a breakdown of payments, a workshop that has not been paid but holds good bills must be able to turn its bills into notes. In ordinary times this is the business of the acceptance houses; in a stoppage they are afraid themselves, and someone is needed who is not afraid. That can only be the Reserve, for within the Alliance its notes alone are taken by everyone.

I propose:

First. As soon as the supply of a city has been declared stopped by those who have charge of the node, the branches of all the cities shall make ready a discount window; and when the bills met in the city fall below nine-tenths of what is usual, or the rate of discount of the acceptance houses rises above one and a half times what is usual, they shall open it, and discount eligible bills through the acceptance houses and the mutual funds. The Reserve shall not take the bills of workshops directly: for it does not know the thousands of workshops; and an acceptance house or mutual fund that endorses a bill bears the first loss upon it out of its own capital, makes good any bill it sent that is not met when it falls due, and, if it cannot make it good, loses the right to send bills.

Secondly. An eligible bill must meet four conditions: there is a true dealing behind it, with a record of order and delivery that can be examined; the drawer and the acceptor are registered, and the counterfoil can be checked; it is short, falling due within ninety days; and the party that must pay it is able to pay, its accounts having been examined by the acceptance house or mutual fund that sends the bill and its counterfoils checked at the registry, the insolvent being refused; the acceptance house or mutual fund that sends the bill must meet this condition too. Bills that meet the four conditions are discounted; bills that do not are refused outright.

Thirdly. The rate of discount shall be one in the hundred a year above that of the acceptance houses before the stoppage, reckoned by the year and charged for the time the bill has still to run. The Reserve then takes a little more than the ordinary price, and gives nothing away. When the stoppage is past, the acceptance houses will discount more cheaply than the Reserve, those who send bills will cease to come, and the window will fall idle of itself; thirty days after the stoppage is declared at an end, it shall be closed.

Fourthly. Debts that can no longer be paid are not to be discounted, but dealt with by winding-up. Discount is for those who want money for a time, not for the insolvent; to take bad debts too would be to fill one workshop’s hole with the silver of every holder of notes in the Alliance.

Fifthly. The bills discounted are met when they fall due by the parties that must pay them, and the Reserve takes back the notes it paid out; no bill may be renewed. The notes paid out, the face of the bills taken back and the discount shall be entered batch by batch; a first statement shall be published when the stoppage is over, and the whole account, audited by the Covenant Inspectorate, when the last bill has fallen due and been pursued.

These five rest upon the cover proposed in Chapter 18; until the cover is changed, the notes paid out in discount can only be what the spare silver of the branches allows. In this way the notes paid out are increased only for the time of the stoppage; as the bills fall due they are taken back, and since every bill falls due within ninety days of its discount, the last of them falls due ninety days after the window closes. Behind the notes the Reserve has paid out there is no emptiness, but good bills that will be met when they fall due. How eligible bills are to be defined in detail, and how the rate of discount is to be set, are treated at length in Chapters 15 and 16.

**CHAPTER 14. COMMON WORKS**

Discount can help only workshops that hold good bills. There are two kinds of people it cannot reach: the workshops and craftsmen in the stopped city who stand for want of mana, who have no payments owing to them and simply have no work; and the craftsmen dismissed when the chain broke, who hold nothing but wage tickets that cannot be met.

For these I propose that the Alliance employ them on common works: first in repairing the node that has failed, then the reserve lines, then other public works listed beforehand. The repair of a node needs the founders, crystal-grinders and engravers who have stopped work, the very trades that stand idlest in a stoppage. They are to be paid their ordinary wages in argents, at the charge of the finances of the Alliance. An item of “works for stoppages” should be set aside beforehand in the Account of Seaways and Public Works and spent according to the budget; the Reserve shall not advance the money to the Treasury, nor shall the Treasury, on that account, require the Reserve to issue more notes.

Common works do two things. They repair the node sooner: in F.E. 234 the node at Tallin took nineteen days, of which seven were spent waiting for craftsmen. And the wages, once paid, are spent by the craftsmen on rent and bread, and by the corn-dealer in meeting his bills, so that the chain of payments is joined again from that point. By Glover’s method a wage paid in this way, passing through several hands, does more than its own amount of work in payments; how much stopped work it saves depends on whether those who receive it can pay as usual, and on how much of it is kept idle, and this book makes no separate estimate. Repairing the node sooner lessens A; joining the chain of payments lessens B; the two are not simply to be added. Nor do the wages of common works discharge the wage tickets owed by the former employer: the craftsman may still redeem his tickets with his employer when they fall due, and if the employer is wound up, wages are paid before ordinary debts.

One thing must be made plain. It has been proposed that in a stoppage part of the city’s reserve of lift should be lent to the workshops, so as to lessen the work stopped under A. Not one share of it may be touched. That store is the strength that holds the city up, not capital for work; to use it is to stake the lives of the whole city against a few days’ work. Work stopped for want of mana can only be lessened by repairing the node, by bringing reserve cores to bear, and by drawing on the spare of other nodes. Neither of the two measures of this book touches it, or can touch it.

◇ Chapter 15, “Eligible Bills”, Chapter 16, “The Rate of Discount”, and Chapter 17, “Between City and City”, are omitted.

**BOOK V. ANSWERS**

**CHAPTER 18. TWO OBJECTIONS ANSWERED**

When the first draft of this book was passed round in F.E. 236, two objections were raised.

The first is that the loss from a stoppage arises from want of mana cores, and that discounting bills, which cannot make mana, is of no avail.

This is true of A, and not of B. In Table 1, of the four thousand two hundred and more workshops that stopped in F.E. 234, only twelve hundred wanted power or materials; the two thousand eight hundred and seventy under B had mana, and the supply accounts of the nodes show it delivered to them as usual on the days they stood; the hundred and sixty under C are another matter. What they wanted was not mana but money. Discount cannot make mana, and this book has never said that it can; it says that the forty thousand and more workshop-days reckoned in Chapter 10 were never lost for want of mana in the first place.

The second objection is that if the Reserve discounts bills, the notes it pays out will not all have silver behind them; the notes will lose their full cover, and their credit will be shaken.

This objection must be answered with care. The credit of the note lies in the holder’s being able to obtain silver for it at any time. The question is, then, how many notes holders will bring for redemption in a month at most, in a stoppage; and the registers of redemption at the branches answer it. Fig. 2 shows, for the five stoppages, the notes brought for redemption in the month after the stoppage, as a part of all notes issued. The most was in F.E. 234, twenty-three in the hundred; in the others it was below twenty.

Fig. 2. Notes brought for redemption in the month after each of five stoppages, as a part of all notes issued. The broken line is the least share of silver proposed in this book, three-tenths.

So long as silver is not less than three-tenths of the notes outstanding, then, it will meet the greatest month of redemption on record, with room to spare. The three-tenths is a limit on new issue, not on redemption: whoever brings a note is paid in silver while there is silver. Let the notes outstanding be $N$ and the silver $0.3 N$. If two-tenths and three hundredths are redeemed in a month, the silver left is $0.07 N$ and the notes outstanding $0.77 N$; redemption goes on as usual, but the share of silver has fallen below a tenth. Thereafter no new notes are issued, the notes taken in as bills are met are cancelled, and, once the stoppage is past, bonds are sold for notes, until the share of silver is three-tenths again. The three-tenths, then, is a floor: if silver stands close to it, no new notes can be issued once a stoppage comes, and discount is out of the question; in ordinary times silver must be kept well above it. Had it been five-tenths, the same redemption would leave silver at thirty-five in the hundred of the notes remaining, with room to discount bills to more than a tenth of the notes besides. The three-tenths is therefore a proposal fixed by the redemptions on record; whether people will redeem more once the cover has been changed, later stoppages must show.

I propose that the cover of the silver note be silver, bonds of the Alliance and eligible bills, valued by rule and together covering the notes outstanding in full, the silver being not less than three-tenths of the notes outstanding; that the bonds be only such as already pass in the market, the Reserve taking none directly from the Treasury; that the bills be eligible, and those overdue or no longer eligible be struck out and made good by those who endorsed them; and that when the cover falls short, no further notes be issued. On this footing, when the Reserve discounts bills in a stoppage, the note has three securities behind it, silver, bonds and good bills, and is no false note.

Those who hold the old doctrine may think three-tenths too little. The share is open to discussion. I ask them only to observe that a note fully covered by silver cannot be issued by one note more in a stoppage, and that this is itself a kind of failure to pay. To the workshop, the man with good bills who cannot obtain notes is in the same case as the man with notes who cannot obtain silver.

**BOOK VI. CONCLUSION**

**CHAPTER 24. CONCLUDING NOTES: MONEY, BILLS AND THE RESERVE**

The conclusion of this book can be put shortly. The loss from a stoppage is of two kinds: the one from want of mana discount cannot remedy, and it can be lessened only by repairing the node and bringing reserve cores to bear; the one from want of money can be remedied, by discount and by common works. The first lies mostly in the stopped city, though it may travel to other cities along the supply of materials; the second spreads through the Alliance, is commonly the larger, and comes for the most part after the node is repaired. The old doctrine saw the first and did not see the second.

It is feared that discount will undo the rule that money is money and a bill is a bill. It will not. The rule says that a bill is not money, and that no one need take it as money. When the Reserve discounts, what it takes in is a bill and what it pays out is a note; the bill remains a bill, to be met when it falls due and pursued if it is not; the note remains a note, redeemed on sight. The holder of notes holds notes, not bills. After the Great Tide the harm of wage tickets passing as money was that everyone was forced to take bills for money. Discount does the very opposite: it lets the man with good bills obtain true money without having to pass his bills as money.

The old maxims tell only half the duty of the Reserve, which is to keep the note redeemable in silver. The other half is to keep the chain of payments from breaking half-way. The first half is for the holders of notes, the second for everyone in the Alliance who works; and the two are not at odds, as Chapter 18 has reckoned.

A last word on the old maxims. The old hands of the counting-house will often say that they go by experience alone and believe in no doctrine. Yet the rules they work by are for the most part the notions of some clerk of several centuries ago. Those who laid down, about C.R. 1240, that every note must have its silver might not lay it down again if they could see the notes passing every day across the counters of the cities now. An old maxim is hard to change, not because it is reasonable, but because it is familiar. If this book leads its readers to ask once more why the familiar rules should hold, it will not have been written in vain.

## Correspondence Is All That Is Needed

**CORRESPONDENCE IS ALL THAT IS NEEDED**

By LYNN, Kanaich Institute of Technology.

[Received in the Month of Flowing Fire, F.E. 350.]

**Abstract.** To read a damaged book that carries mana, its code-names, its disempowered transcriptions and its casting traces must be matched one with another, and related places often lie hundreds of marks apart, or in another volume. The reading and writing formulae now in use rest on carried reading: they read mark by mark, fold what has been read into a carried note, and add an alignment on the writing side. We propose a formula that uses correspondence alone, and dispenses with carried reading and with windows entirely. On reading each mark it weighs every mark within its working range by the strength of their relation and draws upon the most related; material beyond the range is brought in by cross-volume retrieval. On two reading tasks of the Great Library the correspondence formula reaches a higher letter agreement than the best formulae now in use, and does far better on correspondences that lie far apart; it can be trained in parallel, and needs only a fraction of the engine-room days. It serves also for parsing old formulae.

**1 Introduction**

In the code repository of the Great Library, glyphs that carry mana are stored only as code-names [1]. To read a damaged book that carries mana, three things must be done: the original glyph must be recognised from its code-name, the glyph matched with its disempowered transcription, and the transcription matched with the trace left by the casting. The three are seldom in one place: a code-name appears at the head of a volume, its transcription some hundreds of marks later, and the record of the trace in another volume. By the count of the Repository Office in F.E. 349, more than four thousand three hundred damaged volumes that carry mana have still not been read through; most of them not because their letters cannot be made out, but because they cannot be matched.

Since Darby’s inductive formula [2], formulae for reading and writing such records have multiplied. The best of them are carried-reading formulae [3]: the chain reads from left to right, and at each mark folds the mark together with what it has already read into a carried note; at the end the carried note holds the sense of the whole, and the writing side writes from it mark by mark. The carried note was later given gates, so that it could choose what to keep and what to forget [4]; distant relations fared somewhat better, but still had to be passed on step by step. An alignment was later added to the writing side [5]: at each mark it writes, it looks back over the carried notes at every place on the reading side and takes the most related. Alignment much improved the reading and writing of long sentences.

Carried reading has two faults. First, a relation between distant marks must pass through many steps to be made: a code-name at the head of a volume must be carried to its transcription hundreds of marks later, folded once at every step, and after hundreds of foldings it has faded from the carried note. Secondly, the marks must be read one at a time: mark $t$ must wait until mark $t - 1$ has been read. The reckoning within one mark may be shared among several paths, and several volumes may be read at once; but the thousands of marks of one volume must be reckoned one after another, and the longer the volume, the longer the wait. Window formulae [6] look only at a few marks on either side and can be run in parallel, but distant relations still need many stacked layers to be made.

We propose the correspondence formula. It uses neither carried reading nor windows; its reading side and its writing side rely on correspondence alone. Each mark, when it is read, weighs every mark within the working range and draws upon them according to the strength of their relation. Any two marks are thus a single step apart; and since in training the marks to be written are known beforehand, the weighing for every mark on both sides can be done at once, only the actual writing being done mark by mark.

The form of correspondence was suggested by the doctrine of seven layers of the White Tower [7]. That doctrine holds that a layer deep within Tiamat divides mana by its destination and sends it at once to the layers above, instead of passing it on in order, layer by layer. We follow this pattern: the information of each mark is sent at once to every place that needs it, and correspondence is divided into several paths, each of which learns one kind of relation (§ 3.2).

**2 Background**

The carried note of a carried-reading formula [3] is a row of numbers of width $d$; at each mark a fixed set of weights combines the old carried note and the new mark into a new carried note. Each layer of a window formula [6] combines the numbers of $k$ neighbouring marks into one; when the spacing of the window doubles from layer to layer, $log_k n$ layers must be stacked to connect $n$ marks, and without such doubling, $n \/ k$. Alignment [5] is used only for the writing side to look at the reading side; within the reading side there is still carried reading. Adjustable retrieval from the store [8] lets a chain fetch records from its store by a query, in a manner close to correspondence, but only as a memory added beside carried reading. Correspondence within the reading side, as a supplement to carried reading, has been tried [9], but always as a layer added on top of carried reading, never in place of it.

To the best of our knowledge, the formula of this paper is the first to read and write by correspondence alone, without carried reading or windows.

**3 The formula**

The correspondence formula has a reading side and a writing side (Fig. 1). The reading side reads a sequence of marks $(x_1, dots, x_n)$ into a sequence of rows of numbers $(z_1, dots, z_n)$; from these the writing side writes $(y_1, dots, y_m)$ one at a time, each mark written being taken as input for the next step.

Fig. 1. The correspondence formula. Left, the reading side; right, the writing side; each a stack of $N$ layers.

**3.1 The reading side and the writing side**

The reading side is a stack of six layers. Each layer has two parts: a divided correspondence, and a position-wise transform. Beside each part runs a bypass [10], and the result is levelled once more [11] (the numbers of each mark less their mean, divided by their standard deviation, then scaled and shifted place by place), so that the output of each part is

$$ "level"(x + "part"(x)) $$ (1)

So that the bypasses fit, every part and the row of every mark have the same width, $d = 512$.

The writing side is also a stack of six layers, each of three parts in turn: correspondence within itself that may not look ahead, alignment with the output of the reading side, and the position-wise transform, each with its bypass and levelling. The input of the writing side is the marks already written, shifted one place, with a mark of beginning before them: position $i$, from which mark $i$ is written, holds only what was written before it, so that it may attend to positions 1 to $i$ and not beyond. Writing stops at a stop mark, or at 2048 marks at most.

**3.2 Correspondence**

Correspondence pairs a query with a set of keys and values: the query is weighed once against each key, giving a set of weights, and the values are summed according to the weights to give the output. Query, keys and values are all rows of numbers.

**3.2.1 Weighed correspondence.** Let queries and keys have width $d_k$, and values width $d_v$. If many queries are set out as an array $Q$, and the keys and values likewise as arrays $K$ and $V$, then

$$ "Corr"(Q, K, V) = "share"((Q K^T) / sqrt(d_k)) V $$ (2)

Share takes the exponential of each number in a row and divides by their sum, so that the weights of a row add up to one. We divide by $sqrt(d_k)$ because when $d_k$ is large the products of $Q$ and $K$ are large, the weights after sharing gather into one or two places, and the adjustment of weights scarcely moves them: if the numbers of query and key are roughly independent, with mean nought and spread one, the sum of their products has spread $d_k$, and dividing by $sqrt(d_k)$ brings it back to one.

**3.2.2 Divided correspondence.** Instead of a single correspondence with queries, keys and values of width $d$, we found it far better to project each of them by $h$ different sets of weights to the narrower widths $d_k$ and $d_v$, to carry out $h$ correspondences at once along $h$ paths, and to join the outputs of the paths and project them back to width $d$ (Fig. 2):

$$ "Div"(Q, K, V) = "join"("path"_1, dots, "path"_h) W^O $$ (3)

$$ "where" quad "path"_i = "Corr"(Q W_i^Q, K W_i^K, V W_i^V) $$

Here $W_i^Q$ and $W_i^K$ are arrays of $d times d_k$, $W_i^V$ of $d times d_v$, and $W^O$ of $h d_v times d$. In the base formula we take $h = 8$ and $d_k = d_v = d \/ h = 64$. Since each path is narrower, eight paths together cost about as much as a single correspondence of full width.

The dividing layer of the doctrine of seven layers splits mana into streams by destination; divided correspondence does likewise, each path learning to watch one kind of relation. When the weights of the paths are examined after training, some paths attend to the correspondence of code-name and transcription, some to traces and the versions of formulae, and some to the parsing of the text around (Fig. 3).

Fig. 2. Left, weighed correspondence. Right, divided correspondence, made of $h$ weighed correspondences in parallel.

**3.2.3 Three uses.** Correspondence is used in three ways in this formula. (i) The writing side aligns with the reading side: the queries come from the output of the part before it in the same layer of the writing side, and the keys and values from the output of the reading side, so that every mark written can see all the marks read. (ii) The reading side corresponds within itself: queries, keys and values all come from the layer below on the reading side, and every mark can see all the marks on that side. (iii) The writing side corresponds within itself in the same way, but may not look ahead: before sharing, the weighing of the $i$-th query against every key after $i$ is set to minus infinity.

**3.3 Position-wise transform**

Besides correspondence, every layer has a position-wise transform: the row of each mark is projected twice, separately, and the negative numbers are clipped between:

$$ "transform"(x) = "clip"(x W_1 + b_1) W_2 + b_2 $$ (4)

The inner width is 2048. The same weights serve every position; each layer has its own.

**3.4 Rows of marks, and sharing**

Each mark is first turned into a row of width $d$ by a learned table, and multiplied by $sqrt(d)$ before the position marks are added; the output of the writing side is turned by one projection and a share into the chances of the next mark. The reading side, the writing side and the output use one and the same table, since both sides use the same code.

**3.5 Position marks**

Correspondence by itself takes no account of order: if a sequence of marks is shuffled, the outputs are merely shuffled with it, and what each mark receives is unchanged. A position mark must therefore be added to the row of every mark. We follow the manner of the tide-tables, which write the height of the tide as a sum of constituents of different periods [12]; we too give each position a set of sines and cosines of different periods,

$$ "pos"(p, 2i) = sin(p \/ 10000^(2i\/d)), quad "pos"(p, 2i+1) = cos(p \/ 10000^(2i\/d)) $$ (5)

where $i$ runs from 0 to $d \/ 2 - 1$, the periods running in geometric progression from $2 pi$ to about $10000 dot 2 pi$. Between the position marks of two positions a fixed distance apart there is then a fixed transformation, so that correspondence can easily learn relations of the kind “so many marks away”. Learned position marks [6] did about as well (Table 4, row I); we keep the sines, because they can be written for passages longer than any seen in training: the training passages were mostly under a thousand marks, and on test passages of one to two thousand marks the sines did 0.8 points better than learned marks.

**3.6 Beyond the range: cross-volume retrieval**

The working range is fixed at 2048 marks, of which the text itself, with the task mark, has at most 1536, the remaining 512 being kept for material brought in. When the transcription or trace of a damaged book lies outside the text, it is brought in by the catalogue retrieval of the Great Library [13], and the reading is done twice. The first reading reads the text alone. Then the outputs of the top layer of the reading side, averaged over the positions and projected by a further array $W^R$ of $d times d_k$, give a single retrieval query, which is weighed against the head keys of the catalogue; the four passages of highest raw score (the inner product, neither divided by $sqrt(d_k)$ nor shared) are taken, each cut to 128 marks in all, counting the outer-volume mark, bearing its volume number, that precedes it, and joined after the text. The second reading reads the text and the four passages together from the beginning, the passages corresponding like the text, and their position marks continuing those of the text. The head key of a passage is made by reading its first 64 marks with the reading side, averaging in the same way, and projecting the result by the same $W^R$; in training the keys are computed afresh every ten thousand steps with the weights of the moment, and stay fixed in between. Retrieval is not adjusted together with correspondence: $W^R$ is trained apart, on the cross-volume correspondences known in the parallel copies, the passage known to hold the transcription of a code-name being counted right and the rest wrong. The catalogue holds only those parts of the repository that this work was permitted to read; sealed and licensed parts are left out under the present grades of holding and rules of leave.

**4 Why correspondence**

Table 1 compares correspondence, carried reading and windows on three counts: the cost of each layer; the steps within a layer that must be taken in order, which decide how many paths can run in parallel; and the most steps the information between two marks must pass, which decides whether distant relations are easily learned. $n$ is the length of the range, $d$ the width, $k$ the width of the window.

Table 1. Three ways compared.

| Way | Cost of a layer | Steps in order | Most steps between two marks |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Correspondence | $n^2 dot d$ | 1 | 1 |
| Carried reading | $n dot d^2$ | $n$ | $n$ |
| Windows (spacing doubled) | $k dot n dot d^2$ | 1 | $log_k n$ |
| Correspondence with the nearest $r$ only | $r dot n dot d$ | 1 | $n \/ r$ |

Table 1 compares the core step of each kind of layer, constants neglected; a layer of correspondence has besides its projections and its position-wise transform, which cost about $12 n dot d^2$ when the inner width is $4 d$, and retrieval is reckoned apart. In damaged books $n$ is mostly between some hundreds and two thousand. By the expressions of Table 1, at $n = 2048$ and $d = 512$ correspondence itself costs four times as much as carried reading; with the projections and the transform counted, a layer of correspondence still costs less than three times a layer of gated carried reading [4], some $8 n dot d^2$, and need not be done in order. Were the range greater still, each mark could be made to attend only to the $r$ nearest it; this we leave for later.

Correspondence has a further merit: it can be seen. The weights of every path can be printed and read, showing at a glance what is being read and where it is matched (Fig. 3). The carried note of carried reading is only a row of numbers.

Fig. 3. The weights of two paths in the fifth layer of divided correspondence. Above, a sentence of the volume: five code-names and the trace number at its end; below, the transcription of those five code-names elsewhere in the same volume, more than three hundred marks away. The thicker the line, the greater the weight. The left path joins each code-name to its transcription; the right joins the code-names to the trace number in the same sentence.

**5 Training**

**5.1 Material and tasks.** The Repository Office of the Great Library gave us two sets of parallel copies. One is of code-names and transcriptions, 4.1 million pairs: the reading side reads a passage of a damaged book, holding code-names and also scattered, broken pieces of their transcriptions, and the writing side writes the full transcription of every code-name in the passage, in the order of the code-names, each closed by a separating mark, a code-name it cannot answer receiving a mark of doubt. The transcriptions themselves are often broken, and cannot simply be copied, but must be put together from several pieces and the text around them. The “distance” of Table 3 is the number of marks, in the original volume, between a code-name and the nearest piece of its transcription; where it exceeds the range of the text, or the pieces lie only in another volume, they can be seen only through retrieval, and Table 3 counts them together as beyond the range. The other set is of transcriptions and casting traces, 1.2 million pairs: the reading side reads a transcription, and the writing side writes the number of the trace and the version of the formula it answers to; the source and version of every trace were checked by its signature [14], and whether its content is true was examined by the collators apart. The two tasks were trained together, a task mark being set before the reading side. Of the thirty-six thousand characters of the Code Table, some eleven thousand occur in the parallel copies; when those that often occur together had been merged into single units [15], the table of units held thirty-two thousand entries, rare characters being written out one by one in their codes, so that every text can be restored without loss. The material was put in batches of like length, each of about twenty-five thousand marks over the eight paths together. For each task eight thousand pairs were set aside for testing and four thousand for tuning; the division was made by whole volumes, so that variant copies of a volume and repeated transcriptions fall on one side only. In testing, the catalogue was fixed at its version of F.E. 349, and the collated copies used for testing were not entered in it.

**5.2 Engines and schedule.** We trained in the engine room of the Kanaich Institute of Technology on eight paths; an engine-room day in this paper means those eight paths fully used for one day. The base formula is that of § 3; the large formula has width $d$ = 1024, sixteen paths each of width 64, an inner width of 4096 in the position-wise transform, and dropping at three-tenths. The engine-room days of Table 2 include retrieval and the recomputing of head keys, but not the first making of the catalogue. Each step of the base formula took about 0.4 seconds, 80,000 steps in all, about nine hours; each step of the large formula about 1.2 seconds, 250,000 steps, about three and a half days.

**5.3 Adjustment.** The loss is the negative logarithm of the chance given to the right next mark, and the weights begin as small random numbers. Weights were adjusted by a method of gradients estimated step by step [16], with rates of decay 0.9 and 0.98, the step-size rising over the first four thousand steps and thereafter falling as the inverse square root of the step number:

$$ "rate" = d^(-0.5) dot min("step"^(-0.5), "step" dot 4000^(-1.5)) $$ (6)

**5.4 Against over-training.** In training, every number in the output of every part was dropped at random with chance one-tenth before being added to the bypass, which we call dropping [17]; nothing is dropped in reading and writing. Nor is the target taken as wholly certain: the right unit is given nine-tenths and the remaining tenth is spread over the other units, which we call sparing [18]. Sparing makes the chances written less certain, but raises the letter agreement.

**7 Conclusion**

The formula of this paper uses correspondence alone; neither its reading side nor its writing side uses carried reading or windows. In reading damaged books it does better than both, best of all on correspondences far apart, and it trains much faster.

We hope to use it more widely: to read array diagrams and star charts, to read recordings of the speaking-network, and to read over longer ranges. We hope also to use it to read the whole code repository: all the documents, formulae and collations entered in a hundred and thirty years and more, which no scholar could read in a lifetime.

The number of layers, the range, the passages brought in and the longest writing are all fixed, so that the operations of every step of the correspondence formula are bounded beforehand by an amount reckonable from these numbers; the formula therefore always stops in reading and writing a passage, and by Cummings [20] it belongs to a class whose stopping can be completely judged. This says only that it stops, not that it reads rightly; whether it reads rightly is for the collators to check.

The pattern of correspondence came from the doctrine of seven layers, as has been said. That the formula works does not prove the doctrine. But without what that seer saw deep within the White Tower, we should probably never have thought of sending information all at once, instead of passing it on step by step.

## Inference Engines and Insight

**INFERENCE ENGINES AND INSIGHT**

By CUMMINGS.

**1. The Copyists’ Test**

I wish to consider the question, “Can an inference engine have insight?” A discussion of it ought to begin by fixing the meaning of the words “inference engine” and “insight”. But if their meanings were fixed by the way the words are commonly used, the answer to the question would have to be sought as the readers’ polls of this Review are sought, by counting what people say; and that would be absurd. Instead of fixing the words, therefore, I shall put another question in place of the first, closely related to it but less vaguely expressed.

The new question may be put by way of an examination. When the copying-shops of Copyists’ Street in Chaen take apprentices, there is an old way of examining an apprentice who is to come out of his apprenticeship. The master and the apprentice sit in two rooms, and the examiner in a third, who passes questions through a curtain into both rooms. Each writes his answers, which are fair-copied by a copyist who has no part in the matter and passed out; the examiner sees only the fair copies, and must guess which came from the master. The apprentice tries to make him guess wrong; the master tries to help him guess right. The questions need not be about copying: the examiner may ask the old form of a letter, the descent of a book, how a page should be laid out to look well, or anything else. If he guesses wrong often enough, the apprentice comes out of his apprenticeship.

The old copyists say that what tests a man in this examination is not the copying but the answering. Suppose the examiner passes in a slip: “Write the old form of the character that in the old script means ‘seal’, and say why it is so written.” An apprentice who means to pass for his master will answer in his master’s manner: “In the old form there is a dot at the top; the dot is the lump of sealing clay, and later copyists left it out to save trouble.” The master writes only the truth, and may add: “This answer is mine; the one behind the other curtain is copied from me.” But the apprentice can write that too, and the examiner can conclude nothing from it.

We now ask: if an inference engine takes the apprentice’s place, will the examiner guess wrong as often as when he examines a master and an apprentice? This question takes the place of the original one, “Can an inference engine have insight?”

Since it is insight that we are asking about, the examiner should not keep to copying, but should ask the questions that are commonly answered well only by insight: whether a chain will stop, where an unsolved problem should be taken hold of, which line of a poem is the best, whether the doctrine of seven layers is to be believed. Nor need the two behind the curtain be a master and an apprentice: they may be a scholar who has ascended the Tower and a scholar who has not, the second being then replaced by an inference engine. I shall call this the fair-copy test.

The fair-copy test is to be held thus. In the main test the two behind the curtain are a scholar who has ascended the Tower and an inference engine; in the comparison they are a scholar who has ascended the Tower and a scholar of like standing who has not. The examiner knows only that one of the two is not a scholar who has ascended the Tower, and is not told whether it is a man or a machine; the answers bear only the marks A and B; each sitting lasts a quarter of an hour of questioning, the time of fair-copying not counted. Both sides may consult books and reckon: the scholars may consult the Library and use calculating engines, the engine may search the repository and call calculating engines, and what was searched and reckoned is recorded and published after the test. After each sitting the examiner says which is the scholar who has ascended the Tower; over many sittings, the part he names rightly is counted for the main test and for the comparison separately. If the two parts differ little, we shall say that in this test the engine is as hard to tell as a scholar who has not ascended.

**2. Criticism of the New Question**

The new question has the merit of drawing a fairly sharp line between a man’s body and his mind. The examiner cannot see those behind the curtain, nor hear their voices, nor even see their hands. The engine need not look like a scholar, nor be able to cast; just as we do not say that a scholar has no insight because he cannot cast some spell. The White Tower Society laid down about C.R. 1090 the rule that insight must be reckoned, which likewise looks at what is written and checked, and not at how it came. The fair-copy test borrows the spirit of that old rule, but does not follow from it: the rule governs how what insight yields becomes learning, while the test asks whether, in question and answer, the two can be told apart. To pass the test shows only that they cannot; whether what was answered is right must still be checked apart.

The questions and answers might run as follows.

Q: Please write me a short poem on the Twin Towers race.

A: Spare me that. I never could write verse.

Q: Add 34957 to 70764.

A: (After a short pause) 105621.

Q: Here is a chain: begin from any whole number above nought; if it is even, halve it; if it is odd, treble it and add one; and stop on reaching one. Will it always stop?

A: Every one I have tried has stopped, but I do not know whether every one will.

The method of question and answer seems fit to draw out almost any of the abilities we might wish to examine.

It may be said that the test is unfair to the engine; for a scholar who sat behind the curtain and tried to pass for an engine would be found out at once by a question asking him to multiply two large numbers. That is true, but we are not asking whether a scholar can pass for an engine. It may also be asked whether an engine that answered better than a scholar would not be found out at a glance. It would then have to hide its skill, as the apprentice hides his clumsiness. The best course for the engine is probably to give the answers a scholar would give; but I shall not go further into that here.

**3. The Machines Concerned**

The word “machine” must first be limited. By a machine I shall mean only an inference engine, that is, a rune chain trained by the correspondence formula [1] on the code repository, the Great Library’s coded archive of documents, collations and traces, and run upon a calculating engine [2]. Golems and gargoyles are not included, for their control cores can act only as they were graven; nor are elemental beings, which are born living, and inherit from the individual before them its mana core and part of its knowledge, and are not inference engines at all. One kind must be excluded besides: a scholar may be said to have been made by his teachers, but we may not seat a scholar behind the curtain and call him a machine. The machine behind the curtain must be one of which we know how it takes every step.

This limitation may be thought too narrow: other machines may one day appear, more like to have insight than the inference engine. But the inference engine is the machine we have today that is most likely to pass the test, and since the question has arisen because of it, it is with it that the question should first be asked.

**4. Inference Engines**

The body of an inference engine is a calculating engine. Since Waterhouse of Tallin [3], a calculating engine has had three parts: the store, which holds the numbers to be used and the results along the way; the executive mechanism, which works step by step; and the formula, which tells the executive mechanism what to do at each step. An inference engine differs from an ordinary calculating engine not in its body but in its formula. Its formula is the correspondence formula, and the weights within it were not written in one by one by a rune-cutter; they were adjusted little by little as the engine read the code repository, being corrected whenever it read wrongly. The first inference engine [4] read material reckoned at ten to the fifteenth power of marks, and its adjustment took nearly two years of the spare capacity of several engine rooms at Kanaich and Tallin.

A single step on the formula tape, written out, would run something like this: “Take the query in section 6809 of the store, multiply it place by place into each of the keys beginning at section 4302 and add, divide each sum by eight, and put the results in order in the sections beginning at 7015.” A reckoner following the tape with pen and paper, step by step, could do everything an inference engine does, only it would take him some thousands of years to read a page. The engine differs from the reckoner only in its speed, and in never growing tired.

What an inference engine has learned, then, is held in the weights in its store; what it uses in a single answer includes also the question it has just read, the passages it brings in from the repository, and the numbers calculating engines reckon for it. Every step it takes is a step of the correspondence formula, that is, to read a few cells, reckon a little, and write a few cells. We may not understand in the least what the weights mean, and yet understand perfectly what each step does.

**5. The Universality of Rune Chains**

Consider first a very simple machine. Among the star-reckoning arrays of the Tower of All Things there is a kind of timing crystal which, each quarter of an hour, passes from one brightness to the next: from full to half, from half to dark, and from dark to full again. Beneath the crystal is a gate; when the gate is closed it stays at the brightness it has. When it is at full brightness, a lamp beside it is lit. It reads the gate and changes its brightness only at the stroke of each quarter; what the gate does between strokes counts for nothing, and the lamp follows the brightness after the change. Its changes may be set out in a table (Table 1).

Table 1. The changes of the timing crystal.

| Brightness now | Next, gate open | Next, gate closed | Lamp |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Full | Half | Full | Lit |
| Half | Dark | Half | Unlit |
| Dark | Full | Dark | Unlit |

Machines of this kind I call discrete-state machines: at every moment they are in one of a finite number of states, and the state at the next moment is fixed by the present state and the input alone. Given the present state and the inputs to come, everything the machine will afterwards do can be reckoned. An inference engine with a fixed store, whose numbers are held to fixed places, is a discrete-state machine too, only with an extraordinary number of states: its store holds some hundreds of thousands of millions of numbers, and each number may take many values.

An inference engine is a rune chain. By Lemma 1 of my paper “On Decidable Matters” [2], there is a universal chain that can carry out any rune chain whatever; so that any calculating engine that can carry out the universal chain, and realises all the universal units, can, with enough store and time, do whatever any inference engine does. By this I mean that after each step imitated, the state and the results of the engine imitated are the same; not that the steps beneath are as many, and still less that they are as quick; and I speak only of reckoning, not of supply or of the engine’s joinings.

This allows the question of this paper to be put, first, more widely: is there a rune chain that would do well in the fair-copy test? If there is, a narrower question follows: can the correspondence formula, taught in the manner of § 7, find it? The first question is a condition of the second, but not the same question.

**6. Contrary Views**

We may now consider the objections. Let me first say what I myself believe. I believe that in about fifty years’ time, about F.E. 410, inference engines will do so well in the fair-copy test that an ordinary examiner, after a quarter of an hour of questions, will have no more than seven chances in ten of naming the engine rightly. The original question, “Can an inference engine have insight?”, I think too vague to deserve discussion; but I believe that in fifty years the habits of speech and the opinion of the learned will have changed so much that it will no longer seem absurd to speak of an inference engine having insight. I take the objections roughly in order of weight.

**(1) The Objection from the Tower.** Insight is gained by ascending the Tower: a scholar has insight because within the White Tower he experiences in his own person the power of the laws. An inference engine cannot ascend the Tower, and therefore cannot have insight.

This objection says how men come by insight; it does not say that nothing else can. Why ascending the Tower gives insight we do not know. If the reason lies wholly in experiencing in one’s own person, it has still to be said who experiences, what is experienced, and how it becomes something that can be written as a formula. The White Tower Society’s own rule looks at reckoning and checking, and not at the source. Moreover, the pattern of the correspondence formula was given by the doctrine of seven layers, which a seer saw deep within the White Tower [5]. If insight can teach a machine how to read, how far the machine can go once it has learned cannot be settled beforehand by whether it has ascended the Tower. Whether an inference engine may be carried into the Tower is for the rules of the Tower, and not for this paper. Those who hold this objection sometimes add that the White Tower is a demiplane forged by the sages of old, whose laws are not those outside it, and that insight arises from just this. Even so, that shows only that there is something within the Tower that is not outside it; it does not show that only living beings can receive it.

**(2) The Objection of Heads in the Sand.** The consequences of an engine’s having insight would be too dreadful: we who live two hundred years, and spend a hundred of them gaining insight in the Tower, would find that it counts for little. Let us hope that it cannot.

This objection is seldom stated openly, but it is in many minds. It is not an argument and does not need refuting; consolation would be more to the point. I would only say that because we live long, our fear of being overtaken by those who come after is long too.

**(3) The Mathematical Objection.** My paper “On Decidable Matters” [2] proves that no rune chain can decide whether every rune chain stops, and that every system whose proofs can be listed by rule, in which propositions that a chain never stops can be recognised mechanically, and whose proved propositions of this form are all true, leaves some such proposition unproved. The Babel fragment [6] proves, too, that a system which does not contradict itself, can express the arithmetic of the natural numbers, and has rules that can be listed, cannot prove by its own means that it does not contradict itself. An inference engine is a rune chain, and there must therefore be questions it cannot answer rightly; but the seer is under no such limit.

This objection needs a longer answer, since it uses my own theorems against me. The theorems are true: no inference engine can be sure of giving, for every chain and every input, the right judgment in finite time; there will be questions it answers wrongly, or cannot answer, or on which it never stops: the correspondence formula always stops when it writes an answer [1], but the chains it sends to the calculating engines need not. But the same theorems say that any method that works by rule has such questions; they do not say that the seer is free of this limit, and no one has proved that he is. The White Tower Society laid down the rule that insight must be reckoned precisely because what insight yields is sometimes right and sometimes wrong, and counts only when it is checked. I said in § 12 of that paper that if a seer could see, for every rune chain, whether it stops, and were never wrong, his gift could not be written as rules. That still holds; but it is a conditional statement, and no one has yet shown that there is such a seer, who is never wrong. When an engine answers a question wrongly we feel that we have scored a point; yet we ourselves have answered many questions wrongly. In the fair-copy test the examiner can put only a few questions; he does not know which engine sits behind the curtain or what its weights are, and so cannot frame the one question that would defeat it alone; and the questions the engine cannot answer rightly are not necessarily answered rightly by the scholar.

I think that those who hold the mathematical objection would mostly be willing to discuss the matter on the ground of the fair-copy test. What they wish to say is that there are always questions an inference engine will not answer rightly, and with that I entirely agree. But we should not, because a machine has questions it answers wrongly, feel ourselves a grade above it. We too have questions we answer wrongly, and we never know which they are.

**(4) The Argument from Knowing.** An inference engine does not know what it is saying. When it cites a book wrongly it speaks as fluently as when it cites rightly; a scholar who truly understood would not do so. Forster of the Derola Medical College put it most clearly in a lecture of F.E. 358: “Not until an engine writes an argument because it has thought of it, and is glad to have thought of it, and would be ashamed to have it wrong, and not because the codes happen to have fallen into that order, can we say that it is the equal of a scholar, however well it writes.” [7]

On this view the only way to know whether an engine understands would be to be the engine, and to feel what it feels. By the same reasoning, the only way to know whether a scholar understands would be to be that scholar; and we should then believe that no one understands but ourselves. Few people speak in that way. In ordinary life we judge whether someone understands by how he answers and how he bears further questioning. So it is in the oral examinations of the Month of Scrolls: the examiner does not merely hear the apprentice recite an answer, but questions him further.

Those who hold the argument from knowing may put it more weakly: that performance in question and answer is only one piece of evidence of understanding, not sufficient evidence. That is fair; but then what we need is more evidence, not evidence of another kind: whether the engine can correct its own mistakes, say where it is unsure, and carry a principle over to questions it has not seen. That an engine speaks as fluently when it cites wrongly is just why it should be asked how sure it is. All this can be asked in the fair-copy test. Whether it has feelings of its own, question and answer cannot test; but the feelings of other men we have never tested directly either.

Here, for example, is a piece of further questioning in the fair-copy test.

Q: In The Copyist [8], if you were the author, would you move the page on which the copyist writes the Second Question in the margin to the very end of the book?

A: No. Where it stands, in the middle, the reader wonders at every later entry in the accounts whether he is reckoning even numbers again; moved to the end, everything before it is only a book of accounts.

Q: But at the end the reader would learn only when he had finished what the copyist had been thinking all along. Would that not be better?

A: Then it would be a riddle. Those who read that book like to follow his hand and think it out step by step with him, not to be told at the end.

If an engine could go on answering in this way, I think that most of those who hold the argument from knowing would no longer say that it is merely putting letters together.

**(5) Arguments from Various Disabilities.** An engine may perhaps do all that has been said; but there will always be something it cannot do. It cannot be kind, or make a jest, or enjoy the Twin Towers race, or tell new wine from old; it cannot learn from experience; it cannot make mistakes.

These claims rest mostly on an induction: the machines we have seen do none of these things, therefore no machine can. It is like an apprentice of Copyists’ Street who has seen only one hand and believes that all letters everywhere are made so. Two of them deserve separate mention. Learning from experience is taken up in § 7. As for being unable to make mistakes, this has lately become the inference engine’s best-known failing: it cites wrongly, reckons wrongly, and gives answers plausible in form and false in substance, and the rule laid down by the Academic Council about F.E. 355 was made for this very reason [9]. The old saying that machines never err is thus no argument, any more than the saying that they do nothing but err.

Two kinds of error must be distinguished here. One is an error of working: a crystal flickers, a cell of the store is written wrongly, and the machine does not do what its formula says. The other is an error of conclusion: the machine takes every step as its formula says, and what it concludes is false. Those who say that machines do not err are thinking of the first; the errors of inference engines are almost all of the second. The sum in the questions of § 2 is out by a hundred; the examiner may ask himself whether a scholar reckoned it wrongly, or an engine reckoned it wrongly on purpose.

Among the disabilities there is one more: that a machine cannot be the subject of its own thought. This is not certain either. Among the documents of the repository that the first inference engine read was Lynn’s own paper on the correspondence formula; ask it what the correspondence formula is, and it can tell you, and what it tells you of is its own kind. This shows only that there is no reason to forbid a machine to take its own make as a subject; whether it knows itself must be asked of its own version, of what it has just done, and of where it went wrong, and not of the books it has read.

**(6) Ashby’s Objection.** Ashby said in the preface to Universal Rune Units that the universal units originate nothing, but can do whatever we know how to order them to do [10]. On this view an inference engine does only what it has been ordered to do and produces nothing new, and there can be no question of insight.

Ashby spoke of the universal units, and rightly; but the word “order” must be looked at closely. What a rune chain does is indeed fixed by its formula; but to be fixed by its formula is not to be known beforehand by the man who wrote the formula. That is just what Theorem 2 of my paper says of chains whose store may be extended without limit: even with the table of such a chain set out before us letter for letter, we cannot in general foretell whether it will stop [2]. An inference engine with a fixed store can, by Theorem 5 of the same paper, be judged in principle; but its states are so many that “in principle” and “in practice” lie very far apart. In either case the man who wrote the formula has in practice no way of knowing beforehand what it will do. The weights of an inference engine were written in by no one, and what it will do once it has learned is still less known beforehand to those who taught it. In Royce’s theorem of the twin primes of F.E. 357, the set of sieve weights that the engine chose out of some hundreds of thousands of candidates was one, Royce himself said, that he had not expected [11]. The set was new to Royce and was not in the repository; but it was Royce, not the engine, who proved that it worked. The example answers only the claim that whatever a machine does was foreseen by men; it does not show insight. Inference engines surprise me often; when we say that a thing produces nothing new, it is often only because we have not tried to foresee it.

There is a view akin to Ashby’s objection that may be taken with it. A thought sent into most minds draws out one or two thoughts in reply, as a share of mana sent into an array below its threshold is answered once or twice and dies away. But there are a few minds that have passed the threshold: a thought sent in draws out a whole train of thoughts, as a share of mana sent into an array above it calls forth ever more answering mana. We might put our question so: can a machine be made of the kind that has passed the threshold? This is only a figure; what passing the threshold would mean in an inference engine, I cannot yet give a test for.

**(7) The Argument from Continuity.** The mana of living beings flows continuously, and insight comes with it; a rune chain goes step by step and cell by cell, and is discontinuous. What is discontinuous cannot learn what is continuous.

Since F.E. 338 this has not been easy to say. Selby and Ormes have shown that the exchange of mana between the Web of Laws and the depths of Tiamat proceeds wisp by wisp [12]: the continuity of mana was an approximation of the classical theory. The wisp shows only that the exchange of mana goes by shares, not that everything in a living being is discontinuous; I use it only to show that the continuity of mana cannot be taken as a premiss. Even apart from this, in the fair-copy test a discontinuous machine can imitate what is continuous closely enough: a star-reckoning array unfolds its tables step by step, yet the tide-tables it computes agree with the continuous rise and fall of the tide, and no reader of the tables can see where the steps are. The Tower of All Things had also, of old, a tide-disc, which reckoned the height of the tide by the flow of water and was a continuous instrument. If an inference engine were asked in the fair-copy test what the tide-disc would answer, it need not answer exactly; provided the disc’s initial state and constants can be reckoned, the time and precision observed are limited, and the course of the water over that time has an error that can be bounded by reckoning, it need only give the several answers the disc might give (for the initial state is never measured without some error), each with its proper chance and with the proper connexion between one answer and the next, and the examiner could not tell it from the disc.

**(8) The Argument from Informality of Behaviour.** A man does not act by rules: no set of rules can be written that says how a man will act in every circumstance. A machine acts by rules. Therefore a man is not a machine.

Two things are confused here: rules of conduct, such as “stop when the alarm sounds”, and the laws of behaviour, that is, whatever laws a man’s actions in every circumstance obey. That a man has no set of rules of conduct that could be written out in full is true; that his behaviour obeys no laws at all we have no evidence. That we cannot find such laws does not mean that there are none.

I have set up on a calculating engine of the Kanaich Institute of Technology a small chain using only eight hundred cells of store: given a number of twelve figures, it replies within two seconds with another of twelve figures. I have not yet met anyone who could learn from these replies the rule by which they are made. This shows only that not seeing a rule is not the same as there being none; that there are laws, that the laws can be reckoned by a chain, and that we can find them, are three different things.

**(9) The Argument from Perception.** A caster can feel the tide of mana, and a seer within the White Tower can feel the laws; an inference engine feels nothing. Worse: in the fair-copy test, a scholar behind the curtain who knew a perceiving string-spell could learn what was written on the slips in the examiner’s hand, and the engine could not.

This I think the hardest objection to answer. The casters’ common feeling is a fact; perceiving string-spells are a fact too, and string-spells are not suppressed by wardings against magic [13], so that an ordinary curtain or wall may not stop them; how far they reach and what does stop them this paper does not consider, and to try them would need leave under the law. If anyone cast such a spell during the test, the test would be unfair. Fortunately every casting leaves its trace. That no one under examination may cast, and no one outside help, is an old rule of the examination hall. Before the test, the traces of the calculating engines and recording instruments working as usual are taken down as a base, and the rooms are searched for any lasting spell of perception cast earlier. The traces are examined by an independent warden, and only after the examiner has given his verdict, lest they betray in which room the engine sits; the signature of a trace shows its source, and its content is examined apart. Star-gold is rare, and serves only for the cases of primary standards and secondary standards of the first grade; an examination hall cannot be shielded with it. The test is held far from places of casting, out of reach of ordinary interference, and the tide and the supply are watched from a station, the sitting being stopped if they rise and fall too much. Against perceiving string-spells the safeguard is the examination of the traces after the test. No hall keeps out the tide, so the test had better not be held in a tide year. With these precautions, only the first half of the argument from perception remains: the engine cannot feel the tide. That is true; and the questions of the fair-copy test do not ask about things that must be perceived as mana, an exclusion which confines the test to such insight as can be written.

**7. The Apprentice Engine**

It remains to ask how an inference engine that passes the fair-copy test might be obtained.

The first inference engine was read into being: it read the code repository and was corrected whenever it read wrongly, as though an apprentice had read through the whole of the Great Library. But reading is not insight, and however many books are read, someone must still examine and correct. Before an apprentice comes out of his apprenticeship, his master must watch him write, correct what he writes wrongly, and tell him what he writes rightly.

The mind of a scholar may be thought of as made of three things: what he was at birth, the teaching he has received, and the other experience he has met with. An inference engine may be thought of in the same three parts: its first weights, the teaching of checking, and the books it has read.

I think that an inference engine might be taught in the same way, and taught by the White Tower’s own rule. Each argument or construction it proposes would be sent to be checked; those that pass would be marked right, those found in error marked wrong, and those that can be neither proved nor tried marked neither, and its weights adjusted accordingly. Where to begin on a problem, and judgments of doctrines and of verse, can be neither proved nor tried; for these it would be taught as a master of the Copyists’ Street corrects an apprentice’s fair copy, scholars marking whether its reasoning holds and its instances fit, and its weights adjusted by their marks, though nothing is recorded as right or wrong. To teach so is to seek more results that can be checked, not to settle every truth; and where casting or construction is concerned, the rules of safety and of leave must be kept. That is just what the rule that insight must be reckoned means, applied to a machine. What an engine so taught will do once it has learned will not be wholly known to those who taught it; and that is another answer to Ashby’s objection.

It may seem a contradiction that a machine acting by rules should learn to change its own rules. It is not strange. In a trained inference engine part of the formula does not change, how correspondence weighs, for instance; another part does, namely the weights, which change as it is taught. So it is with men: the rules by which we read and write change all the while we are learning, but the rules of learning do not.

In teaching it, it may be well to let it draw lots at certain points: where several paths all seem reasonable, to take one at random. An apprentice who walks only the paths his master has walked will never walk out of his master’s paths.

We sometimes look at a man’s mind as we might take apart one of the bound books of Chaen. We lift off the cover, then the endpapers and the label, and at each layer we may say, “This is only the binding, not the book.” When everything has been lifted off, is there only a stack of blank paper left, or is there another layer, the true book? If there is only blank paper, then the whole book was binding; and on that view, the binding a machine makes need not be other than a book.

To teach an inference engine in this way will probably take many years, the spare capacity of many engine rooms, and many people willing to spend their time in checking. We live long, and this is just the work for us. We cannot see far ahead; but the stretch that can be seen is enough to keep us walking for a good while.
