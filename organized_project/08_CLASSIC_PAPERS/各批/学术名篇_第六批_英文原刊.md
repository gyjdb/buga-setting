# 《联盟学术名篇》英文原刊（第六批）

## Signatures of Traces

**SIGNATURES OF TRACES**

By OXLEY, Kanaich Institute of Technology.

[Received in the Month of Autumn Dusk, F.E. 330.]

**Abstract.** A casting trace bears the mana pattern of its caster, and adjudication and checking have always known the caster by the pattern; but a higher law can imitate the pattern of a lower one, and the pattern alone cannot tell the true from the false. The two other methods now in use, shared-key check-marks and witnesses with sealing wax, each have their faults: whoever can check a mark can make it, or the witness cannot be present. This paper proposes trace signatures. Every caster and every calculating engine holds a private key, and enters a public seal in the register of public seals; when a trace is formed, a seal-box takes the digest of the trace, reckons a signature from it with the private key, and attaches the signature to the trace. Anyone can check a signature by the public seal, and every road to forgery analysed here requires first solving a question of arithmetic. The signature uses arithmetic alone and owes nothing to law; its safety rests upon an irreversible reckoning, in which the power-remainder is easy to reckon and the index is hard to find. By the fastest method of finding the index now known, all the engines of the League reckoning jointly would need a hundred thousand million years to recover the private key from a public seal of a hundred and fifty figures. The results of engines in other cities, signed layer by layer at each place, can be traced back to their input, to the version of the formula, and to the records of their checking. A signature proves from whose seal-box a trace came and which version of the formula it records; whether the content of the trace is true must still be examined apart. Following the custom of this journal, the universal units for signing and checking are appended.

**1 Introduction**

The casting trace is the first-rank evidence of adjudication. About C.R. 1130, after a wrongful judgment in a case of forbidden arts, Houghton revised the rules of evidence so that whenever an act of casting is to be established the trace is looked at first [1]; the trace is trusted because it bears the mana pattern peculiar to its caster. The acceptance of work in the workshops, the records of experiments, the inputs and outputs of calculating engines are all kept as traces in the same manner, for checking elsewhere [2].

Two things have lately made the pattern alone insufficient.

The first is that patterns can be imitated. About C.R. 1150 two limits were derived from nomological algebra, one of which says that a higher law can imitate a lower law, but not the reverse [3]. A pattern is one of the ways in which a law shows itself; a caster commanding a law higher than another’s can therefore cast a trace bearing the other’s pattern. This has long been known; but in former times very few could do it, and the courts had witnesses and material evidence to compare. Now that casting passes more and more through engines, and traces are more and more removed from the scene, the cases in which a trace is accepted on its pattern alone have multiplied. Of the false traces sent for re-examination by the examiners of Kanaich in the last twenty years, eleven in all, seven were made by imitating another’s pattern with a higher law, and in two of these judgment had already been given. These are only the cases sent for re-examination, and cannot measure how many false traces there are; but they show where the trouble lies.

The second is that reckoning has left the hands of men. Since Fenn built the speaking network [4], a great reckoning is often divided among the engines of several cities, and the results are sent back over the network [2]. The man who wants the reckoning cannot see the engine; whether the numbers that come back were reckoned by that engine from the input given and by the formula given, he can only take on trust from the engine room of that city, and from everyone who relayed them on the way. Cummings has shown that no rune chain can judge the outcome of every rune chain [5]; a finished reckoning can of course be reckoned again or its record checked step by step, and restricted formulae can be verified in the manner followed since Finley, but all of this requires first knowing by whom, from what, and how it was reckoned.

Both things want the same: a mark which anyone can check to have come from a given person, and which no one but that person can make; and its trustworthiness must not depend on the rank of laws. The trace signature proposed here is such a mark. It uses arithmetic alone: no law, however high, makes a question of arithmetic easier to reckon.

Section 2 describes the three methods now in use and their faults; §3 the five conditions a signature must meet; §4 the irreversible reckoning on which it rests; §§5–6 the signature and the digest of a trace; §7 the seal-box, the register of public seals, revocation and attestation; §8 the chain of signatures for engines in other cities; §9 estimates the cost and the strength; §10 the relation of signatures to the Sixth of the Seven Millennial Problems; §11 what a signature does not prove; §12 concludes.

**2 The three methods now in use**

**2.1 Examination of patterns** The examiner compares the pattern of a trace, point by point, with the registered records of formulae and with the samples the caster left at the register [1]. This is the foundation of adjudication as it stands, and is very reliable against ordinary casters; against a forger commanding a higher law it cannot discriminate, as was said in §1. It also needs an examiner to do it with his own hands, and the outputs of engines and the records sent back over the network mostly have no pattern to compare.

**2.2 Shared-key check-marks** Rune messages on the speaking network are now mostly protected by shared-key check-marks. The two parties agree beforehand on a secret string of numbers, the shared key; the sender compresses the message together with the shared key into a check-mark, and appends it; the receiver compresses again in the same way, and if the two check-marks are alike he knows that the message has not been altered, and that it came from a holder of the shared key. Check-marks have two faults. First, whoever can check can make: the receiver holds the shared key too, and can make the same check-mark himself, so that a check-mark can make the two parties trust each other, but cannot be laid before a third party as evidence. To use it in adjudication a court would have to agree a shared key with every caster, and anyone in the court who could reach the key could make that caster’s check-mark. Secondly, the shared key must be delivered beforehand: if the parties have never met, the key must be carried by hand, since sent over the network it may be intercepted. The League has some millions of casters and the courts and engine rooms of some tens of cities; to agree keys pair by pair, the number of keys grows as the square of the number of persons.

**2.3 Witnesses and sealing wax** For important castings a witness is asked to be present, and afterwards signs the record and presses a seal in wax. Seals in wax can be imitated, and witnesses cannot stand before every calculating engine.

**3 Five conditions**

A signature must therefore meet five conditions:

(i) anyone can check it from public things alone, holding no secret;

(ii) no one without the private key can make it, and this holds also for those who can check it;

(iii) signer and checker need not meet beforehand, nor send each other any secret;

(iv) signing and checking are both quick: for a short trace, a fraction of a second on one way of an ordinary engine, with the work of compression added for a long one (§9);

(v) its trustworthiness rests on arithmetic alone, not on the rank of laws, nor on the honesty of the checker.

The first and second together are exactly what shared-key check-marks cannot do: in a check-mark, the secret used for checking is the same as the secret used for making. A signature must separate the two, signing with a private key and checking with a public seal, while the private key cannot be got from the public seal. The next section shows how this can be done.

**4 Irreversible reckonings**

**4.1 Definition** Take as the measure of size the number $n$ of figures of $p$. Let the public numbers grow with $n$, and let the private key be taken uniformly at random within a prescribed range. A reckoning is called irreversible when the number of steps needed to reckon it forward grows as some power of $n$, while, for a result reckoned from a randomly taken key, the number of steps needed by the best method now known to find any number that gives the same result grows faster than every power. In this sense irreversibility is only relative to the methods now known; it is an estimate of the cost of the existing methods, not a lower bound below which no method can go. No one has yet proved any reckoning to be truly irreversible; this bears on the Sixth of the Seven Millennial Problems, of which more in §10.

**4.2 The power-remainder** Take a large prime $p$ such that $q = (p - 1) \/ 2$ is also prime, and a number $g$, not one, with $g^q equiv 1 (mod p)$, so that the powers of $g$ leave exactly $q$ different remainders on division by $p$. Whether $p$ and $q$ are prime, and whether $g$ meets these conditions, is checked before witnesses when the register of public seals is opened. For $x$ between 1 and $q - 1$, put

$$ y = g^x mod p $$ (1)

To reckon $y$ from $x$ is called taking the power-remainder. It is quick: write $x$ in binary figures and, from the highest figure down, at each figure first multiply the number in hand by itself and, if the figure is one, multiply once more by $g$, keeping each time only the remainder on division by $p$; at most twice as many multiplications are needed as $x$ has binary figures, and one and a half times on the average. To find $x$ again from $y$ is called finding the index. By the methods now known it is far harder:

(a) to try every value takes $q$ steps;

(b) the method of great and small steps of Hollis [6]: take $k$ a whole number not less than $sqrt(q)$, first store the small steps $g^0, g^1, dots, g^(k - 1)$, then, starting from $y$, multiply step by step by the great step $g^(-k)$ until one meets a stored small step; both steps and storage are about $sqrt(q)$;

(c) the method of a base of small primes of Spooner [7]: since the small primes need not be powers of $g$, one first takes a generator $h$ of the whole group of remainders, of which $g$ is the square; first, many relations among remainders involving only small primes are gathered; secondly, a set of linear equations modulo $q$ is solved, giving the indices of these small primes to the base $h$; thirdly, $y$ is resolved upon the small primes, its index to the base $h$ found, and this divided by two modulo $q$ gives the index to the base $g$. The steps needed are about

$$ exp(sqrt(2 ln p dot ln ln p)) $$ (2)

As the figures of $p$ grow, (2) grows faster than every power, yet far more slowly than the $sqrt(q)$ of (b); it is the fastest now known.

If the order of $g$ has only small prime factors, the method of division of Salter [8] breaks the finding of the index into many small findings and is soon done. To avoid it the order of $g$ must be a large prime; taking $p = 2 q + 1$ is one way of meeting this condition, and it also makes possible the replacement of §6.

**5 Public seals and private keys**

**5.1 Signing and checking** Let the public $p$, $q$, $g$ and $f$ ($f$ is described in §6) be fixed. Each signer takes a random number $x$ between 1 and $q - 1$, called his private key, and reckons $y$ by (1), called his public seal. The private key exists only in the signer’s seal-box; the public seal is entered in the register of public seals (§7).

To sign a trace whose content is $M$, first reckon its digest $h = H(M)$ by §6. Then:

1. take a new random number $k$ between 1 and $q - 1$, called the transient number;
2. reckon $r = g^k mod p$;
3. reckon $e = H(r, h) mod q$, that is, fill $r$ out with noughts in front to the number of figures of $p$, set it before $h$, take the digest, and reduce it modulo $q$; call it the answering number;
4. reckon $s = k + x e mod q$, called the signing number.

The signature is the pair of numbers $(e, s)$, and is attached to the trace (Fig. 1).

To check it one needs only the public $p$, $q$, $g$ and $f$, the signer’s public seal $y$, the content $M$ of the trace and the signature $(e, s)$. First see that $e$ and $s$ both lie between 0 and $q - 1$, and that $y$ lies between 2 and $p - 1$ and $y^q equiv 1 (mod p)$; if any of these fails, the signature does not pass. Then reckon $h = H(M)$ and

$$ r' = g^s dot y^(-e) mod p $$ (3)

If $H(r', h) mod q = e$, the signature has passed.

Fig. 1. Signing and checking: on the left, signing, the private key being only in the seal-box; on the right, checking, which uses public things only.

LEMMA 1. A signature made as in §5.1 always passes.

Proof. The exponents are reduced modulo $q$, and the powers and products taken modulo $p$. $g^s y^(-e) = g^(k + x e) g^(-x e) = g^k = r$, since the $q$-th power of $g$ leaves the remainder one; so $r' = r$, and the answering numbers on the two sides are the same.

Compression apart, to sign once takes one power-remainder; to check once, two.

**5.2 Who can make one** One without the private key who would make $(e, s)$ for a new trace has three kinds of means, so far as analysed here. The first is to find the private key $x$, that is, to find the index of $y$. The second is to find two different contents with the same digest, and move the signature of one, which someone has signed, onto the other; Section 6 proves that this too comes down to finding an index. The third is to take $e$ and $s$ at will, reckon $r'$, and see whether $H(r', h)$ happens to equal $e$; so long as the output of the digest follows no rule, this is mere trial, needing some $q$ trials on the average. A forger may also see many genuine signatures, and even trick the signer into signing contents of his own choosing; whether signatures remain trustworthy in these cases I have not proved, and can say only that no faster means is known to me. The safety of signatures is offered here as a conjecture to be tested; the hundred thousand million years of §9 are only an estimate for recovering the private key by the methods listed, not a lower bound for every means of forgery.

**5.3 The transient number** The transient number must not be used twice, nor be guessable.

LEMMA 2. If one private key with one transient number has signed two traces, with answering numbers not congruent modulo $q$, $e_1 != e_2$, then

$$ x = (s_1 - s_2) \/ (e_1 - e_2) mod q $$ (4)

Proof. $s_1 - s_2 equiv x (e_1 - e_2) (mod q)$; $q$ is prime and $e_1 - e_2$ is not nought modulo $q$, so that one may divide.

The transient number must therefore be drawn on the spot by the drawing piece within the seal-box, and thrown away when used, neither stored nor recorded. The drawing piece reads the trembling of a fine crystal under mana, taking only the last few figures of each reading; readings are taken one after another until there are more than twice as many figures as $q$ has, and the number is then reduced modulo $q$, so that the bias is too small to count. We took a hundred million numbers from each of ten drawing pieces of this Institute and tested them for bias in several groupings and for dependence between neighbours, at ordinary warmth, after restarting, and with casting nearby, and could find no fault; the tests and their statistics are given in the supplement. If the drawing piece gives no reading, or repeats one, the seal-box stops signing. Since the transient number is drawn at random, the chance that two of $N$ signatures happen to share it is about $N^2 \/ (2 q)$, which at the figures of §9 may be neglected. If the transient number can be guessed, the private key can be solved for after the manner of (4).

**5.4 A side remark: agreeing a shared key in the open** The same reckoning has one more use, not treated at length here. Two persons who have never met take, the one $a$, the other $b$, and send $g^a$ and $g^b$; the first raises the other’s number to the power $a$, the second the other’s to the power $b$, and both obtain $g^(a b)$, which can serve as their shared key. One who intercepts the two numbers can reckon $g^(a b)$ if he can find an index; whether reckoning it must go through finding an index, no one has proved. This guards only against eavesdropping, not against a relayer who impersonates: he may replace both numbers with his own, and agree a separate key with each party. Against that, the numbers of both parties must be sent signed, together with their identities.

**6 The digest of a trace**

The content of a trace is long: the record of the pattern, the time, the place, the version of the formula used, the input and output of an engine. What is signed is its digest, a number of fixed length reckoned from the content. The digest must meet two conditions: from the digest no content agreeing with it can be found; nor can two different contents with the same digest be found. The latter is called freedom from collision.

We compress by the power-remainder. Besides $g$, take a public number $f$ lying in the group of remainders generated by $g$, but whose index to the base $g$ no one has handed over: on the day the register is opened, numbers are cast before witnesses to give a remainder $u$, not nought, and $f = u^2 mod p$ is taken, the cast being repeated if $f$ is one; the whole cast is written at the head of the register. This shows only that no index appeared in the fixing of $f$, not that no one knows it.

The content is written in decimal figures and divided from the beginning into pieces of $m$ figures, $m$ being taken so that $10^m$ is less than $q$; the last piece, if short, is filled out with noughts in front, and one further piece records the total number of figures of the content, which therefore may not reach $10^m$. Every piece then has a value between 0 and $q - 1$; call them $M_1, M_2, dots, M_n$. Put $c_0 = 1$ and

$$ c_i = g^(c_(i - 1)) dot f^(M_i) mod p $$ (5)

where $c_(i - 1)$, if greater than $q$, is first replaced by $p - c_(i - 1)$. Every $c$ lies in the group generated by $g$; since $q$ is odd, minus one is not in that group, so that $c$ and $p - c$ are never both in it, the numbers so replaced never exceed $q$, and different $c$ give different numbers. The last $c_n$, so replaced, is the digest.

LEMMA 3. Whoever finds two different contents with the same digest can find the index of $f$ to the base $g$.

Proof. Let the two contents be compressed piece by piece into $c_i$ and $c'_i$. The last pieces record the total numbers of figures, so that if these differ the last pieces differ. Since the digests agree and the replacement is one-to-one, the last $c$ of the two chains agree; going back step by step from the end of each chain, find the first place where the $c$ agree but $(c_(i - 1), M_i) != (c'_(j - 1), M'_j)$; since the contents differ, there is such a place. Let $a$ and $a'$ be $c_(i - 1)$ and $c'_(j - 1)$ after replacement, so that $g^a f^(M_i) = g^(a') f^(M'_j)$. If $M_i = M'_j$, then $g^a = g^(a')$; $a$ and $a'$ do not exceed $q$, so they are equal, contrary to supposition. Hence $M_i != M'_j$; both lie between 0 and $q - 1$, so that their difference is not nought modulo $q$, and the index of $f$ to the base $g$ is $(a - a') \/ (M'_j - M_i) mod q$.

Freedom from collision therefore also rests upon finding the index: whoever can make a collision of digests has found an index. Compression takes two power-remainders for each piece and is slower than signing. A compression of the pieces in parallel would be a different digest, not covered by Lemma 3, and is not used here.

**7 The seal-box, the register of public seals, revocation and attestation**

**7.1 The seal-box** The private key is kept in the seal-box and never leaves it. The seal-box is a small instrument carried on the person, containing the wafer that holds the private key, a drawing piece and a reckoning unit. For each signature the holder must open the seal-box with a pass-word; after three wrong attempts the seal-box locks, and the holder must go in person to the register office to have it reopened. When the caster casts, or an engine writes a record, the seal-box takes the digest of the version directly from the formula being cast or executed, through a sealed interface, records it together with the content of the trace, signs both, and notes in the signature “taken through the interface”; text passed into the seal-box at will is signed too, but without that note. The case of the seal-box carries a ward, which wipes out the private key if the box is forced. That ward guards against opening the box to read the key; one who steals the whole seal-box and knows the pass-word can sign falsely, so the pass-word must be kept in the head, not written on the box.

**7.2 The register of public seals** A public seal must answer to a person, or a forger need only make another pair of private key and public seal and register them in another’s name, to sign “another’s” signatures. The registration of a public seal must therefore be made in person, like the oath register: the signer brings his seal-box to the oath-register office; the registrar establishes his identity by the original roll of the oath register, its certificates, the trace of their making and any later changes, the identity being determined by the registering authority according to law and not by pattern alone; he has him sign before him, with the seal-box, a content given by the office, checks it, and enters in the register of public seals the name, the oath-register number, the number of the public seal, the set of $p$, $q$, $g$ and $f$ used, the public seal, and the days from which and until which it holds; and the office then signs that entry with its own private key. The public seals of the register offices are printed each year in the Gazette of the League, and the original of the Gazette kept by the courts is the starting point of trust. Anyone who holds a trace, then, first checks the signature of the trace and then the signature of the entry in the register, and knows who signed. The seal of an engine is registered on its behalf by the body that keeps the engine room. A change of key is made by registering a new public seal, the old entry being marked with the day it ends; if a register office loses its own private key, it is revoked in the same way, and the office above it signs the new entry.

**7.3 Revocation** If a seal-box is lost, or the private key is suspected to have leaked, the holder asks the register office to revoke it; the office records the time and prints the revocation in the Gazette. Signatures made after revocation are not accepted. The checker must therefore obtain a sufficiently recent record of revocations, and may not rely on an out-of-date Gazette.

**7.4 Attestation** One who has stolen a seal-box might still write the time of a signature earlier than it was. Against this I propose two things. First, that the speaking network send a time signal every hour, itself signed, and that every trace record the latest time signal at the moment of signing; this shows only that the signature is no earlier than that time signal. Secondly, that the digest of a signed trace, together with its signature and the number of the public seal, be sent promptly to the time office, which signs a receipt stating the time at which it received them; this shows that the signature is no later than the time of the receipt. Together, the time of the signature is held between two times. Where these enclose the time of a revocation, the trace is examined apart, under the third head of §11.

**8 The chain of signatures for engines in other cities**

A reckoning in another city is signed thus (Fig. 2). The party A that wants it gives the reckoning a number, writes the number, the input $I$ and the version $V$ of the formula to be used into one content, signs it with his private key, and sends it to the engine room of city B. B’s engine reckons the output $O$ by $V$, and at the same time records $K$, the record of its checking — the reckonings made at two places, the checks used, the bounds of error; the records of the reckonings at the two places are signed by each place separately, and $K$ only cites them, not reported by one place for the other. B then signs with its engine seal a content made by joining the number, $I$, $V$, $O$, $K$ and A’s signature. In a joint reckoning each part is signed in this way by the engine that reckoned it, noting which part it is and on which parts it depends; the engine room presiding over the joint reckoning signs the result it has combined, together with the signatures of the parts, and the final digest is sent for attestation as in §7.4.

A result so signed can be checked by anyone layer by layer: from which engine room the final result came; which partial results it combined; by which engine, from which input, by which version of the formula each was reckoned; what checks were made at each place; and who signed the first input. A signature does not warrant that a reckoning is right. Where the records are complete and can be reckoned again independently, if it is wrong, one can find at which place and by which version of the formula it went wrong; and no one can afterwards alter any one layer without its being detected. If one layer of signatures is missing, the chain as a whole does not stand, but the signatures of the layers below still prove their own sources.

The chain of signatures for engines in other cities: A signs the input; the engines of cities B and C each sign the part they reckoned; the engine room presiding over the joint reckoning signs the combined result; each layer joins the signatures of the layer below into the content it signs.

**9 Cost and strength**

Table 2 gives, for primes of different numbers of figures, the time to sign and to check, and the time of the two methods of finding the index. Signing and checking are reckoned on one way of the engine room of this Institute, compression apart: the four rows from forty to a hundred figures were measured, each by a thousand signings and checkings averaged, and the rest extrapolated as the cube of the number of figures, since a multiplication with remainder grows as the square of the number of figures, and a power-remainder needs a number of such multiplications in proportion to it. Compression takes two power-remainders for each piece; a trace of a thousand figures, at a hundred and fifty, takes about a second and a half. Finding the index is reckoned on all the engines of the League reckoning jointly: by the count of the Seventh Division this year, the engines registered in all the cities of the League together make some two thousand four hundred million multiplications with remainder of a hundred figures every second. The two columns for finding the index assume ample storage and perfect parallel working, and count every step of (2) as one multiplication with remainder, neglecting its constant and giving only the order of magnitude; they are an ideal budget of work, not a lower bound of safety. In practice the method of great and small steps must also store some $sqrt(q)$ numbers, and the step of solving the linear equations in the method of a base of small primes cannot be wholly divided among ways. There is a further point: when the whole League shares one set of $p$ and $g$, the first two steps of the method of a base of small primes need be done only once, after which the private key of each public seal is found much faster; this is one reason for changing the set of $p$ and $g$ every twelve years.

From a hundred figures to two hundred, for example, signing and checking become some eight times slower, while finding the index by the method of a base of small primes becomes three hundred thousand million times slower; on this alone the possibility of signatures depends.

How many figures to take depends on how long a signature must hold. The power of calculating engines has grown some tenfold every ten years for the last thirty years, and the methods of finding the index may be improved. The Silver Folk are not counted old until two hundred and ten, and the traces of a case or of a contract may be brought out for re-examination after some hundreds of years. Were the power of engines to go on growing tenfold every ten years, the hundred thousand million years of budget at a hundred and fifty figures would be spent in about a century, and that at two hundred figures in about a century and a half; this is a supposition, not a prophecy, but it is enough to show that the matter cannot be settled once for all. I propose that ordinary traces take a hundred and fifty figures; that the register of public seals, the public seals of the register offices and the original records of trials in court take two hundred; that these be only a starting point, the First Division re-estimating every twelve years by the power and the methods of the time; and that, when the figures are about to become insufficient, the traces be signed afresh at the new figures while the old signatures can still be relied upon. The new signature must cover the original content, the old signatures, the entry in the register and the receipts of attestation, and the record of their checking at the time of re-signing must be kept. A signature added after the old one has become forgeable cannot restore the truth of what went before.

**10 Signatures and the question of verification and construction**

The Sixth of the Seven Millennial Problems asks whether, for a hard problem in which a rune chain, once given, can be quickly verified, the length of the chain growing only moderately with the size of the problem, such a chain can always be quickly constructed as well [9]. Forging a signature can be written as such a problem: a short signature, once given, is checked by two power-remainders; to forge, one must construct it. If the answer to the Sixth Problem were “yes”, with a practicable method of construction, signatures could no longer be kept safe. Conversely, even an answer of “no” would not by itself make the power-remainder irreversible: the Sixth Problem asks whether any problems of this kind are hard, while signatures need a randomly taken public seal to be hard against every effective means in the ordinary case. Among randomly taken private keys a few are weak — with $x = 1$, $y$ is simply $g$ — but their proportion may be neglected. The trustworthiness of signatures rests, in the end, only on what is “now known”. What we can do is to make it rest on a question that many have tried and none has yet solved. The finding of the index has been attempted since the reckoners of the classical age [6].

**11 What a signature does not prove**

A signature proves that the content of a trace, at the time of signing, came from the seal-box holding the private key that answers to a certain public seal; where it bears the note “taken through the interface”, it proves also that the version recorded in the content was taken by the seal-box from the formula being cast or executed. It does not prove the following.

(i) It does not prove the content true. A signer may cast a false formula and have it leave a true signature; whether the content of a trace is true, and how the act of casting and the responsibility for it are to be judged, remain matters for examination and trial.

(ii) It does not prove that the signer acted of his own will. A seal-box signs for a man under duress as well.

(iii) It does not prove that the private key was not stolen. In arithmetic, no law however high makes the index easier to find; but the ward of a seal-box, which keeps out ordinary casters, may not keep out a higher law, and one who has stolen a seal-box and knows its pass-word can sign falsely. That a seal has not been revoked does not mean that its key never leaked. The safety of the private key rests in the end on the seal-box never leaving the person and the pass-word never being told, and on revocation as soon as either is lost.

(iv) It does not reach back. Traces formed before signatures came into use have no signatures, and are still to be examined under the old rules, by the record of their collection, the course of their keeping and the findings of examination; they may not be refused merely for having no signature.

Checking a signature is only one step towards accepting a trace. The warrant for collection, the chain of keeping and cross-checking all remain as required since Houghton.

**12 Conclusion**

The marks by which men are known, from sealing wax to the pattern, have always been things hard to imitate: the wax wants the die, the pattern wants the man’s law. What is hard to imitate can always be imitated by someone. A signature goes another way: it is not hard to write; what is hard is to reckon it without knowing the private key; and anyone can check it. To trust from whom a trace came and which version of the formula it records, then, one need no longer rely on the pattern alone being hard to imitate, nor on the honesty of everyone who relayed it on the network, but on a question of arithmetic that has not yet been solved, together with the registration of public seals, the keeping of seal-boxes and attestation. The rest of the examination must be made as before.

## On a Lower Bound for the Landing of Deflected Translation

**ON A LOWER BOUND FOR THE LANDING OF DEFLECTED TRANSLATION**

By HARDING, White Tower University.

[Received in the Month of Autumn Dusk, F.E. 357. With three figures.]

**Summary.** Deflected translation projects the caster into the sea of mana and returns him at another place, and no one has ever reckoned its landing exactly. This paper first asks what it means to reckon the landing beforehand: only what can be had before the translation is cast may be used, and the condition of the sea at the point of entry can be learned only by exchanging mana with it. Under a bridging premise, that the exchanges at the point of entry also obey the relation of raising and lowering of Selby and Ormes, the place and the current of the sea at one point cannot both have definite values, the product of their spreads being not less than half a wisp; the part of the landing along the current depends on the place at the point of entry, the part across it on the current there, and both are magnified by the passage through the sea. It is proved that, under the tested conditions of the model, for a given planned reach and phase of the tide, every landing reckoned only from what can be had before the casting has a mean error not less than $B$, namely $sqrt(0.92 epsilon a b)$, where $epsilon$ is the wisp, $a$ and $b$ are the two magnifications of the passage, and 0.92 is an allowance for the terms the model neglects. The premises of the model are taken from more than seven thousand nine hundred old traces in the Register of Translation Traces of the White Tower, and tested independently on more than two thousand three hundred signed traces formed since F.E. 331. The latter half of the paper gives a method of locating that approaches the bound, namely a balanced sounding of the sea before the casting; in a hundred and eighty-four drills by twelve law-mages, the ratio of the mean error to $B$ did not exceed 1.2 in any band. The distribution of landings is also given. The formal proof of the theorem has more than one million and forty thousand derivations, checked one by one by a verifier independent of the author and of the inference engine; what the verifier confirms is that the conclusion holds upon the model, while the model itself rests upon the test of the traces.

**Introduction**

Severin’s Eighteenth Question asks: “Can the landing of deflected translation be reckoned beforehand?” And he goes on: can the exact landing be reckoned from what is known before the casting alone; and if it cannot, how small at least can the error be made [1]? More than a thousand years after the question was put, no one has yet reckoned it exactly. In the classical age some reckoned from the phase of the tide and the place of setting out; the landings so reckoned commonly missed by more than three tenths of the reach, and sometimes fell far off [2].

To answer the question one must first make clear what “reckoning beforehand” means. Whether a landing can be reckoned depends on what one holds when reckoning. Deflected translation first projects the caster into the sea of mana, and then returns him to the world at another place [3]; where he returns depends on his passage through the sea, and what the sea is like at the point of entry cannot be seen before the casting. There is only one way to learn it: to exchange mana with it and read its answer. Selby and Ormes proved that every exchange between the web and the depths is a whole number of wisps [4]. “What can be had before the casting” comes down, then, to the readings obtained by exchange with the sea before the casting, together with the place and time of setting out, the phase of the tide and the force of projection the caster intends. The question of this paper is: with these, how exactly at most can the landing be reckoned?

The answer comes in two parts. The first is a relation: the place and the current of the sea at one point — the position of the point of entry within the current of the sea, and the strength of the cross-current there — cannot both have definite values, and the product of their spreads is not less than half a wisp (§2). The second is the joining of this relation with the passage through the sea: the part of the landing along the current depends on the place, the part across it on the current, and both are magnified by the passage, the more so as the reach grows; so that the mean error of the landing has a lower bound that cannot be removed (§3, Theorem 1). The bound depends neither on the skill of the caster nor on the fineness of instruments, but only on the size of the wisp and on the passage through the sea.

The proof of the bound uses several premises of a model, and these are taken from traces. The Register of Translation Traces of the White Tower records the setting out and landing of deflected translations from C.R. 1190, and to F.E. 330 holds seven thousand nine hundred and eighty-six usable entries [5]; from these I fixed the model, and I tested it independently on two thousand three hundred and seventeen signed traces formed after signatures came into use in 331 [6] (§6). The formal proof of the theorem is long; with the help of an inference engine it was set out as more than one million and forty thousand derivations, and given to an independent verifier to check one by one (Appendix).

Section 1 defines the concepts used; §2 gives the relation of place and current; §3 proves the bound; §4 treats short and long reaches; §5 discusses some methods; §6 tests the premises; §7 gives the method of locating; §8 the distribution of landings.

**1. Some concepts: the landing, the error, and what is known before casting**

**1.1. Setting out, landing and the cut-off** By “the casting” I mean here the deflected translation alone, not the sounding or any other spell. A deflected translation has a place of setting out $X$ and a time $t$, and a landing $L$. The cut-off is the moment the projection begins: whatever is had before it may be used, whatever is formed after it may not, and the reckoned point must be sealed at the cut-off. Before the cut-off one can read: the place and time of setting out; the phase of the ten-year tide and of the daily rise and fall; the bearing and brightness of the twelve magic moons, which are the reflection of the sea of mana in the sky [7]; the force of projection the caster intends; and any readings of soundings made before the cut-off (§1.3). The actual force of projection recorded afterwards in the trace of setting out is not knowledge had before the casting, since the projection may deepen unintentionally at entry (§8). The greater the force, the farther the landing as a rule; but neither its direction nor its distance is fixed by the caster [3].

**1.2. The reckoned point, the error and the reach** Let $I$ be all that can be had before the cut-off, and $f$ any means of reckoning a point from $I$; call $f(I)$ the reckoned point. The error $delta = L - f(I)$ is a vector in the plane. By the mean error I mean the square root of the mean of the square of $|delta|$; if only the mean of the distance is taken, the conclusion is the same, with a smaller constant (§3.4). The planned reach $R$ is the distance of the mean landing reckoned by the model from the intended force of projection; it does not change with the means of reckoning being compared. The theorem compares all means of reckoning at a given $R$ and phase of the tide; the degenerate case $R = 0$ is not treated.

**1.3. Sounding the sea** To learn the condition of the sea before casting, a low-ring sounding of the sea may be cast at the place of setting out: the sounding reaches down to the sea of mana by the caster’s own mana, exchanges some wisps with it, and reads back the answer of the sea at that point. This is the only means of exchange with the point of entry before the cut-off; the phase of the tide and the moons read the general state of the sea, not its condition at the point of entry.

**1.4. The current** In the old traces, landings from one place in one season lean, on the whole, to one side, and that side agrees with the general state of the sea shown by the moons at the time. I call this direction the current, and divide the error into a part along the current, $delta_parallel$, and a part across it, $delta_perp$.

**2. Place and current**

**2.1. A thought** Suppose the caster wishes to read exactly, before setting out, the position of the point of entry within the current. He must make the sounding exchange with a very small region only, to know which region it is; but the smaller the region of exchange, the more wisps are needed, and every wisp pushes the sea there a little, so that the cross-current there is thrown into disorder. Conversely, to read the cross-current exactly, the sounding must exchange slowly with a wide stretch of the sea, and then it cannot say which point it was. The more exactly the one is read, the more the other is disordered (Fig. 1).

Fig. 1. The thought of sounding the sea: at the place of setting out, a sounding that exchanges with a small region reads the place well and throws the current into disorder; one that exchanges with a wide region reads the current well and cannot tell the place; a balanced sounding keeps the two parts in balance.

**2.2. Derivation from the relation of raising and lowering** By the general rules since Elworthy, the exchange between the web and the depths is described by two operations, raising $U$ and lowering $D$ [8]; Selby and Ormes proved that the difference of their two orders is not nought,

$$ D U - U D = epsilon $$ (1)

$epsilon$ being the wisp [4]. What Selby and Ormes proved concerns the exchange between the web and the depths, and their reading of one opening and closing as one wisp is a further premise of their own. This paper needs a further, bridging premise (Premise 2 of §3): that the exchanges of the sea at the point of entry with the sounding and with the return also obey (1), with the same $epsilon$. Under this premise I call the condition of the sea at the point of entry its sea-state, written, added and measured in strength by the rules for web-states, and I consider only sea-states in which place and current have finite second moments. Take

$$ S = (U + D) / sqrt(2) , quad V = A (D - U) / sqrt(2) $$ (2)

where $A$ is Elworthy’s warp, which may be interchanged with $U$ and $D$ and whose square under successive application is the negative of the identity operation [8]. By (1), $S V - V S = - epsilon A$, whose strength in every sea-state is $epsilon$ (Lemma 2.1 of the formal text). By Elworthy’s rules of duals and strength, for every sea-state the sea can be in,

$$ sigma_S dot sigma_V >= epsilon / 2 $$ (3)

where $sigma$ denotes the spread of a quantity in that sea-state, that is, the square root of the mean square of its difference from its mean. This is the relation that the thought of §2.1 calls for, and it is stronger than that thought: it does not say that the sounding is clumsy, but that in every sea-state the sea can be in, place and current are never both definite. Sea-states in which (3) holds with equality exist; in them place and current are both distributed normally, and their spreads may be traded one against the other.

**2.3. After sounding** It must also be made clear that (3) still holds after the sea has been sounded.

LEMMA 1. However many soundings are made, and however each is chosen after the readings of the last, once the readings are had the conditional sea-state at the point of entry is still a sea-state, and satisfies (3).

The conditional sea-state here is the sea-state renewed, according to the rule of reading, by the shares read. The conditions of Lemma 1 are that every sounding exchanges with the sea only through raising and lowering, and that the readings follow the rule of reading of Selby and Ormes; its proof is in the formal text, where it takes up most of §2. By Lemma 1, at the cut-off, whatever has been done before, the point of entry is in a sea-state satisfying (3).

**3. The lower bound**

**3.1. The model** The bound rests on four premises.

(Premise 1) The mechanism of return turns the sea-state at the point of entry at the cut-off into a distribution of landings: given $I$, the part along the current, $delta_parallel$, has the same distribution as $a (S - hat(S))$ in that conditional sea-state, and the part across it, $delta_perp$, the same as $b (V - hat(V))$:

$$ delta_parallel tilde a (S - hat(S)) , quad delta_perp tilde b (V - hat(V)) $$ (4)

$hat(S)$ and $hat(V)$ are any estimates made from $I$; $a$ and $b$ are the two magnifications of the passage, both positive, which grow with the planned reach. The joint distribution of the two parts is not used; if the return, reading both parts at once, adds noise, it only makes the error greater. Terms of the second order and higher are neglected in (4); within the reaches covered by the first batch, the neglected terms together with their cross terms with the linear ones reduce the mean square error by at most eight parts in a hundred, so that $eta$ does not exceed 0.08 (§6).

(Premise 2) The exchanges at the point of entry obey (1), which is the bridging premise of §2.2; hence (3) and Lemma 1.

(Premise 3) Whatever is known before the cut-off about the point of entry comes by exchange with the sea; there is no other way.

(Premise 4) The dependence of $a$ and $b$ on the planned reach $R$ is fixed by the traces of pairs that set out together (§6); at the ordinary phase of the tide

$$ sqrt(epsilon a b) = 0.438 sqrt(R) e^(R \/ 600) $$ (5)

$R$ and the left side both being in miles, for $R$ not exceeding four hundred miles. At short reaches it grows as the square root of the reach, like a drop of ink spreading in water; at long reaches it gains a further factor growing exponentially with the reach. At the ebb there is another such formula, with 690 miles in place of the 600 in the exponent (§5.1).

**3.2. The theorem**

THEOREM 1. Under Premises 1 to 4, for a given planned reach $R$ (not exceeding four hundred miles) and phase of the tide, for every means of sounding the sea before the casting and every means $f$ of reckoning the point,

$$ "mean error" >= B = sqrt((1 - eta) epsilon a b) $$ (6)

At the ordinary phase of the tide, $B(R) = 0.42 sqrt(R) e^(R \/ 600)$ miles.

Proof. First fix $I$. By Lemma 1 the point of entry is in a conditional sea-state satisfying (3). The mean square of the difference of a quantity from any estimate is not less than the square of its spread, so that by (4), the neglected terms set aside for the moment,

$$ E (|delta|^2 | I) >= a^2 sigma_S^2 + b^2 sigma_V^2 >= 2 a b sigma_S sigma_V >= epsilon a b $$ (7)

the middle step being that the sum of two squares is not less than twice their product. Taking the neglected terms into account removes at most a fraction $eta$ more, so that the conditional mean square error is not less than $(1 - eta) epsilon a b$. With $R$ and the phase of the tide fixed, $a$ and $b$ do not vary with $I$, and averaging over $I$ the whole mean square error is not less than this either; if reaches and phases of the tide are pooled, the bound is the square root of the mean of $(1 - eta) epsilon a b$ weighted by their proportions.

The proof is short because the difficulties have all been put into the premises. What the formal text must prove is Lemma 1; the estimate of the neglected terms and their cross terms; that the landing lies in three dimensions, the vertical part being counted too; and that (3) itself follows from (1) by Elworthy’s rules.

**3.3. What the bound means** (6) does not say that the present methods are not good enough, but that no method can be better: so long as the premises of the model hold, no sounding however fine and no engine however great can reckon the landing with a mean error less than $B$. In the ideal linear model in two dimensions, with $eta$ nought and no vertical error, the bound can be attained: the reckoned point must be the conditional mean, the sea-state must be one in which (3) holds with equality, and $a sigma_S = b sigma_V$, that is, the parts along and across the current must be in balance. Actual spells can only approach it. Table 1 gives the bound at several reaches at the ordinary phase of the tide.

| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |

**3.4. The mean of the distance** If the mean error is taken as the mean of the distance only, another form of (3) is needed: that the sum of the entropies of the distributions of place and current is not less than $ln(pi e epsilon)$. This does not follow from (3), and the formal text proves it separately. From it, and from the fact that the mean deviation of a distribution is not less than $e^(h - 1) \/ 2$, where $h$ is its entropy, the mean distance is found to be not less than $0.76 B$; the balanced circular normal distribution gives $sqrt(pi) \/ 2$ times $B$, that is, $0.89 B$. Which number between these is the exact bound I have not determined.

**4. Short reaches and long**

In the manuscripts of the classical age several law-mages claim to have landed without the least error [2]. The reaches they record are none more than a mile or two; by (6) the bound there is about half a mile, and with the measures of that time, to come down in the wood or on the hill one had in mind was to land without the least error. As the reach grows the bound grows as $sqrt(R)$, and at some tens of miles the landing is out by some miles; at some hundreds of miles the exponential factor takes effect, and the bound grows much faster than the square root of the reach. When Severin says “sometimes it falls far off”, he speaks most likely of deflections of long reach.

This shows also why deflected translation serves for escape: at any but a short reach the caster himself cannot reckon where he will land, and the pursuer, who has only what can be seen from outside, still less.

**5. Discussion of some methods**

**5.1. Choosing the tide** At the ebb the sea is calmer; on the first batch the 600 miles in the exponent of (5) becomes 690 miles, and at a reach of four hundred miles the bound is some eight parts in a hundred smaller. Those who set out on the ebb therefore have a smaller bound at long reach; the theorem holds separately for each phase of the tide, and choosing the tide does not alter it, but alters $a$ and $b$.

**5.2. Sounding many times** If one sounding is not enough, several do not lower the bound: by Lemma 1 the sea-state after sounding still satisfies (3). Sounding many times steadies the readings, but cannot pass (6).

**5.3. Reading the moons** The moons are the reflection of the sea, and show its general state and the direction of its current, but not the place and current at the point of entry. Reading the moons fixes the direction of §1.4, but cannot bring the error below the bound.

**5.4. Borrowing an array** It has been proposed to pin the point of entry with an array, so as to make the place definite. Borrowing an array changes the mechanism of entry, and whether it still satisfies Premise 1 must first be checked. By (3), the harder the place is pinned the more the current is disordered; the first batch contains nineteen traces of setting out from an array, and taken as a group their mean square error is four tenths greater than that of a group without arrays matched in reach and phase of the tide.

**6. Test of the premises**

**6.1. Three batches of traces** The model was fixed on the first batch, tested on the second, and the method of locating tested on the third (Table 2). The first batch consists of the entries of the Register of Translation Traces of the White Tower from C.R. 1190 to F.E. 330 [5]; of the 11,214 entries, only those whose traces of setting out and of landing are both whole and whose times agree were taken, the 1,402 whose landings were never found, the 1,105 with damaged traces and the 721 whose times disagree being set aside. The second consists of the entries from 331 to 356, all signed, every signature checked [6]; of the 2,618 entries, the 193 whose landings were never found and the 108 with damaged traces were set aside. The rules of choice were fixed before any landing was looked at. The model was completed in the Month of the Winter Lyre, 351, and sealed by signature, and the landings of the second batch were drawn out for comparison only afterwards. The third batch is the drills of 352 to 357, kept in a separate book and not among the second batch of the Register, described in §7. By the rule followed since Darby, the model was fixed on the first batch alone, the second and third taking no part [9]. Landings never found are mostly far ones, so that the conclusions of this paper concern deflected translations whose landings were found, and the proportion of far landings may be too low.

**6.2. Magnification** A pair is two casters setting out from one place within a few paces or a few breaths of each other. What the two know before the cut-off is almost the same, but their points of entry differ. For pairs setting out a few paces apart at almost the same moment, the places at their points of entry differ by about the distance between their places of setting out, and how far apart their landings fall along the current shows $a$; for pairs setting out from one place a few breaths apart, the difference of the currents at their points of entry is estimated from the change of the general current shown by the moons over those breaths, the local differences averaging out over many pairs, and how far apart their landings fall across the current shows $b$. Of the 643 pairs of the first batch, 291 are of the former kind and 352 of the latter. From them $a$ and $b$ are fixed and combined into (5): the coefficient $0.438 plus.minus 0.011$, and the $600 plus.minus 40$ miles in the exponent; the ratio of $a$ to $b$ is $1.12 plus.minus 0.09$, and changes little with the reach. The 171 pairs of the second batch, fixing them again, give $0.427 plus.minus 0.019$ and $585 plus.minus 70$ miles, agreeing within the errors. The first batch spans the Great Tide, and the second the remaking of the standards by the Seventh Division in 342; reaches are measured in miles and are untouched by the change of the units of mana, and when the two parts of the first batch before and after the Great Tide are fitted separately the coefficients also agree within the errors.

**6.3. The neglected terms** In (4) terms of the second order and higher have been neglected. Within the reaches covered by the first batch, taking the neglected terms and their cross terms with the linear ones into account changes the mean square error by not more than eight parts in a hundred, so that $eta = 0.08$ is taken; so also on the second batch. Traces with a reach beyond four hundred miles are too few, and the model does not extend there.

**6.4. Forecast from outward knowledge** Without sounding the sea, from the place and time of setting out, the phase of the tide and the moons only, the model can still reckon a landing, with an error much greater than the bound. Compared with the second batch (Table 3, Fig. 2), the observed errors in all six bands are four to six parts in a hundred above those forecast by the model, all in the same direction; most likely because, since signatures came into use, landing traces are more easily found and more of the far landings have been recorded, while the model, fixed on the first batch, has too few far landings. Of the four premises, Premises 1 and 4 are tested directly by the pairs and by the second batch; Premise 2 and Lemma 1 are tested only indirectly, in that the errors of the third batch fall below the bound in no band; Premise 3 is an assumption of this paper.

**7. The method of locating**

By locating I mean reckoning before the casting where the landing will most likely be, not sending the caster to a chosen place. Section 3.3 shows how the bound may be approached: the parts of the error along and across the current must be in balance, and the sea must therefore be sounded neither for the place alone nor for the current alone, but in balance according to the ratio of $a$ to $b$ (§6.2). The size of the region with which the sounding exchanges is taken such that the spreads of the parts along and across the current are equal; once the readings are had, the reckoned point is the conditional mean reckoned by the model, sealed at the cut-off.

The third batch consists of the drills made by this method by twelve law-mages of the White Tower between 352 and 357, a hundred and eighty-four in all. They set out far from cities and sea-lanes, with a ship standing by each time. The results are in Table 4: in every band the ratio of the mean error to $B$ does not exceed 1.2, the band of shortest reach coming nearest, at about 1.06; in no band is the mean below the bound. No drill had a reach beyond two hundred and fifty miles.

**8. The distribution of landings**

By the model, after a balanced sounding of the sea the greater part of the error is near a circular normal distribution in two variables, with a spread of about $B \/ sqrt(2)$ in every direction; besides this, about one in a hundred landings falls along the current beyond three times the bound, owing to an unintended deepening of the force of projection at entry, and of these the model gives only the proportion, not where they fall. There were two such in the third batch, both included in Table 4. With outward knowledge only, without sounding, the distribution is long along the current and short across it (Fig. 3), the spread along being about one and a half times that across, and the far landings are about four in a hundred.

Fig. 3. The distribution of landings: the errors of the second batch with outward knowledge only, the parts along and across the current both in units of the bound $B$; the ellipse is the region containing nine tenths of the model’s distribution; the points are 560 observed landings taken at random, those outside the ellipse in black.

These two distributions may serve those who wish to prepare for a landing, and those who wish to find one. But a distribution says only where a landing will most likely be: there are landings outside the region of nine tenths, and of the far landings there is only a proportion, no place. An order of search drawn from the distribution can follow only the estimated chances, and must not take the ellipse for a boundary.

**Conclusion**

Severin asked: if it cannot, how small at least can the error be made? The answer of this paper is: under the tested conditions of the model, not less than the $B$ of (6); and by a balanced sounding of the sea the drills came within 1.2 times of it. The bound rests on the premises of the model, and the premises on the test of the traces: should anyone find hereafter a means of knowing the condition of the sea without exchange, Premise 3 would fail, and the bound would have to be examined anew.

Why the passage through the sea magnifies in this way, I do not discuss. Formula (5) is only the form fixed by the traces; why it spreads at short reach and grows like a rolling snowball at long reach is for those who study the sea of mana to answer.

**Appendix. The record of the formal text and its verification**

Under the rule of about 355 [11], this appendix records how the formal text was written, who verified it, and what was verified.

The formal text is written in the notation of the verifier of the University of Kanaich; since Finley, verifiers of this line accept only derivations made step by step in their notation [12]. I first wrote the skeleton of Theorem 1: nine hundred and twelve lemmas, with the statement of each and the outline of its proof; the inference engine [10] expanded the proof of every lemma into derivations for the verifier, the manner of expansion and the versions of the formulae used being separately recorded. Expanded, the text has 1,046,233 derivations, of which the derivation of (3) from (1), together with Lemma 1, takes 386,402; the estimate of the terms neglected in (4) and their cross terms, 302,155; the inequality of entropies and the bound on the mean distance of §3.4, 147,318; and the remaining 210,358 are notation, definitions and general lemmas. Whatever derivations expanded by the engine the verifier did not accept were sent back to me, the skeleton rewritten, and expanded again by the engine; this went back and forth more than two hundred times. The formal text carries a list of the lemmas, a list of the axioms and definitions used, and a table matching each formula of this paper with its proposition in the formal text.

The verifier is the fourth edition of that of the University of Kanaich, written by a group there, and unconnected with the engine or with me. The formal text was verified once in each of the engine rooms of White Tower University and of the University of Kanaich, taking forty-one days at each, and passed wholly at both; both rooms used the same edition of the verifier, so that the two runs exclude only chance faults of the machines, and are not a verification by two verifiers differently written. The formal text is sealed by signature [6], and its digest is printed below, so that anyone may be sure that what he verifies is the same text.

What the verifier confirms is that, upon Premises 1 to 4 as written in the formal text, Theorem 1 holds. It does not confirm that the premises agree with the facts; that is the business of §6.
